My Incapacity Is Its Inimitability

The Great Nūniyya — a qaṣīda on the ordering of the Book · strict classical al-Kāmil · rhyme-letter nūn
۞ ❖ ۞

This is a poem that was not written the way poems are written — it was built the way proofs are built. Every image in it derives from a computed measurement on the text of the Qur'an itself: its rhyme is extracted from a census of the verse-endings of the Book; its center sits where the Book's geometric center actually lies; its caravan-and-stars tableau translates a mathematical discovery about the ordering of the sūras; and its satire of the number-mystics rests on rigorous computational refutation, not on passing opinion.

Its subject is the oldest question in Arabic criticism: why did the poets fail to imitate the Qur'an? Its answer is the doctrine of ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī in Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz: the secret lies in naẓm — the ordering, the way word and meaning are welded together past human reach. But the poem does not merely state this; it performs it: a poet musters his full arsenal, makes the attempt, measures his own failure line by line — until he understands that his incapacity is itself the evidence. And so he prostrates. Hence the title: ʿAjzī Iʿjāzuh — "my incapacity (ʿajz) is its inimitability (iʿjāz)" — a derivational pun on a single Arabic root (ʿ-j-z) that carries the poem's entire thesis in two words.

Throughout, the poem is reverence, not rivalry: no verse of the Qur'an is ever placed in the poet's mouth, and every fracture in the poem falls on the poet alone.

Meter & Rhyme

al-Kāmil al-tāmm — the maqṭūʿ muḍmar cadence — a bound, wāw-buttressed rhyme

مُتَفاعِلُنْ مُتَفاعِلُنْ مُتَفاعِلُنْ ‖ مُتَفاعِلُنْ مُتَفاعِلُنْ مُتْفاعِلْ

⏑⏑–⏑– ⏑⏑–⏑– ⏑⏑–⏑– ‖ ⏑⏑–⏑– ⏑⏑–⏑– – – –

The meter: al-Kāmil ("the Complete"), the most kinetic of the Arabic meters — the meter of ʿAntara's boasts and Labīd's wisdom. Each half-line carries three feet of mutafāʿilun (⏑⏑–⏑–), whose first two light syllables may freely fuse into one heavy (iḍmār: – –⏑–), line by line, as the classical license allows.

The cadence: the final foot of every line undergoes two classical operations — iḍmār (fusing) and qaṭʿ (docking) — leaving مُتْفاعِلْ = three heavy beats: – – –. This locked cadence falls at the end of all 29 lines without exception, like a seal stamped at the close of every breath.

The rhyme: a muqayyad rhyme (the rhyme-consonant nūn is silenced at pause), buttressed by a long ū (ridf): maknūn, mawḍūn, ʿurjūn… Every rhyme-word in the poem is distinct — except one deliberate return: المَكْنون (al-maknūn), the word that opens the poem at line 1, seals it at line 29. At a distance of 28 lines this repetition is no defect (the flaw of īṭāʾ concerns near repetition); it is design — "returning the close upon the opening" — for a poem whose very subject is a Book that circles back on itself.

The most alive hemistich in the poem is the front half of line 15 — the tawḥīd line — three fully live mutafāʿilun in a row: the fastest pulse in the poem, beating exactly at its heart.

The Secret of the Nūn: why is the rhyme-letter ن?

Because nūn is the Book's own rhyme — the classical science of fawāṣil, now measured

The nūn was not chosen by taste but extracted by measurement. The verse-endings of the Qur'an (fawāṣil al-āy) are a classical science — al-Zarkashī devotes an entire chapter of al-Burhān to them, and al-Suyūṭī a full division of al-Itqān (nawʿ 59). Computed over the whole text:

So the nūn — not the mīm, as is popularly assumed — is the third most frequent letter of the Book, and it closes half its verses: the hidden crown of its endings. A poem about the Book's ordering, rhymed on nūn, is rhymed on the Book's own rhyme. That is what "written mathematically" means here: even the rhyme-letter is a measured decision.

The Architecture

29 lines: 14 + 1 + 14 — tawḥīd as the axis of the mill

Line 15 (gold) = the tawḥīd line, with 14 + 14 lines on either side

The heart at the exact mathematical center: twenty-nine lines, so line 15 is the precise midpoint — and it alone carries pure tawḥīd: هُوَ أَوَّلٌ هُوَ آخِرٌ هُوَ واحِدٌ صَمَدٌ. This deliberately mirrors a measured discovery: Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ — the sūra of pure tawḥīd — is the geometric center-of-mass of the muṣḥaf (rank 1 of 114 by proximity to the statistical centroid of all sūras). As the Book's measured heart is tawḥīd, so the poem's heart was set — same position, same finding.

29 and 14: the line-count, twenty-nine, equals the number of sūras opened by the "mysterious letters" (al-muqaṭṭaʿāt); flanking the heart, 14 + 14 = 28 — the number of letters those openings are built from, and of the lunar mansions to which the crescent-line (27) returns. A deliberate craftsman's correspondence, openly declared — not a claim of numerical mystery. That is the whole difference between making a proportion and hallucinating one.

The dramatic arc: the anti-ruins opening (1) → the poet's hubris and full muster (4–5) → the caravan and the stars: the shortest path (6–8) → the human tradeoff law (10–14) → the heart: tawḥīd (15–17) → incapacity as proof; the ṣarfa doctrine refused (18–20) → the satire of the counters (21–23) → the prostration (24–26) → the returning moon and the drowned poet (27–28) → return to the beginning: the text is a ring (29).

۩ A reader asked: in line 1, do the last two words — ظَلَّ المَكْنونْ — even fit together?

Yes — the construction is classical, and the friction the ear feels is the engineered point of the verse. Three answers:

1. Grammar: ظَلَّ ("remained") is a sister of كانَ: its subject is an implied pronoun referring back to rasm al-waḥy ("the trace of Revelation"), and المَكْنون ("the treasured one") is its predicate. A definite predicate after these verbs is textbook Arabic — like saying kāna Zaydun al-amīra, "Zayd was the prince." The definite article adds exclusivity: it remained the treasured one — as if nothing else were. (The final nūn is silenced because the rhyme is muqayyad.)

2. Meaning — where the objection turns into the beauty: a rasm is a visible, exposed trace; maknūn means hidden away, sheltered. How can the exposed thing be the sheltered thing? That tension is deliberate, and the first half of the line resolves it: every campsite's landmarks were effaced because they lay exposed to wind and time; the trace of Revelation alone stayed fresh because it is kept-treasured. The two words are not enemies — one is the reason for the other. Only what is treasured survives. And the word is the Qur'an's own self-description: ﴿إِنَّهُ لَقُرْآنٌ كَريمٌ في كِتابٍ مَكْنونٍ﴾ "a noble Qur'an, in a treasured Book" (56:77–78), and ﴿كَأَمْثالِ اللُّؤْلُؤِ المَكْنونِ﴾ "like treasured pearls" (56:23) — both Book and pearl, the poem's two master images.

3. The double meaning: rasm carries two senses at once — the trace of a campsite in the ruins-convention of pre-Islamic poetry, and rasm al-muṣḥaf: the Uthmanic orthography, the written skeleton of the Qur'anic text itself. In its second register the line says: the very script of Revelation has remained preserved as it was set down. The opening is thus a complete inversion of the classical ruins-opening: the old poet stands weeping over a trace the desert is erasing; this poem opens with the one trace that does not erode — it begins where all previous poetry ended.

And the poem closes on the same word: «فَالخَتْمُ بَدْءٌ مَكْنونْ» (line 29) — the word in question is the arc of the whole poem: it opens with it and is sealed by it.

Line 1 — The opening: the ruins-motif overturned
عَفَّتْ مَعالِمُ كُلِّ دارٍ، وَالهُدى‖غَضٌّ، وَرَسْمُ الوَحْيِ ظَلَّ المَكْنونْ

Vocabulary
عَفَّتْʿaffat — "were effaced, worn away": the inherited verb of the ruins-opening; Labīd's Muʿallaqa begins with this very word (عَفَتِ الدِّيارُ) مَعالِمmaʿālim — landmarks; the signs by which an abandoned camp is recognized غَضّghaḍḍ — fresh, dewy, unwilted — said of a plant just emerged رَسْمrasm — the ground-trace of a dwelling; simultaneously, the orthography of the muṣḥaf (a deliberate double meaning) المَكْنونal-maknūn — the treasured, kept-hidden, sheltered; the Qur'an's own epithet for itself and for pearls
Meaning

The traces of every dwelling on earth have been wiped away — that is time's law. Except one trace: the Guidance is as fresh as the day it descended, and the trace of Revelation endures precisely because it is treasured.

Craft & structure

The opening revives the most sacred convention of the Arabic qaṣīda — standing over the ruins — in order to overturn it from inside: the pre-Islamic poet weeps for a trace that is vanishing; this poet witnesses a trace that cannot vanish. The antithesis of ʿaffat ("effaced") and ghaḍḍ ("fresh") folds thirteen centuries into a single half-line. (See the boxed answer above on the grammar, the paradox, and the double meaning.)

Line 2 — The history of the imitation attempts
كَمْ حاوَلَ الشُّعَراءُ نَظْمًا مِثْلَهو‖فَنَبَتْ قَوافيهِمْ، وَخابَ المَغْبونْ
How often the poets attempted an ordering like it — their rhymes glanced off like blunted swords, and the out-bargained went home cheated.
Vocabulary
كَمْkam — exclamatory "how many!": multiplying the attempts نَظْمnaẓm — "ordering, composition": al-Jurjānī's central technical term for what makes the Qur'an inimitable نَبَتْnabat — from nabā al-sayf: a sword glancing off without cutting — their rhymes are swords that bounced off the Book المَغْبونal-maghbūn — the one cheated in a bargain: paid everything, took nothing
Meaning

Century after century they tried to rival it; their rhymes recoiled like blunt blades, and every gambler at this table left fleeced.

Craft & structure

The metaphor of nabat turns imitation into a duel where the weapon betrays its bearer. The line compresses a whole chapter of criticism — the muʿāraḍa challenge documented by al-Bāqillānī in Iʿjāz al-Qurʾān: attempts were recorded; what survives of them is the report of their failure.

Line 3 — The sea and the diver
بَحْرٌ تَغوصُ وَلا تَنالُ قَرارَهو‖وَالدُّرُّ في الأَعْماقِ باتَ المَدْفونْ

This is al-Niffarī's maxim — "the wider the vision, the narrower the expression" — given a body: the tongue has knots that tighten before what is seen. Line 23 — The answer: naẓm, not numbers

Line 24 — The one inhabited dwelling

Eloquence has perished — the eloquence of every gifted tongue; the scrolls of the ancients lie wasteland, and one dwelling alone stays inhabited.
Vocabulary
فَنِيَfaniya — perished, went extinct مُفَوَّهmufawwah — a man of mighty mouth: the supremely eloquent قَفْرqafr — desolate waste, no living soul in it رَبْعٌ مَسْكونrabʿ maskūn — one dwelling still inhabited: the abode that never emptied
Meaning

The eloquence of the ancients has died — every silver tongue of them — and their scrolls lie as wastelands no reader walks. In all the earth one dwelling remains inhabited: recited at every hour, on every horizon.

Craft & structure

The doubled lament ("eloquence… the eloquence of every gifted tongue") tolls like a bell struck twice — classical epanalepsis. And the line is the echo of the opening: there, "the landmarks of every dwelling are effaced"; here, "one dwelling stays inhabited" — the inner ring closes. It is also plain sociological fact: the dīwāns of the masters live in archives; the Book lives in millions of breasts and is chanted every dawn.

Line 25 — The prostration: the turn
فَخَرَرْتُ، لا عَجْزًا فَقَطْ، بَلْ سَجْدَةً‖وَالعَجْزُ نُسْكي، وَالجَبينُ المَطْحونْ
So I fell — not in incapacity only, but in prostration: my incapacity is my rite, and my brow the one ground into the dust.
Vocabulary
خَرَرْتُkharartu — "I fell": the same verb the Qur'an uses for falling into prostration — ﴿وَخَرَّ راكِعًا وَأَنابَ﴾ (38:24) لا عَجْزًا فَقَطْ، بَلْlā ʿajzan faqaṭ, bal — "not incapacity only, but…": the double truth — the failure is real and not denied; it is transfigured نُسْكيnuskī — my rite, my act of worship and offering المَطْحونal-maṭḥūn — ground like grain: a brow milled into the dust by the length of its prostration
Meaning

So I fell — and the falling was incapacity, I do not deny it; but it was not incapacity only: it was a prostration. My defeat itself became my worship, my offering — and my dust-ground forehead, my insignia.

Craft & structure

The summit of the poem and its alchemical turn: the one verb kharra carries both meanings — collapse and prostration — so the transmutation of defeat into worship happens inside a single word. "Not incapacity only, but" holds both truths: deny the ʿajz and the whole poem lies; stop at it and it is mere defeat — so it is affirmed, and raised. With this the hubris-arc opened at "I summoned it down" (4) closes: he called the speech down, and came down himself — prostrate.

Line 26 — Who adorned whom?
ما زِنْتُ بِالشِّعْرِ الكِتابَ، وَإِنَّما‖زانَ القَريضَ، فَراحَ وَهْوَ المَأْذونْ
I did not adorn the Book with poetry — it adorned the poem, which went out into the world only by its leave.
Vocabulary
ما زِنْتُmā zintu — "I added no adornment": from zāna, to grace القَريضal-qarīḍ — poetry; this very poem المَأْذونal-maʾdhūn — the licensed one: the poem leaves by permission, not by its own power

I have not graced the Book with poetry — God forbid the thought; it graced my poem. Whatever beauty this qaṣīda has is borrowed from it, and it walks among people only as one given leave.

Craft & structure

The inversion of the entire panegyric tradition: the Arabic poet adorns his patron; this Patron adorns His poet. There is also a quiet confession about the poem itself: its lexicon — maknūn, mawḍūn, masnūn, ʿurjūn, mashḥūn — is borrowed from the Revelation it praises. The poem is ornamented by the very thing it fails to match.

Line 27 — The returning moon

And the sūras of Guidance run on, each at the last one's heels, dwindling and curving like the moon through its mansions — from full splendor down, mansion by mansion, until it comes home to the ancient palm-crescent: the thin, bowed sliver.

The most beautiful simile in the poem — taken from the moon-verse itself (36:39) to describe the very Book in which that verse descended. Line 28 — The drowned poet

The sea of line 3 returns — but the confident diver ("you dive…") has become the drowned. The distance between those two lines is the entire journey of the poem. Line 29 — The seal: the ring

The close returned upon the opening: the poem's final word is its first rhyme-word — al-maknūn — deliberately, so the qaṣīda itself becomes a ring that comes back to its start, exactly as the Book's reader returns from the seal to the opening.
