# Naskh (Abrogation) — Catalog and Phase-B Analysis

Phase: B (hypotheses). Date: 2026-04-12. Scope: classical naskh theory, Q 2:106 as locus classicus, the famous candidate pairs, the tilāwah/ḥukm typology, and the contested stoning-verse claim. Purpose: give this project a defensible map of where "abrogation" claims are strong, where they are weak, and where they are probably mis-diagnoses of rhetorical or legislative-phase phenomena that can be modelled without invoking naskh at all.

## 1. Root n-s-kh: the uppercut/copy dyad

The Arabic root n-s-kh (ن-س-خ) carries in the classical lexica (Lisān al-ʿArab, Tāj al-ʿArūs, Rāghib's Mufradāt) a double semantic axis that European vocabularies usually split between two distinct verbs:

1. To remove, cancel, efface, cause to pass away (ʿazāla, rafaʿa). Ibn Fāris: "izālat shayʾ wa-ithbāt ghayrihi makānahu" — "the removal of a thing and the placement of another in its stead." This is the "uppercut" sense: one thing knocks another out. Classical Arabic uses nasakha-l-shamsu-l-ẓilla, "the sun effaced the shadow," and tanāsakha-l-qurūnu, "the generations succeeded one another," both leaning on this sense.
2. To copy, transcribe, duplicate (istaktaba, naqala). A nuskha is a manuscript copy; istansakha is to make a copy. This is the "copy" sense, preserved in modern standard Arabic for "photocopy" (nuskha, nassākha).

These two senses are not two roots but one root polarised across a transitive relation. In naskha-X bi-Y, the X-side is effaced and the Y-side is its replacement. If the focus falls on X one reads "cancellation." If the focus falls on Y one reads "copy/substitute." Every occurrence of the root in the Qur'an trades on this polarity.

### Qur'anic occurrences of the root

- Q 2:106 — "mā nansakh min āya aw nunsihā naʾti bi-khayrin minhā aw mithlihā." Verb: nansakh (imperfect 1pl). This is the locus classicus for the theological doctrine of naskh.
- Q 7:154 — "fī nuskhatihā hudan wa-raḥma." The Tablets of Moses, and "in their nuskha" (their copy/transcription) there is guidance. Here the "copy" sense dominates; the tablets inscribe a duplicate of the divine speech.
- Q 22:52 — "fa-yansakhu Allāhu mā yulqī al-shayṭān." God "effaces/abrogates" what Satan casts into the recitation of a prophet — the classical "Satanic verses" (gharānīq) proof-text. Here the "cancellation" sense is sharp.
- Q 45:29 — "innā kunnā nastansikhu mā kuntum taʿmalūn." "We were causing [your deeds] to be copied out." The istafʿala stem foregrounds the "copy" sense: the ledger of deeds as transcript.

One additional passage is sometimes invoked by analogy though it does not use the root: Q 16:101 — "wa-idhā baddalnā āyatan makāna āyatin" — where the verb is b-d-l, "substitute." Classical tafsīr reads this as a near-synonym of 2:106's naskh.

The Qur'an therefore uses n-s-kh in both polarities, and crucially the "archive/ledger" sense (45:29) and the "manuscript copy" sense (7:154) sit alongside the "cancellation" sense (22:52, and — contested — 2:106). Any theory that reads 2:106 as asserting only cancellation must ignore the other half of the root's spectrum.

## 2. Q 2:106 — deep analysis

Text: "mā nansakh min āya aw nunsihā naʾti bi-khayrin minhā aw mithlihā. a-lam taʿlam anna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr."
Gloss: "Whatever sign (āya) we nansakh or cause to be forgotten (nunsihā), we bring [something] better than it or like it. Do you not know that God is powerful over every thing?"

### Semantic cruxes

1. **āya** — not necessarily "verse." The word means "sign" broadly: cosmic sign, prophetic miracle, prophetic sign-scripture. Every pre-Islamic prophet's miracle is an āya. Qurʾān-internal usage spans all three registers. Reading 2:106 as "whatever legal verse we abrogate" narrows āya to its later technical sense; a reading that hears "whatever sign-dispensation we revoke" is at least as grammatical and arguably closer to the rhetorical context (2:104-108 addresses People-of-the-Book polemic about why revelation should come to Muḥammad at all, not intra-Muslim legal dispute).
2. **nansakh / nunsihā** — the pairing is telling. Nansakh = "we efface"; nunsihā (from n-s-y, "to forget," causative 4th form) = "we cause to be forgotten." The second reading *nansaʾhā* (from n-s-ʾ, "to postpone, defer") is attested in several qirāʾāt (e.g., Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr), yielding "we defer it." The gap between "cause to be forgotten" and "defer" radically changes what 2:106 claims. If nansaʾhā, the verse is about postponement, not deletion — an entirely different legal-theological object.
3. **bi-khayrin minhā aw mithlihā** — "better than it, or the like of it." The disjunction is crucial. "Like" (mithl) cannot mean "cancellation"; it must mean replacement with an equivalent — which is an odd way to describe a legal change where one ruling ends and another begins. It fits far better a picture of successive prophets receiving comparable or superior sign-dispensations. This reading (favoured e.g. by Muḥammad ʿAbduh and by many moderns) sees 2:106 as inter-scriptural — each prophet's āya is replaced by a comparable or superior āya in the next dispensation — rather than intra-Qurʾānic.
4. **ālam taʿlam anna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr** — the rhetorical punch-line is about divine omnipotence over change, not a declaration that the Qurʾān contains abrogated verses. The pragmatics are defensive: critics are wrong to mock shifts in revelation.

### Occasion-of-revelation (asbāb al-nuzūl)

The dominant reports (Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Ṭabarī; Wāḥidī) place 2:106 in the Medinan period during Jewish polemic against the Muslim community, typically in connection with either (a) the qibla change or (b) Jewish objections to abrogation of prior Mosaic rulings by the Qurʾān. Both framings support an inter-dispensational reading; only later juristic readings pull 2:106 inward to become self-referential (Qurʾān about Qurʾān).

### Juristic weaponisation

By the time of al-Shāfiʿī (d. 204/820), 2:106 is the textual foundation of a developed doctrine that the Qurʾān abrogates the Qurʾān, that the Qurʾān abrogates the sunna, that the sunna abrogates the sunna, and (contested) whether the sunna may abrogate the Qurʾān. The later tafsīr tradition (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) catalogues abrogated pairs by the hundred. This is a maximalist phase. A counter-movement from al-Ghazālī onward, sharpened in the modernist period (Shāh Walī Allāh, Muḥammad ʿAbduh, Rashīd Riḍā, Ṣubḥī Ṣāliḥ), tightens the criteria and reduces the list drastically.

## 3. Catalogs: classical maximalist vs modern minimalist

### Classical maximalists

- **al-Nahhas** (d. 338/949), *al-Nāsikh wa-l-Mansūkh*: ~134 verses.
- **Ibn al-Jawzī** (d. 597/1201), *Nawāsikh al-Qurʾān*: ~247 verses.
- **Hibat Allāh ibn Salāma** (d. 410/1020): ~237.
- **al-Suyūṭī** (d. 911/1505), *al-Itqān fī ʿulūm al-Qurʾān*, ch. 47: Suyūṭī prunes the inflated classical lists to ~20 (commonly cited as 21) cases he considers genuinely abrogative rather than merely specificatory (takhṣīṣ), delimitative (taqyīd), or explanatory (tafsīr). His list is in effect the late-classical consensus "shortlist."

### Modern minimalists

- **Shāh Walī Allāh al-Dihlawī** (d. 1176/1762), *al-Fawz al-kabīr*: reduced the certain cases to 5.
- **Ṣubḥī al-Ṣāliḥ**, *Mabāḥith fī ʿulūm al-Qurʾān*: reduces genuine abrogations to ~4.
- **Muḥammad ʿAbduh / Rashīd Riḍā** in *al-Manār*: close to full denial of intra-Qurʾānic abrogation; read 2:106 as inter-dispensational; treat most "abrogations" as specificatory.
- **Muḥammad al-Ghazālī** (the modern, d. 1996), *Naẓarāt fī al-Qurʾān*: skeptical of most cases.
- **Abū Muslim al-Iṣfahānī** (d. 322/934), already in the classical period: denied intra-Qurʾānic naskh entirely.

The trend line is: the more tightly one defines "abrogation," the shorter the list gets, because most alleged cases resolve into one of the following softer operations that should not be called naskh:

- *takhṣīṣ al-ʿāmm* (specification of a general statement by a later particular)
- *taqyīd al-muṭlaq* (qualification of an unrestricted ruling)
- *bayān al-mujmal* (elucidation of a summary)
- *takhfīf* (legislative easement, not cancellation)
- *tadarruj* (progressive legislation — teaching by stages)

Much of the shrinkage from ~250 to ~4 is absorbed by tadarruj (progressive legislation), which reframes "abrogation" as pedagogical sequencing.

## 4. Famous candidate pairs

### (a) Qibla change — Q 2:144 abrogating 2:142-143?

The Muslim community prayed toward Jerusalem for roughly 16-17 months after the hijra before the qibla was redirected toward the Kaʿba in Mecca. Classical jurists often cite this as a naskh case, pointing to the direction-toward-Jerusalem as the "abrogated" ruling.

But 2:142-143 does not legislate the Jerusalem qibla; it reports (in the past) that "we made the qibla that you were on" a test. The legislation-of-the-new qibla is in 2:144, but the legislation-of-the-old qibla is nowhere in the Qurʾān we have. Classical authorities place it in sunna-practice or in a pre-existing Abrahamic-Jewish consensus that the Prophet adopted. So the pair 2:142-143 / 2:144 is not a Qurʾān-to-Qurʾān abrogation but at most a sunna-to-Qurʾān succession (or a Qurʾānic ratification of a change in practice). Most moderns classify this as *not* naskh in the technical sense.

### (b) Wine prohibition — progressive Q 16:67 → 2:219 → 4:43 → 5:90-91

- **Q 16:67** (Meccan): vines and palms yield sakaran wa-rizqan ḥasanan — "an intoxicant and a goodly provision." Neutral-to-positive framing; sakar on one reading is a pre-Islamic word for fermented drink, on another an archaic word for nourishment; either way, no prohibition.
- **Q 2:219** (early Medinan): "they ask you about wine and gambling. Say: in both is a great sin and [some] benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit." Strong moral warning; no prohibition.
- **Q 4:43** (Medinan): "do not approach prayer while you are intoxicated, until you know what you say." Partial prohibition: no prayer while drunk. This implies drinking still happens outside prayer times.
- **Q 5:90-91** (late Medinan): "wine, gambling, sacrificial stones, and divining arrows are an abomination of Satan's work, so avoid it. ... Will you then desist?" Full prohibition.

Classical naskh theory counts 5:90-91 as abrogating both 4:43 and 2:219 and 16:67, but this is a paradigm case of *tadarruj*, progressive legislation, and not naskh. None of 16:67, 2:219, or 4:43 contains a ruling that 5:90-91 contradicts; each is a waypoint on an escalator. Ṣubḥī Ṣāliḥ and most moderns remove this from the abrogation list. The significance for our Phase-B project: the wine sequence is a *designed* legislative curriculum, which argues for deliberate composition of the corpus and is a positive data-point rather than a problem.

### (c) Widow waiting-period — Q 2:240 abrogated by Q 2:234

- **Q 2:240**: widows of the deceased receive one year of maintenance (matāʿ ilā al-ḥawl) and should not be turned out; if they leave voluntarily, no blame.
- **Q 2:234**: widows wait four months and ten days (ʿidda), then are free.

Classical tradition uniformly treats 2:234 as abrogating 2:240, with the one-year provision cancelled in favour of four-months-ten-days. This is the strongest "certain" case in every minimalist list and is one of Ṣubḥī Ṣāliḥ's four. The oddity is editorial: 2:234 precedes 2:240 in the muṣḥaf, so in reading-order the "abrogating" verse comes *before* the "abrogated." This is taken as evidence that the muṣḥaf is not arranged in reported-revelation-order, which everyone already knows.

Counter-reading (Rashīd Riḍā, Ṭabāṭabāʾī): 2:240 is not a competing waiting-period. It is a will/bequest provision — the husband *may* bequeath a year's maintenance and non-eviction to his widow, which is a protective generosity, while 2:234 gives the mandatory ʿidda. On that reading, the two verses are not in contradiction and there is no abrogation, only a combined picture: mandatory four-months-ten-days plus an optional bequest-protected year of residence. This is a live contested reading and should be flagged, not closed.

### (d) Night-prayer — Q 73:1-4 abrogated by Q 73:20

- **Q 73:1-4** (very early Meccan): "stand in prayer by night, save a little — half of it, or a little less, or a little more, and recite the Qurʾān in measured tone." This prescribes an extensive night vigil.
- **Q 73:20** (late Meccan or early Medinan): "your Lord knows you stand two-thirds of the night, half, a third, and a group of those with you ... he has turned to you in clemency — so recite what is easy from the Qurʾān."

The classical tradition treats 73:20 as abrogating 73:1-4, lightening the night-vigil obligation from "most of the night" to "what is easy." This is Ṣubḥī Ṣāliḥ's second certain case. Structurally it is a *takhfīf* (easement), which is arguably a sub-type of naskh rather than an overthrow. The interesting feature here is that both verses sit inside the same sūra — strong internal evidence that the sūra was composed or redacted with the later-period easement grafted onto the earlier vigil prescription, and that Q 73's present ordering is deliberately diachronic. For our project this is important: it is a case where a single sūra visibly encodes two phases of revelation, and we should expect to find similar grafting elsewhere.

### Other commonly listed cases (shortlist, not exhaustive)

- **Bequest to parents/near-kin**, Q 2:180, said to be abrogated by the fixed inheritance shares of Q 4:11-12.
- **Private counsel with the Prophet requiring prior alms**, Q 58:12 abrogated by Q 58:13 (Ṣubḥī Ṣāliḥ's fourth case; the two verses sit consecutively — again a designed graft).
- **Seclusion of adulterous women in the home** (Q 4:15) reportedly abrogated either by the stoning-verse tradition or by Q 24:2's flogging prescription.
- **Fighting during the sacred months** (Q 2:217 vs 9:5) — contested; 9:5 is often cited as abrogating many earlier non-combatant verses, but most moderns reject this as a category error.
- **Sayf-verse superiority claim** (Q 9:5 as "āyat al-sayf" allegedly abrogating dozens of peaceful verses) — this is the most politically charged and most widely rejected classical claim in modern scholarship; Ṣubḥī Ṣāliḥ explicitly rejects it.

## 5. Naskh typology: tilāwah vs ḥukm vs both

Classical uṣūl al-fiqh distinguishes three kinds of naskh:

1. **Naskh al-ḥukm dūna al-tilāwa** — the ruling is cancelled but the recitation remains. Example: Q 2:240's widow bequest. Standard and relatively well-attested.
2. **Naskh al-tilāwa dūna al-ḥukm** — the recitation is removed from the muṣḥaf but the ruling is still in force. The classical prime example is the so-called āyat al-rajm (stoning verse) and a reported verse about adult breastfeeding (riḍāʿat al-kabīr). This category is deeply problematic: it requires that a verse once was Qurʾān, is now not in the Qurʾān, and yet its legal force remains. If accepted, it concedes that the muṣḥaf is incomplete as a legal record; if rejected, it undercuts a set of ḥadīth-grounded rulings.
3. **Naskh al-tilāwa wa-l-ḥukm** — both recitation and ruling are cancelled. Example: the reported "ten clear suckings" ḥadīth (which was reportedly Qurʾān then reduced to five then removed altogether). This category is even more problematic than (2) because it posits Qurʾānic text that leaves no legal trace and no textual trace, i.e., purely hadith-attested lost Qurʾān.

Types (2) and (3) exist only because they rescue specific hadith-based rulings from the embarrassment of not being in the muṣḥaf. Modern minimalists (Ṣubḥī Ṣāliḥ; Muṣṭafā Zayd's major monograph *al-Naskh fī al-Qurʾān al-Karīm* reluctantly accepting some; ʿAbduh rejecting) are sharply divided on (2) and (3), with most either rejecting them outright or confining them to the stoning case. From a text-critical standpoint both are red flags — they describe an archaeology of loss without archaeological evidence.

## 6. The stoning verse (āyat al-rajm)

The classical claim, attributed to ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb in a famous sermon (al-Bukhārī, Muslim, Mālik via Muwaṭṭaʾ), holds that a verse once was part of the Qurʾān prescribing stoning (rajm) for adulterers, but that its recitation was abrogated while its ruling remained. A commonly reported form:

> al-shaykh wa-l-shaykhatu idhā zanayā fa-rjumūhumā al-battata nakālan mina-llāhi wa-llāhu ʿazīzun ḥakīm.
> "The elder man and the elder woman, when they commit zinā, stone them absolutely, a deterrent from God, and God is Mighty, Wise."

Observations:

1. The meter and rhythm of the reported line do not sit comfortably in Qurʾānic saj'. The syntax is flatter and the lexical register less marked than surrounding Q 24 material.
2. Q 24:2 prescribes 100 lashes for zinā with no exception for "elders" and without referring to stoning. If the stoning rule stood alongside it, one would expect the muṣḥaf to either encode the distinction or at least gesture toward it (e.g., via āyat istithnāʾ, a clause of exception). It does not.
3. The classical solution — naskh al-tilāwa dūna al-ḥukm — is doctrinally awkward and under-evidenced. It posits a verse that used to be Qurʾān but is not now, and yet whose ruling binds. This is a minority, contested position even in the classical tradition.
4. Modernist critiques (e.g., Muḥammad Shaḥrūr, Qurʾāniyyūn movement, and more moderately Muḥammad al-Ghazālī) either deny the abrogation category altogether or treat the stoning ruling as Mosaic-continuity sunna, not Qurʾānic.
5. For our project: the āyat al-rajm is methodologically a limit case. It is the strongest classical counter-witness to the claim that the muṣḥaf is the complete Qurʾān. It is also the weakest such counter-witness because its evidentiary base is a small number of companion reports about a text no one ever recited in canonical salat.

We should treat it as a case-in-dispute and decline to rely on it for any positive claim. If our structural analyses predict that Q 24's zinā section is internally complete and self-consistent, that is itself a relevant data-point against the rajm-verse hypothesis.

## 7. Implications for Phase B

- **n-s-kh is a two-polar root**, not a one-way "cancellation" verb. The Qur'an uses it as copy (7:154, 45:29) and as cancellation (22:52) and as a mixed/contested term (2:106). Any structural reading should retain this ambivalence.
- **Most "abrogations" are progressive legislation** (tadarruj), specification (takhṣīṣ), or qualification (taqyīd), and these are *features* of a designed curriculum rather than bugs in a revelation.
- **A small core of genuine cancellation pairs** (widow's bequest, night vigil, the consultation-alms verse, possibly a few others) is defensible and — importantly — in every case the pair sits in the same sūra or near-neighbouring contexts, suggesting deliberate redactional placement of diachronic material.
- **The tilāwah-only categories (especially the stoning verse)** should be flagged as contested and not relied upon.
- **The qibla case and the sword-verse case** are best treated as non-naskh phenomena misread under the naskh rubric.

For deep-hypothesis candidates downstream, the phenomena we actually want to model are: (a) progressive legislation as designed pedagogical sequence, (b) in-sūra grafting of earlier-and-later material as a deliberate redactional signature, and (c) the inter-dispensational reading of Q 2:106 as a claim about successive prophetic sign-dispensations rather than intra-Qurʾānic editing. None of these requires the full classical doctrine of naskh, and all of them are compatible with a tightly redacted corpus.
