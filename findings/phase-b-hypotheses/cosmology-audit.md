# Quranic Cosmology — Phase B Rigorous Audit

**Date:** 2026-04-12
**Scope:** Twelve cosmology-themed verses most frequently cited in modern "scientific miracle" (iʿjāz ʿilmī) literature.
**Method:** For each verse — (a) Arabic text and gloss, (b) classical tafsīr (Ṭabarī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr as available), (c) modern apologetic reading with its strongest form, (d) critical/philological objections, (e) a calibrated verdict on a 5-point scale.

**Verdict scale:**
- **V1 — Overreach.** Reading requires semantic force the Arabic does not bear; classical tafsīr excludes it; modern reading is retrofit.
- **V2 — Weak.** Possible but unmotivated; the classical sense is far better attested and the "scientific" match is loose.
- **V3 — Ambiguous.** The Arabic underdetermines the referent; both classical and modern readings are defensible; neither is distinctively confirmed.
- **V4 — Striking but inconclusive.** The language is unusually compatible with a modern finding, but (i) classical readings are not thereby falsified, and (ii) the match could be coincidence of metaphor.
- **V5 — Strong correspondence.** The Arabic is a close match in semantic content that is hard to generate from 7th-century cosmological assumptions alone.

No verse in this audit reaches V5. One (**ratq/fatq**) reaches a qualified V4. Most sit at V1–V2. The honest summary is in Section 14.

## 1. Q 21:30 — ratq / fatq ("joined, then We separated")

**Arabic:** *a-wa-lam yara alladhīna kafarū anna al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa kānatā ratqan fa-fataqnāhumā, wa-jaʿalnā mina al-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy. a-fa-lā yuʾminūn.*

"Have those who disbelieved not seen that the heavens and the earth were **ratq** (a joined/sealed entity), and We **fataqnāhumā** (pulled them apart), and We made from water every living thing?"

**Lexical base.** Root *r-t-q*: to sew shut, to seal, to bind together tightly (used of a torn garment mended, of a woman with an imperforate anatomy — *ratqāʾ*). Root *f-t-q*: to rip a seam open, to split, to unstitch. The pair *ratq/fatq* is a seamstress-and-tailoring metaphor before it is ever a cosmological one.

**Classical tafsīr.** The dominant reading is meteorological-agricultural, and it is striking how uniform this is across otherwise disagreeing exegetes.
- **Ṭabarī** (*Jāmiʿ al-bayān*): two dominant opinions from the earliest layer. (a) Ibn ʿAbbās and Ḥasan al-Baṣrī: the heavens were **sealed without rain** and the earth sealed **without plants**; God pulled them apart so rain descends and plants emerge. (b) Mujāhid, Saʿīd b. Jubayr: heavens and earth were fused as one mass, and God separated them into distinct realms. Ṭabarī himself prefers (a) because the closing clause — "and We made from water every living thing" — thematically governs the *fatq*.
- **Zamakhsharī** (*Kashshāf*): same two options, with a stylistic note that *ratq* and *fatq* form an auditory pair (jinās) and that the verse works as a rhetorical wonder-argument, not a primitive cosmogony.
- **Rāzī** (*Mafātīḥ al-ghayb*): lists five variants, including (iii) the night was joined to the day until God separated them; (iv) the heavens were a single mass of smoke that was separated into seven layers; (v) the whole cosmos was one, and God divided it. Rāzī considers (i)–(ii) most consistent with the Arabic.
- **Qurṭubī** and **Ibn Kathīr** essentially reproduce Ṭabarī's preferred reading.

Only the minority opinions (iv–v) attributed to Mujāhid and others come close to a "cosmic separation" — and even there the imagery is not a singularity exploding but a mass being cleaved into strata.

**Modern apologetic reading.** The verse anticipates the primordial hot-dense state separating into the matter that formed cosmic structure (Big Bang cosmology, Lemaître 1927, Hubble 1929, Penzias–Wilson 1965). Proponents (Bucaille 1976; Zindani; Naik) emphasize that *ratq* — "fused" — and *fatq* — "separated" — map onto "singularity" and "expansion/nucleosynthesis."

**Critique.**
- The grammatical subject is **"heavens and earth"** — a 7th-century cosmological category, not elementary particles. In Quranic cosmology the *arḍ* (earth) exists from the start as a distinct category (cf. Q 41:9, where the earth is created in two days *before* the heavens are "smoothed out" in 41:12 — a sequence that does not match Big Bang chronology).
- The closing conjunction — "**and** We made from water every living thing" — is almost certainly the rhetorical point. In Ṭabarī's reading, *ratq/fatq* and *water → life* are one continuous agricultural image.
- The *ratq/fatq* pair is borrowed from textile and rainfall discourse. Pre-Islamic poetry uses *fataqa* for the sky splitting open to let rain fall (cf. Q 78:14, *al-muʿṣirāt* — the rain-pressing clouds).
- Big Bang cosmology did not describe a "joined heavens and earth." In the Planck era there was no distinct Earth. Retrofitting the two categories onto the singularity already requires an ad-hoc mapping.

**Verdict: V3 with a minor lean toward V4.** The apologetic reading is not *textually* impossible — Mujāhid's minority tafsīr shows that a "cosmic mass → division" interpretation was available in the 1st–2nd Islamic centuries. But the preferred classical reading is meteorological, the linguistic register is artisanal (sewing/tearing), and the "two separated entities" are explicitly "heavens" and "earth," not the continuum of spacetime. The verse is interpretively interesting — there is a real sense in which a 7th-century Arabic cosmogony saying "the cosmos was originally one thing that was differentiated" is more compatible with modern cosmology than most ANE parallels — but the evidentiary weight is constrained by the fact that ancient cosmogonies *routinely* begin with an undifferentiated primordium (Hesiod's Chaos, Genesis 1:2, Enūma Eliš's Apsu-Tiamat, Egyptian Nun). Being in the same neighborhood as a general cosmogonic trope does not amount to a specific anticipation of Hubble-Lemaître cosmology.

## 2. Q 51:47 — *bi-aydin wa-innā la-mūsiʿūn*

**Arabic:** *wa-l-samāʾa banaynāhā bi-aydin wa-innā la-mūsiʿūn.*

"And the heaven — We built it with [strength / hands] and indeed We are **mūsiʿūn**."

**Lexical base.** *Ayd* (not *aydī*, "hands," plural of *yad*) is the verbal noun meaning "strength, might." *Wāsiʿ* / *mūsiʿ* (Form IV active participle of *w-s-ʿ*): one who makes wide, one who has capacity, one who is generous/abundant. The root is stative-locative: *waṣiʿa l-makānu* = "the place became spacious."

**Classical tafsīr.** Near-total consensus against a dynamic reading.
- **Ṭabarī**: *mūsiʿūn* = *dhū saʿah* = "possessing capacity / ample provision." The verse is about God's power and provision, not about the heavens stretching.
- **Zamakhsharī**: *mūsiʿūn li-l-khalq bi-rizqinā* — "we are bounteous to creation with our sustenance." He notes the semantic bridge to *al-wāsiʿ*, a divine name (Q 2:247, 2:261, etc.).
- **Rāzī**: three options: (a) power to provide sustenance; (b) spaciousness of the heaven itself as a container (the sky is vast); (c) power unbounded. None of them is "the heaven is continuously expanding."
- **Qurṭubī** and **Ibn Kathīr**: follow Ṭabarī.

It is especially important that no classical exegete read *mūsiʿūn* with an imperfective/progressive aspect ("we are [currently, ongoingly] expanding it"). Arabic participles express state or habitual property, not continuous process in the English sense. *Mūsiʿūn* predicated of God means "we are those characterized by width/capacity/generosity."

**Modern apologetic reading.** Maurice Bucaille's 1976 *La Bible, le Coran et la science* made this the paradigm case, claiming the participle encodes the expansion of the universe (Hubble redshift, 1929). The reading is reinforced by translators who render *mūsiʿūn* as "[ever] expanding."

**Critique.**
- **Grammatical:** Arabic active participles are not present-continuous. *Innā la-mūsiʿūn* is "we are those possessing wide capacity," parallel in form to *innā la-qādirūn* ("we are capable") — a divine-attribute predication, not an event description.
- **Object:** if the verse meant "we are expanding **it** [the heaven]," Arabic would naturally supply a pronoun — *mūsiʿūhā* or *namuddu al-samāʾa*. The absolute participle *mūsiʿūn* without object is the cue that it is an attribute, not a transitive action.
- **Lexical distribution:** *al-Wāsiʿ* as a divine name (Q 2:115, 2:247, 2:261, 2:268, 3:73, 4:130, 5:54, 24:32, 53:32) is overwhelmingly used of God's provision (*rizq*) and forgiveness (*maghfirah*), not cosmology.
- **Classical reception:** zero pre-modern reader saw "cosmic expansion" here; this is an exclusively post-1929 reading.

**Verdict: V2.** The Arabic does not say the universe is expanding. It says God has (or exercises) vast capacity, in parallel with his being "he who built the heavens." The apologetic reading requires (i) mistranslating a stative participle as a continuous verb, and (ii) suppressing the strong classical consensus that *mūsiʿūn* refers to divine amplitude/provision. This is one of the clearest cases of post-hoc mapping in the iʿjāz literature.

## 3. Q 21:33 and Q 36:38–40 — "each swimming in a *falak*"

**Arabic (21:33):** *wa-huwa alladhī khalaqa al-layla wa-l-nahāra wa-l-shamsa wa-l-qamara, kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.*

"It is He who created the night and the day, the sun and the moon — each in a *falak* swimming."

**Arabic (36:38–40):** *wa-l-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā... wa-l-qamara qaddarnāhu manāzila ḥattā ʿāda ka-l-ʿurjūni al-qadīm. lā al-shamsu yanbaghī lahā an tudrika al-qamara wa-lā al-laylu sābiqu al-nahār, wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.*

"The sun runs to a resting-place for it... the moon we have decreed stations for it until it returns like the old date-palm stalk. The sun is not able to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day — each in a *falak* swimming."

**Lexical base.** *Falak*: a "sphere" or "orbital circle" in astronomical usage; in pre-Islamic Arabic poetry it can denote the celestial vault or the turning heavens (cf. Aws b. Ḥajar, al-Nābighah). The verb *sabaḥa* (to swim, to glide) is also used of a running horse and of stars traversing their path. Both words are astronomical commonplaces in the Hellenistic-Babylonian-Aramaic milieu.

**Classical tafsīr.**
- **Ṭabarī, Qurṭubī**: *falak* is the "sphere" (in the sense of the celestial sphere / orbital circle) in which each heavenly body moves. The standard gloss is a Ptolemaic-Aristotelian celestial sphere. Ṭabarī cites traditions comparing *falak* to a millstone or a spinning wheel.
- **Zamakhsharī**: *al-falak majrā al-kawākib* — "*falak* is the path/track of the stars." He makes explicit the kinematic picture: each body moves on its assigned track, not colliding.
- **Rāzī** discusses at length whether the *aflāk* are material crystalline spheres (Aristotelian) or merely imagined tracks (an Ashʿarī preference, to avoid implying uncreated celestial matter). Either way: geocentric spheres/circles.

**Modern apologetic reading.** "Each in an orbit swimming" is said to encode (a) that orbits are not straight lines, (b) that the sun has its own orbital motion (around the Milky Way's Sagittarius A*), (c) that celestial bodies do not collide because of celestial mechanics.

**Critique.**
- A "*falak*" is a **sphere/cycle**, exactly the unit of pre-Copernican cosmology. Speaking of the sun "in a *falak*" was *more* naturally said in the Ptolemaic system than in the post-Newtonian one. Apologists cannot have it both ways — counting *falak* as "orbit" when it evokes modern ellipses, and ignoring that the same word in 7th-century usage was the hallmark of geocentric cosmology.
- The line "the sun is not able to overtake the moon" in 36:40 is straightforwardly geocentric and already part of the ancient near-eastern "choreography of the lights" topos.
- The sun's "galactic orbit" around Sagittarius A* takes ~225 million years and was unknown until the 20th century. But the verse does not specify *what* the sun orbits — the implied referent is the earth, as standard in the period.
- *yasbaḥūn* (to swim/glide) is used of stars in pre-Islamic poetry. It is metaphorical, not mechanical.

**Verdict: V2.** Compatible with modern astronomy only at the trivial level that celestial bodies do move on non-linear paths, which every ancient culture already accepted. The specific Ptolemaic-Aristotelian lexicon argues against a privileged modern reading.

## 4. Q 55:33 — "penetrating the regions of heaven and earth"

**Arabic:** *yā maʿshara al-jinni wa-l-insi, in istaṭaʿtum an tanfudhū min aqṭāri al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi fa-nfudhū; lā tanfudhūna illā bi-sulṭān.*

"O company of jinn and humankind — if you are able to pass through the regions of the heavens and the earth, then pass through; you will not pass through except with **sulṭān** (authority/warrant/power)."

**Classical tafsīr.** The setting is **eschatological**. The *sūrat al-Raḥmān* is a refrain-structured oath/judgement discourse; verses 31–36 address the Day of Judgement.
- **Ṭabarī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr**: *sulṭān* means divine **authority/warrant** (as in Q 17:65, "you have no *sulṭān* over my servants"). The challenge is: you cannot escape God's jurisdiction. Even if you could flee through the dimensions of creation, you could not escape without a *warrant* — which you do not possess.
- **Rāzī**: makes the rhetorical structure explicit — this is a challenge-formula ("if you can... then do") designed to assert the impossibility of escape on the Day of Recompense.

**Modern apologetic reading.** Humans and jinn can penetrate heaven and earth only by *sulṭān* — interpreted as "technology/power" (e.g., rocketry). The verse is said to anticipate space travel.

**Critique.**
- *Sulṭān* in the Quran almost never means "technology." It means **authority, warrant, or manifest proof** (cf. Q 3:151, 7:71, 11:96, 17:33, 37:30, 52:38). The word is semantically cognate with English "sultan" — dominion, not devices.
- The eschatological setting is explicit. The preceding verse 55:31 — *sa-nafrughu lakum ayyuhā al-thaqalān* ("We shall settle your account, O two species") — frames the entire passage as final judgement.
- The verb *tanfudhū* ("penetrate, pass through") is used in the sense of *escape* here, not "explore." "If you could pass through the edges of creation..." is a standard ANE idiom for "even if you fled to the ends of the earth..." (cf. Psalm 139:7–8, Amos 9:2).

**Verdict: V1.** The "space travel" reading is a clear overreach. It ignores the eschatological frame, mistranslates *sulṭān*, and inverts the rhetorical function of the verse (impossibility of escape) into its opposite (possibility of conquest).

## 5. Q 41:11 — "the heaven was *dukhān* (smoke)"

**Arabic:** *thumma istawā ilā al-samāʾi wa-hiya dukhānun, fa-qāla lahā wa-li-l-arḍi: iʾtiyā ṭawʿan aw karhan, qālatā: ataynā ṭāʾiʿīn.*

"Then He turned toward the heaven while it was **dukhān** (smoke/vapor), and said to it and to the earth: 'Come willingly or unwillingly.' They said: 'We come willingly.'"

**Classical tafsīr.** Universal reading: "smoke" = a vapor-like primordium. Several traditions cite Ibn ʿAbbās: the heaven was smoke rising from the surface of a primordial water.
- **Ṭabarī**: cites the Ibn ʿAbbās tradition that God's throne was on water, and the heavens were fashioned from vapor rising from that water.
- **Zamakhsharī**: *dukhān* is vapor-smoke — a transparent reading of material cosmogony.

This is directly paralleled by:
- **Genesis 1:2** — the spirit of God moving over the face of the waters;
- **Enūma Eliš** — the creation from the primordial waters Tiamat and Apsu;
- **Egyptian Nun** — the undifferentiated primordial ocean.

**Modern apologetic reading.** *Dukhān* is identified with the primordial **nebular gas/plasma cloud** of modern cosmology (cf. the hot, ionized gas of the recombination era, or pre-galactic nebulae).

**Critique.**
- *Dukhān* is not *plasma* and not *nebular gas* in any technical sense. It is smoke — a visible suspension of particulate matter from combustion, which 7th-century Arabic speakers saw rising from fires. The analogy is *visual*, not chemical.
- The cosmogony as a whole is explicitly sequential, with the **earth** (Q 41:9–10) created in two days *before* the heavens are addressed in 41:11–12. That contradicts the modern sequence (in which Earth forms ~9 Gyr after the first hydrogen clouds).
- The shared ANE motif — creation from a vapory or watery primordium — is so widespread that the Quranic version is best read as continuous with that tradition, not as a special anticipation of stellar nucleosynthesis.

**Verdict: V2.** Loose resonance with "the universe began in a gaseous/undifferentiated state" at the level of general ANE cosmogony. Specific identification with modern nebular theory requires ignoring the fine-grained content (earth-first sequence, "smoke" vs "plasma," the dialogic address).

## 6. Q 15:14–15 — "a gate opened in the heaven"

**Arabic:** *wa-law fataḥnā ʿalayhim bāban mina al-samāʾi fa-ẓallū fīhi yaʿrujūn, la-qālū: innamā sukkirat abṣārunā bal naḥnu qawmun masḥūrūn.*

"Even if We opened for them a gate in the heaven and they kept ascending through it, they would say: 'Our eyes have only been blurred — no, we are a people bewitched.'"

**Classical tafsīr.**
- **Ṭabarī**: the sense is rhetorical-psychological. Even confronted with the clearest miracle — an open gate into the heaven, through which they continually ascend — the deniers would still reject it as illusion/magic. *Sukkirat* = "dimmed, obscured."
- **Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr**: same. The verse is about hardness of heart, not physiology.

**Modern apologetic reading.** Some readings (Kaheel, others) interpret *sukkirat abṣārunā* as oxygen deprivation or the physiological effects of high altitude — hypoxic visual impairment, cerebral hypoxia.

**Critique.**
- *Sukkira* (from the root *s-k-r*) is the same root as *sukr* (drunkenness) — "their eyes were made drunken/blurred." It is a standard Arabic metaphor for mental, not physiological, clouding.
- The next clause — *bal naḥnu qawmun masḥūrūn* ("no, we are a people bewitched") — identifies the alleged cause as magic, not altitude.
- Rhetorical force of the verse: an argument from denial. Even the maximal miracle would not persuade the hard-hearted. The verse is pedagogical, not medical.

**Verdict: V1.** The hypoxia reading collapses a rhetorical hyperbole into a physiological description it does not support. Classical tafsīr is unanimous in reading 15:14–15 as a psychological-theological statement about disbelief.

## 7. Q 78:12–13 — "seven firm/sturdy heavens"

**Arabic:** *wa-banaynā fawqakum sabʿan shidādā, wa-jaʿalnā sirājan wahhājā.*

"And We built above you seven **shidād** (firm ones), and We made a blazing lamp."

Parallels: Q 2:29, Q 17:44, Q 41:12, Q 65:12, Q 67:3, Q 71:15.

**Classical tafsīr.**
- Universal: *sabʿ shidād* = seven solid/firm heavens. **Q 41:12** specifies they were made in two days and "adorned with lamps" (the stars); **Q 67:3** describes them as stacked strata (*ṭibāqan*); **Q 65:12** pairs "seven heavens and similarly [*mithlahunna*] of the earth."
- Ṭabarī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr all describe seven literal superposed heavens, each with its own floor/ceiling structure, on which the celestial bodies move.

**Pre-Islamic and ANE backdrop.** The seven-heavens cosmology is found in:
- **2 Enoch / 3 Enoch** (Jewish apocalyptic, pre-Christian to early centuries CE);
- **Babylonian cosmology** — seven levels of celestial geography;
- **Testament of Levi**, **Apocalypse of Abraham**, **Ascension of Isaiah** — all pre-Islamic;
- **Talmud (Ḥagigah 12b)** — explicit seven-heavens list (Vilon, Raqiaʿ, Sheḥaqim, Zebul, Maʿon, Makhon, Arabot).

**Modern apologetic reading.** The "seven heavens" are identified with atmospheric layers (troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, exosphere, ionosphere, magnetosphere — though this requires fudging the count), or with cosmological scale layers, or with some other septenary modern schema.

**Critique.**
- The number **seven** is an ANE literary-cosmological constant, not a count obtained from observation. Using *sabʿ* to evoke completeness is a fixed convention (cf. seven earths in Q 65:12 — which no apologist matches to seven geological layers coherently).
- The modern atmospheric-layer division is conventional and variable (some schemes give five, some six, some seven, depending on which boundaries are counted); the match is retrofit.
- Q 67:5 names the "lowest heaven" as the one adorned with *maṣābīḥ* (lamps = stars). That places the stars in the first heaven, which is incoherent with any atmospheric-layer reading and coheres only with the pre-Copernican picture where the stars are on the first celestial sphere as seen from earth.

**Verdict: V1.** Seven-heavens cosmology is a direct inheritance from late antique Jewish and Babylonian cosmography. No modern reading succeeds without systematic distortion of the text.

## 8. Q 86:3 — *al-najm al-thāqib*

**Arabic:** *wa-l-samāʾi wa-l-ṭāriq, wa-mā adrāka mā al-ṭāriq, al-najmu al-thāqib.*

"By the heaven and the *ṭāriq*! And what will tell you what the *ṭāriq* is? The piercing star."

**Classical tafsīr.**
- *Ṭāriq*: "the one who comes by night / knocks at night" — a stock Arabic epithet for nocturnal visitors and, by metaphorical extension, for stars that appear at night.
- *Thāqib*: from *th-q-b*, "to pierce, to bore through." Applied to a star, it denotes **brightness that pierces the darkness** — a poetic commonplace. Ṭabarī cites traditions identifying it variously with Saturn, with a general bright star, or with a specific constellation.
- Zamakhsharī, Qurṭubī: same.

**Modern apologetic reading.** *Al-najm al-thāqib* ("the piercing/pulsing star") is identified with **pulsars** (discovered by Jocelyn Bell, 1967), whose pulsed emissions "pierce" or "throb."

**Critique.**
- *Thāqib* means to pierce by penetration or brightness, not to *pulse*. The Arabic for "pulsating" would be *nābiḍ* or *mutadhabdhib*, not *thāqib*.
- Pre-Islamic poetry uses *thāqib* of arrows, spears, and bright stars (Imruʾ al-Qays, al-Muʿallaqāt). The usage is **common** poetic currency, not a hidden astrophysical reference.
- The match to pulsars depends on a translation that does not follow from the Arabic.

**Verdict: V1.** One of the clearest cases of etymological fabrication in the iʿjāz literature. *Thāqib* is a pre-Islamic poetic cliché for a bright star, not a reference to pulsed electromagnetic emission.

## 9. Q 36:38 — "the sun runs to its *mustaqarr*"

**Arabic:** *wa-l-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā; dhālika taqdīru al-ʿazīzi al-ʿalīm.*

"And the sun runs to a *mustaqarr* (resting-place/appointed limit) for it. That is the decree of the Mighty, the Knowing."

**Classical tafsīr.** Two clusters of opinion:
- (a) **Temporal** — the sun runs to its *mustaqarr* in the sense of the eschatological end (the Day when it will be rolled up, Q 81:1). This is the majority view among mufassirūn.
- (b) **Spatial** — the sun runs to a daily resting-place (under the Throne, per a hadith tradition in Bukhārī/Muslim where the sun prostrates beneath the Throne each night before continuing its course). This reading is pre-scientific and geocentric.
- A variant reading (qirāʾah of Ibn Masʿūd) has *lā mustaqarra lahā* — "to no resting-place for it" — which pushes toward a "ceaseless motion" interpretation.

**Modern apologetic reading.** The sun's *mustaqarr* = the galactic orbit around Sagittarius A* (~225 Myr period), or the solar apex direction toward the Hercules–Lyra border, or the eventual red-giant endpoint.

**Critique.**
- The dominant classical reading is either eschatological-temporal or the geocentric "sun prostrates under the Throne" hadith — not galactic astronomy.
- *Tajrī* ("runs") is appropriate for ancient solar-motion imagery (cf. the sun-chariot topos across Indo-European and Semitic traditions) and does not uniquely encode orbital motion.
- The "apparent solar motion through the zodiac" was perfectly familiar to pre-Islamic Arabs and to every Hellenistic astronomer. The verse says nothing that requires heliocentric or galactic insight.

**Verdict: V2.** The verse is compatible with modern solar astronomy in a weak sense (the sun does move, and it does have a kind of terminus), but the compatibility is the same compatibility that any pre-scientific solar-motion verse would have. The specific galactic-orbit claim is underdetermined.

## 10. Q 71:15–16 and Q 25:61 — *nūr* vs *sirāj*

**Arabic (71:15–16):** *alam taraw kayfa khalaqa Allāhu sabʿa samāwātin ṭibāqā, wa-jaʿala al-qamara fīhinna nūran wa-jaʿala al-shamsa sirājā.*

"Have you not seen how God created seven heavens stacked in layers, and made the moon in them a **nūr** (light) and made the sun a **sirāj** (lamp)?"

Also Q 25:61: *wa-jaʿala fīhā sirājan wa-qamaran munīrā* — "and placed in it [the heaven] a lamp and a moon giving light."

**Lexical base.**
- *Nūr*: general word for "light" — any light, source or received (cf. "*Allāh nūr al-samāwāti*" in Q 24:35, where God is "the light of the heavens"; also Q 5:15, 5:44, 5:46, 6:91, 33:46 — light as illumination in general, often reflected or conceptual, not intrinsically "borrowed").
- *Sirāj*: lamp — a physical light-emitting object (oil lamp, torch). In Q 33:46, Muḥammad himself is called *sirājan munīrā* — "an illuminating lamp." The word denotes function (light-source), not mechanism (fuel burning).
- *Munīr* (from *n-w-r*): "light-giving, illuminating" — applied to both the moon (Q 25:61) and the Prophet (Q 33:46).

**Classical tafsīr.**
- **Ṭabarī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr**: unanimous in glossing *sirāj* as *al-sirāj al-wahhāj* (the blazing lamp) — heat-and-light combined — and *nūr* as "light." No classical tafsīr derives from this verse the doctrine that the moon's light is reflected. The Ptolemaic astronomical tradition — well-known in Islamic civilization by the 9th century — did teach that the moon shines by sunlight, but the Arabic of the verse is not the source of that knowledge.

**Modern apologetic reading.** The lexical distinction is claimed to encode a precise physical difference: *sirāj* = emitter, *nūr* = receiver/reflector, anticipating the modern discovery that the moon reflects sunlight.

**Critique.**
- **Ancient Greek astronomy already knew the moon reflects** (Anaxagoras, 5th c. BCE; Aristotle; Ptolemy). This was not a 20th-century discovery.
- *Nūr* is **not semantically restricted to reflected light.** In Q 24:35 ("*Allāhu nūru al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ*"), God is nūr — source light, par excellence. In Q 33:46 the Prophet is *sirājan munīrā* — both sirāj and nūr-derivative. The two roots overlap heavily; they do not form a clean emitter/reflector pair.
- If the verse were distinguishing emission from reflection, Q 10:5 — *huwa alladhī jaʿala al-shamsa ḍiyāʾan wa-l-qamara nūran* ("He made the sun *ḍiyāʾ* and the moon *nūr*") — creates a problem: the word for the sun shifts from *sirāj* to *ḍiyāʾ* (brilliance/radiance), which is itself closer to *nūr* than to a "lamp." The lexical mapping is not consistent enough to encode a physics.

**Verdict: V2.** The lexical distinction between *sirāj* and *nūr* is real but general (functional lamp vs light-in-general), not a specialized emitter/reflector distinction. The claim of scientific foreknowledge requires (i) ignoring that Greek astronomy already had the mechanism, (ii) forcing *nūr* into a meaning it does not consistently carry in the Quran. Lexical-semantic overreach, though less egregious than cases V1.

## 11. Q 79:30–31 — *wa-l-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā*

**Arabic:** *wa-l-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā. akhraja minhā māʾahā wa-marʿāhā.*

"And the earth — after that He **daḥā**-ed it. He brought forth from it its water and its pasture."

**Lexical base.** The root *d-ḥ-w/y*:
- Primary meaning in classical dictionaries (*Lisān al-ʿarab*, *Tāj al-ʿarūs*): **to spread out, to extend, to flatten**. *Daḥā al-farrāshu al-firāsha* = the attendant spread the bed out.
- Derived noun *al-udḥiyyah*: the shallow depression that an ostrich scrapes out in the sand to lay its eggs. **This is the origin of the "egg" association** — not *daḥā* means "egg," but the noun for the ostrich's scraped nest shares the root. The egg-reading is a secondary folk etymology.
- *Midḥāh*: a smoothing/spreading tool.

**Classical tafsīr.** Ṭabarī, Zamakhsharī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr: *daḥāhā* = *basaṭahā*, "He spread it out." Parallel to Q 91:6 (*wa-l-arḍi wa-mā ṭaḥāhā* — "by the earth and Him who spread it"), Q 13:3, 15:19 (*wa-l-arḍa madadnāhā* — "and the earth, We extended it"), Q 51:48 (*wa-l-arḍa farashnāhā* — "and the earth, We made it a couch"), Q 78:6 (*alam najʿali al-arḍa mihādā* — "have we not made the earth a cradle").

The classical picture is a wide, spread-out earth — not flat in the strict cosmological sense (some Muslim astronomers, following Greek science, knew the earth was a sphere), but the Quranic *lexicon* uses spreading/extending/flattening verbs consistently.

**Modern apologetic reading.** *Daḥā* is claimed to mean "ostrich-egg-shaped," identifying the earth as an oblate spheroid.

**Critique.**
- Primary lexical meaning (by a large margin) is "spread out." The egg-meaning is via a derived noun (*daḥya, udḥiyyah*) for an ostrich's ground-scrape, and even that has the semantic primitive of *the scraped depression in the sand*, not *the egg itself*.
- If the Quran meant "God made the earth egg-shaped," idiomatic Arabic options include *kuwwirat* (cf. Q 39:5, *yukawwiru al-layla ʿalā al-nahār*, "He rolls the night over the day" — using the root *k-w-r* which is directly about spherical/rolling motion). The Quran does use spherical language for other cosmological matters (39:5) but does not use it of the earth.
- The parallel earth-verses (13:3, 15:19, 51:48, 78:6, 88:20) consistently use *spreading/extending/flattening* vocabulary.

**Verdict: V1/V2.** The primary lexical sense of *daḥā* is "spread out," which fits the classical flat-to-wide-expanse picture perfectly. The egg-reading is a post-hoc etymological stretch via a secondary derived noun. The verse is not a clandestine statement of oblate spheroidality.

## 12. Q 88:20 and Q 16:15 — "the mountains, how they were erected"

**Arabic (88:20):** *wa-ilā al-jibāli kayfa nuṣibat* — "and toward the mountains, how they were set up / erected."

**Arabic (16:15):** *wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum* — "and He cast on the earth *rawāsī* (anchoring weights/mountains) so that it [the earth] would not sway with you."

Similarly 21:31, 31:10, 78:7 (*wa-l-jibāla awtādā* — "the mountains as stakes/pegs").

**Classical tafsīr.**
- *Rawāsī*: anchoring weights, stabilizers. *Awtād*: tent-pegs. The classical tafsīr (Ṭabarī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, Rāzī) read these consistently: mountains keep the earth from tilting/swaying. This is a pre-modern geostatic concept — the earth is imagined as an expanse that could pitch without ballast.
- *Nuṣibat* (88:20): "erected, set firm."

**Modern apologetic reading.** Mountains as "tent-pegs" anticipates modern isostasy and the concept of mountain roots (the "root" of Mount Everest extending ~45 km below sea level). Orogeny and plate tectonics are said to be encoded here.

**Critique.**
- Tent-pegs go *down*, which gives rhetorical support to the "mountain roots" reading — this is the strongest iʿjāz case in this cluster. However:
- The *function* given in the Quran is **to prevent the earth from swaying** (*an tamīda bikum*). This is the opposite of modern geology, where plate tectonics tells us that mountain formation is *caused by* crustal movement — mountains are a consequence, not a preventative, of lithospheric motion.
- Isostasy (Airy, Pratt, 19th c.) is about mountains floating in buoyant equilibrium on the mantle, not about anchoring. Mountain "roots" are crustal thickening, not pegs driven into a substrate.
- The tent-peg metaphor is pre-Islamic and functions in a specific semantic field: the earth is imagined as a tent (*mihād, firāsh*), the mountains are its pegs holding it taut. This is a coherent pre-modern cosmology, not cryptic geophysics.

**Verdict: V2/V3.** The "mountains have depth" aspect is genuinely suggestive; it is the best-looking modern match in the mountain-related verses. But the **function** attributed in the Quran (stabilizing against sway) is in direct tension with modern orogenic theory (mountains are *products* of the tectonic motion they are said to prevent). The apologetic reading has to ignore the function to extract the morphology.

## 13. Cross-cutting observations

### 13.1 The Arabic of the Quran is consistently 7th-century Arabian Arabic.
In none of these verses does a term appear that cannot be accounted for from pre-Islamic poetry, Jewish/Christian apocalyptic, or Greco-Aramaic learned discourse available in late-antique Arabia. The lexicon is dense with **metaphors drawn from visible Arabian life**: tents, pegs, lamps, seams, rain, smoke, gates, tracks. Modern readings succeed by abstracting these terms into generic "scientific" concepts, but they do not arise from the Arabic itself.

### 13.2 Classical tafsīr rarely supports the iʿjāz readings.
With the **partial exception of ratq/fatq** (where Mujāhid's minority reading is at least in the neighborhood of a cosmic-separation image), the classical tafsīr tradition reads these verses in ways that *contradict* the modern apologetic readings. This is a significant datum: the first thousand years of Islamic interpretation did not see a hidden science in these verses. Modern iʿjāz is a 20th-century phenomenon shaped by apologetic response to Western scientific hegemony (beginning systematically with Ṭanṭāwī Jawharī's *al-Jawāhir*, 1923–1935; consolidated by Bucaille, 1976; popularized by Naik, Zindani, and Kaheel).

### 13.3 ANE parallels are dense.
Every motif on this list has a pre-Islamic antecedent:
- Seven heavens: 2 Enoch, Talmud Ḥagigah 12b;
- Primordial water/vapor: Genesis 1:2, Enūma Eliš;
- Sun on a sphere or in a chariot: Greek, Egyptian, Mesopotamian;
- Mountains as stabilizers: Ugaritic, Akkadian cosmology;
- Earth as an expanse: Psalm 136:6, Isaiah 40:22 (*chūg ha-āretz*, "the circle of the earth").

This does **not** entail that the Quran borrows consciously — but it does entail that we cannot count any of these motifs as uniquely predictive of modern science.

### 13.4 Stronger vs weaker cases.
Within this cluster, the cases rank roughly:
1. **Ratq/fatq (Q 21:30)** — interpretively most interesting; V3–V4.
2. **Mountains as stakes (Q 78:7; 88:20)** — morphologically suggestive but functionally wrong; V2–V3.
3. **Smoke cosmogony (41:11)** — shared ANE motif; V2.
4. **Sun runs to its resting-place (36:38)** — shared with any solar-motion text; V2.
5. **Moon as nūr, sun as sirāj (71:15–16)** — partial lexical basis but post-hoc; V2.
6. **"Swimming in a falak" (21:33)** — Ptolemaic lexicon, V2.
7. **Dahāhā as "egg-shaped" (79:30)** — folk-etymological; V1/V2.
8. **Seven firm heavens (78:12)** — direct ANE inheritance; V1.
9. **Mūsiʿūn as cosmic expansion (51:47)** — grammatically forced; V2.
10. **"Penetrate heaven with sulṭān" (55:33)** — category error; V1.
11. **"Eyes blurred on ascent" (15:14–15)** — rhetorical, not physiological; V1.
12. **Al-najm al-thāqib as pulsar (86:3)** — etymologically fabricated; V1.

## 14. Summary verdict

The honest reading of the iʿjāz ʿilmī literature as a whole is: **it is a defensive apologetic genre, not a form of exegesis**. It retrofits 20th-century scientific concepts onto Quranic vocabulary that classical exegetes — who had every incentive to find anticipatory wonders — did not read that way.

This is not a dismissal. Three qualified observations:

1. The Quran's cosmogonic vocabulary is **unusually ambiguous** in ways that make retrofit relatively easy. This is partly because Arabic is morphologically rich (active participles, root ambiguity) and partly because the Quran's cosmological discourse is rhetorical-theological rather than descriptively technical. That ambiguity is a fact worth noting.

2. **Ratq/fatq (21:30)** is a genuine case where a minority classical reading (Mujāhid) and the modern reading converge on a "differentiated-from-undifferentiated" cosmogony. This is not a Hubble-Lemaître prediction, but it is an unusually clean formulation of a cosmogonic topos that also happens to align, at a general level, with modern cosmology. That deserves the label "interpretively interesting."

3. The overreach is concentrated in **retranslation tricks** (*mūsiʿūn* as "expanding," *thāqib* as "pulsing," *daḥā* as "egg-shaped," *sulṭān* as "technology"). In each case the modern reading requires shifting the Arabic word out of its attested classical semantic range. This is diagnostic of apologetic, not exegetic, motivation.

**The Quran's cosmology is a competent late-antique cosmology.** It draws on Arabian, Jewish, Christian, and Greco-Aramaic materials; it uses them for theological and rhetorical ends; it is not a secret physics textbook. Acknowledging this is compatible with taking the Quran seriously as a religious text. The iʿjāz ʿilmī program, by insisting on the hidden-science reading, paradoxically **narrows** the text — it substitutes the richness of late-antique cosmology for a thin list of 20th-century cross-references.

## Appendix: Method note

This audit triangulates three sources:
- **Classical tafsīr** (Ṭabarī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) — read for their earliest-attested glosses, not their theological conclusions.
- **Arabic lexicography** (*Lisān al-ʿarab*, *Tāj al-ʿarūs*, *Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib) — read for root semantics.
- **ANE parallels** (Hebrew Bible, Enochic literature, Talmud, Mesopotamian cosmogony, Greek astronomy) — read for pre-Islamic availability of the motifs.

Where disagreement exists in the classical tradition, the audit has noted the spread of opinion rather than forcing unanimity. Where the modern apologetic reading has a non-trivial textual hook (as with *ratq/fatq* via Mujāhid, or mountain-roots via *awtād*), the audit has flagged the hook rather than dismissing the whole reading.

The verdict scale (V1–V5) is designed to separate (a) texts that straightforwardly exclude the modern reading, from (b) texts that underdetermine it, from (c) texts whose compatibility with modern science is striking enough to warrant note. No verse in this cluster reaches V5. One reaches V4 qualified. The rest are V1–V3.
