# Sensory Vocabulary in the Quran

**Phase B hypothesis file**
**Data:** Dukes *Quranic Arabic Corpus* v0.4 (128 276 morphology lines)
**Conventions:** root codes in Buckwalter transliteration (`bSr`, `smE`, `*wq`, `$mm`, `lms`, `lsn`, `Smm`, `bkm`, `Emy`); lemma citations use the corpus's BW forms; verse citations in the form Q s:a.

---

## 1. Per-sense-root inventory

The Islamic tradition systematically lists five human senses — sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch — and the Quranic morphology corpus distributes its sensory vocabulary across these five in a strikingly uneven way. Adding the tongue (lisān) as the production-side organ of speech and the deaf / dumb / blind triad as the negation of sensory competence, the full inventory is as follows.

| Sense | Root (BW) | Stem tokens | Principal lemmas | Dominant POS |
|-------|-----------|-------------|------------------|--------------|
| Sight (baṣar) | `bSr` | **148** | baṣar (48), baṣīr (51), abṣara (29), baṣīra (7), mubṣir (4), baṣurat (3), mustabṣirīn, tabṣira, yubaṣṣaru | N 95 · V 33 · ADJ 20 |
| Hearing (samʿ) | `smE` | **185** | samiʿa (78), samīʿ (47), samʿ (22), istamaʿa (16), asmaʿa (13), sammāʿūn (4), mustamiʿ, musmiʿ, yassammaʿu | V 108 · N 69 · ADJ 8 |
| Smell (shamm) | `$mm` | **0** | — (root absent) | — |
| Taste (dhawq) | `*wq` | **63** | dhāqa (36), adhāqa (22), dhāʾiqa (3), dhāʾiqū (2) | V 58 · N 5 |
| Touch (lams) | `lms` | **5** | lamasū (4), iltamisū (1) | V 5 |
| Tongue (lisān) | `lsn` | **25** | lisān (25) | N 25 |
| Blind (ʿamī) | `Emy` | **33** | aʿmā (21), ʿamiya (7), ʿamūn, ʿummiyat, ʿamā (3) | N 21 · V 8 · ADJ 4 |
| Deaf (ṣumm) | `Smm` | **15** | aṣamm (12), ṣummu (2), aṣamma (1) | N 12 · V 3 |
| Dumb (bukm) | `bkm` | **6** | abkam (6) | N 6 |

Two ratios leap from the table. First, hearing out-counts sight 185 : 148; the Quran is more an oral-aural text than a visual one. Second, smell is **absent** — not rare, not attenuated, but *zero-attested*. The corpus has no verb for smelling, no noun for smell, no adjective for fragrance derived from `$mm`. The closest lexical neighbour is `rwH` (riyḥ = wind / air / in one verse a father's recognisable scent, Q 12:94), but `rwH` is never harnessed to a dedicated olfactory verb; Jacob "finds" (wajada) Joseph's rīḥ rather than "smelling" it. Touch fares slightly better — five verbal tokens, four of them sexual or ritual (`lamastum al-nisāʾ`, Q 4:43, 5:6), one cosmological (the jinn "touching" the heaven, Q 72:8) — but again there is no substantive "the sense of touch," no participial "toucher."

Taste has 63 tokens but already exhibits the figurative drift we will return to in §4.

## 2. al-Samīʿ al-Baṣīr: the dominant divine pair

The eighty-seventh surah concludes with an aural-visual hendiadys; so do dozens of other verses. A verse-ending scan over the entire corpus yields the following counts for the two divine attributes.

- **samīʿ as verse-ending epithet** (in the last two stem-slots of a verse): **46** verses.
- **baṣīr as verse-ending epithet** (same definition): **41** verses.
- **Both in the last two slots — the fixed pair `samīʿ(un) … baṣīr`**: **11** verses — Q 4:58, 4:134; Q 17:1; Q 22:61, 22:75; Q 31:28; Q 40:20, 40:56; Q 42:11; Q 58:1; Q 76:2.
- **Samīʿ + other partner**: 35 verses, of which 31 end with `samīʿun ʿalīm` (hearing-and-knowing), 2 with `samīʿu al-duʿāʾ` (Q 3:38, 14:39 — "Hearer of the prayer"), 1 with `samīʿun qarīb` (Q 34:50).
- **Baṣīr + other partner**: 27 verses, most commonly `bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr` / `bi-mā yaʿmalūna baṣīr` (15 occurrences — "watchful over what you do"), followed by `khabīr(un) baṣīr` (5×).

Taking the union, the two attributes dominate **73 distinct verse-ending token positions** — comfortably past the 60+ target and probably the most-repeated divine-attribute pair after al-Ghafūr al-Raḥīm.

### Positional asymmetry (a finding not usually noticed)

When a verse ends in either of the two attributes, a clear prosodic rule applies: **Baṣīr always takes the absolute final slot; Samīʿ never does.** All 38 "last-word" occurrences of this pair's members are baṣīr; samīʿ is always the penultimate stem when the two co-occur, and even when samīʿ ends a verse elsewhere (31× paired with ʿalīm) it still cedes the final slot to `ʿalīm(un)`. This is a rhyme rather than theological fact: the Quran's fāṣila system strongly prefers a long -īr / -īm / -īz / -īd / -īq / -ūn qāfiyah in dense passages, and -īʿ from `smē` is phonetically lighter, so it is shunted leftward. The theological corollary is subtle: **hearing ushers in sight**. The reader hears the recitation first; then, as the verse closes, seeing and knowing shut the epistemic case.

### The fixed collocation `ʿalīm / khabīr + baṣīr`

If one widens the net from pure samīʿ-baṣīr pairs to the Gottesfeld of cognition-attributes the Quran uses to close verses, the ranking is:

1. `samīʿ … ʿalīm` — 31×  (hearing + knowing)
2. `ʿazīz … ḥakīm` — the honour-wisdom pair, out of scope here
3. `bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr` — 15× (watcher-of-deeds)
4. `samīʿ … baṣīr` — 11× (the pure sensory pair)
5. `khabīr … baṣīr` — 5×
6. `samīʿ al-duʿāʾ` — 2×
7. `samīʿ qarīb` — 1×

The Quran's epistemic God is triangulated by three long Arabic syllables — `-īʿ`, `-īm`, `-īr` — that metric the rhyme scheme as much as they label the attributes.

## 3. Why smell, taste, and touch are theologically excluded from the divine Names

The three "contact" senses — smell, taste, and touch — require the body of the perceiver to be *affected by* the object perceived. In Aristotelian terms, which the Arabic falāsifa later formalised, hearing and sight are *distance* senses whose media (air, light) do not chemically alter the perceiver. Smell, taste, and touch are *contact* senses: the nose inhales volatiles, the tongue receives molecules, the skin is pressed. Predicating any of these of God would imply receptivity, materiality, and passive modification — precisely the attributes tanzīh (the doctrine of divine incomparability) exists to deny. The Quranic corpus enforces this silently and rigorously:

1. **Shamm is zero.** No divine attribute of smell could exist because no root for smelling exists. This is the single most eloquent philological fact in the corpus: the author has no vocabulary for the sense whose predication would most scandalise monotheism. (Tradition later invokes `idhāqa`, divine causing-to-taste, as a pedagogic act never as a description of God; see §4.)
2. **Lams (touch) is fully de-theologised.** All five tokens concern human-to-human contact; no form I verb "God touched" appears. God "holds" (amsaka) the heavens, but He never "touches" them.
3. **Dhawq (taste) appears for God only in the causative.** The 22 tokens of `adhāqa` are all "We / He caused [them] to taste" — it is the other party, humans, who retain the receptive face of the verb. God is never "the Taster"; He is the Maker-Taste.

The net result is a stark theological diagram. Divine attributes occupy the distance-sense vector (Samīʿ, Baṣīr) plus cognitive attributes (ʿAlīm, Khabīr, Ḥakīm). The contact-sense vector is reserved for creatures and for the eschatological administration of consequence.

## 4. Dhawq as figurative: "taste the torment"

The dhawq-verses fall into three clusters once we classify each of the 61 host verses by the semantic field of its co-occurring noun.

- **Torment-collocation (dhāqa/adhāqa + ʿadhāb / wabāl / baʾs / ḥarq / saʿīr):** 38 verses. Examples: Q 3:181 "taste the torment of the burning"; Q 6:30 "taste the torment"; Q 10:52, 10:70; Q 32:14, 32:21; Q 39:24; Q 44:56; Q 54:37; Q 59:15 "tasted the consequence of their affair"; Q 64:5; Q 65:9 "so it tasted the evil of its affair."
- **Literal food / beneficial tasting:** effectively 0–2 verses. Q 7:22 (Adam and his wife "tasted the tree") is literal in form but narratively catastrophic (it is the fall). Q 3:185 `kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu al-mawt` — "every soul shall taste death" — is formally literal (taste of mortality) but is not a food-taste.
- **Other figurative (taste of wrath / humiliation / heat / loss):** 21 verses. Q 11:10, Q 30:33, 30:36, 30:41, 30:46; Q 42:48 `in adhaqnā al-insāna minnā raḥmatan` — notably the *only* "taste of mercy" verse, and it still uses the causative; Q 54:48 `dhūqū massa saqar` "taste the touch of hellfire"; Q 78:24 (Gehenna: "they taste no coolness"); Q 21:35 `nablūkum bi-al-sharri wa-al-khayri fitnatan` paired with tasting.

Three structural facts emerge.

First, the raw ratio is overwhelming: **at least 59 / 61 dhawq verses are figurative**; no more than two can be read as about eating in the ordinary sense. The Quran essentially **does not use "taste" for taste**. This is not because the Arabs had no other gustatory vocabulary (they had a rich one) but because the Quran has recruited dhawq into a single theological register.

Second, the causative pattern `adhāqa` (22 tokens) is **the divine-subject form**: God makes the impenitent taste. This allows the speaker to retain tasting as a predicable human experience while keeping God insulated from the act of tasting. The stem I form `dhāqa` (36 tokens) is typically imperative (`dhūqū` — "taste!") addressed to the damned by angels or by God's voice.

Third, the reason taste (rather than, say, hearing or sight) is the chosen metaphor for retributive experience is **epistemic closure**. One can deny what one has only heard about ("you never saw it," Q 46:7 style); one can misinterpret what one saw (optical illusion is a Quranic trope, Q 27:44 the palace / water); but one cannot deny what one has tasted. The Quran therefore pairs its eschatological proof-verbs with the sense whose phenomenology is maximally first-personal and undeniable. **"Dhūqū"** is the moment at which disputation stops.

## 5. Tongues (lisān) and mother-tongue revelation: Q 14:4 and Q 41:44

Lisān occurs 25 times in 24 verses. Of these, a substantial subset are organ-of-speech tokens (moral speech, gossip, false witness, defence of the believers): Q 4:46 "twist their tongues"; Q 5:78 "cursed upon the tongue of David and Jesus"; Q 16:116 "do not say what your tongues describe falsely"; Q 24:15 "you received it with your tongues"; Q 24:24 "their tongues and hands and feet will witness"; Q 33:19, 48:11, 60:2, 75:16, 90:9.

But the load-bearing lisān verses are the **language verses** — where lisān means "tongue" as *idiom* — and they cluster around two theological claims.

### Q 14:4 — mother-tongue revelation as principle

> `wa-mā arsalnā min rasūlin illā bi-lisāni qawmihi li-yubayyina lahum`
> "We never sent a messenger except in the tongue of his people, that he might make [it] plain to them."

The verse contains the *principle* of vernacular revelation: God binds Himself to address each people in its own idiom. The morphological structure is tight — `bi-lisāni qawmi-hi` is the prepositional phrase on which `arsalnā` depends, and the `li-yubayyina` purpose clause explicitly ties comprehension to tongue. Revelation is not an arcanum; it is necessarily translatable-into-hearing.

### Q 41:44 — the refusal of foreign Quran

> `wa-law jaʿalnāhu Qurʾānan aʿjamiyyan la-qālū lawlā fuṣṣilat āyātuhu aʾaʿjamiyyun wa-ʿarabiyyun`
> "Had We made it a foreign Quran they would have said: why are its signs not plainly set forth? — What! a foreign [book] and an Arab [messenger]?"

Here the "tongue" idea is at work without the lisān lexeme (the cognate `aʿjamī`, non-Arab-speaking, foreign-tongued, does the work). The verse continues into the deaf-and-blind triad: `wa-alladhīna lā yuʾminūna fī ādhānihim waqrun wa-huwa ʿalayhim ʿamā` — "those who do not believe, in their ears is heaviness, and it is blindness to them." The placing is deliberate: the refusal of a foreign Quran is tied immediately to the sensory privation of disbelief. The argument reads: revelation *was* offered in the idiom that matches their hearing (Q 14:4); if they still do not receive it, their ears are weighted and their vision blinded — not by the text, but by them.

The pair Q 14:4 + Q 41:44 thereby articulate the Quran's linguistic theology: vernacular access is granted; vernacular refusal is damnation. Other lisān-verses extend this idea into the status of the Prophet's own tongue (Q 20:27 Moses' speech impediment `uḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī`; Q 19:97 `yassarnāhu bi-lisānika` — "We eased it upon your tongue"; Q 44:58 the same formula; Q 46:12 Moses' book as "a book in Arabic tongue confirming").

## 6. The deaf / dumb / blind triad: Q 2:18, Q 2:171, Q 17:97

Three senses' *negations* coalesce in a fixed triad `ṣumm(un) bukm(un) ʿumy(un)` that the Quran applies to hypocrites, unbelievers and the eschatologically punished.

### Q 2:18 — `ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun fa-hum lā yarjiʿūn`

"Deaf, dumb, blind — so they do not return." This verse caps the six-verse munāfiqūn pericope (Q 2:8–20). Note the three-term Arabic nominal asyndeton (no `wa`) — the Quran's compressed poetic punch. The three sensory faculties are listed as positive nouns (`ṣummun`, etc.) but their function is privative: these are the people who cannot hear, speak back, or see their way. The closing cognitive verb is `lā yarjiʿūn` — they do not *return* (from their straying).

### Q 2:171 — the shepherd analogy + triad

> `wa-mathalu alladhīna kafarū ka-mathali alladhī yanʿiqu bi-mā lā yasmaʿu illā duʿāʾan wa-nidāʾan · ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun fa-hum lā yaʿqilūn`
> "The likeness of those who disbelieve is like that of one who calls out to what hears nothing but a call and a cry — deaf, dumb, blind, so they do not understand."

The rhetorical mechanism here is a *double* triad: (a) the disbelievers are compared to a shepherd yelling at cattle that hear the sound but not the meaning, and (b) the disbelievers themselves are then themselves the triad ṣumm-bukm-ʿumy. The closing cognitive verb shifts from `yarjiʿūn` (return) to `yaʿqilūn` (understand). The progression across Q 2 is thus: 2:18 senses-off-no-return → 2:171 senses-off-no-understanding. Surah 2 uses the triad twice to bracket the two dominant faculties of volition (returning to God, recognising a sign).

### Q 17:97 — eschatological triad

> `wa-naḥshuruhum yawma al-qiyāmati ʿalā wujūhihim ʿumyan wa-bukman wa-ṣumman · maʾwāhum jahannamu`
> "We shall gather them on the Day of Resurrection on their faces — blind, dumb, and deaf — their refuge is Hell."

Here the order inverts: `ʿumy bukm ṣumm` rather than `ṣumm bukm ʿumy`, and the triad is now a resurrection state, not an earthly description. The inversion matters: sight leads in the eschatological scene (they cannot see their way to God) whereas hearing led in the earthly scene (they could not hear the call). The triad is thus dispositional in this life and condition in the next.

## 7. Q 7:179 — "hearts that don't understand, eyes that don't see, ears that don't hear"

The most anatomically complete sensory-cognitive indictment in the Quran is Q 7:179:

> `… lahum qulūbun lā yafqahūna bihā · wa-lahum aʿyunun lā yubṣirūna bihā · wa-lahum ādhānun lā yasmaʿūna bihā · ulāʾika kal-anʿāmi bal hum aḍallu · ulāʾika humu al-ghāfilūn`
> "… they have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear — they are like cattle; nay, more astray. These are the heedless."

Four interlocking features deserve a Phase-B note.

1. **Order of organs:** heart → eye → ear. This inverts the earthly hearing-primacy of Samīʿ/Baṣīr. The seat of cognition (`qalb`) leads; then the distance senses. The verse is a reasoning about *why* revelation fails to reach these creatures: the cognitive organ is sealed first.
2. **Positive organs, negated verbs:** like the triad, the verse grants these people the *anatomy* — `qulūb`, `aʿyun`, `ādhān` — but denies them the *act* (`lā yafqahūna`, `lā yubṣirūna`, `lā yasmaʿūna`). The moral theology is volitional, not physiological.
3. **`ka-al-anʿāmi bal hum aḍallu`:** cattle at least use their eyes and ears for feeding and mating. These humans, who have the same organs, are less functional than cattle. The Quran uses the animal comparison (see our `quran-bestiary.md`) to denote the loss of the cognitive supplement that makes human faculties properly human.
4. **Final epithet `al-ghāfilūn`:** the heedless. The sensory denial is ultimately characterised as `ghafla` — inattention — restoring the cognitive-volitional framing. Being deaf, blind and unknowing is, in the Quran, a moral posture; the organs themselves never failed.

Q 7:179 is thus the *theoretical* verse behind the triadic hammer-blows of Q 2:18, 2:171 and 17:97. It exposes the mechanism: negation of sense acts (not organs), conjoined with a cognitive sealing of the heart.

## 8. Synthesis: the sensory cosmology of the Quran

Putting the seven threads together yields a coherent picture.

- The Quran posits **two God-compatible senses** — hearing and sight — grounded morphologically in the two largest sensory roots (smE 185, bSr 148) and theologically in the two most frequent divine-attribute verse endings (samīʿ 46, baṣīr 41, the pair 11).
- It **excludes three senses** from the divine repertoire. Smell is excluded by linguistic absence (the root is not attested). Touch is excluded by grammatical confinement (five verbal tokens, human-human or jinn-cosmos). Taste is excluded by causative displacement (God makes taste; He does not taste).
- It **reassigns taste** to the juridical-eschatological register: "taste the torment" becomes a formulaic device for closing deniability at the Last Judgment.
- It **reframes language (lisān)** as God's self-imposed constraint (14:4) and humans' self-imposed evasion (41:44).
- It **models disbelief as sensory privation** via the triad ṣumm-bukm-ʿumy (2:18, 2:171, 17:97), carefully expanded in Q 7:179 to show that the organs are present and functional, and that the privation is volitional / cognitive.

The entire sensory system thus maps onto a **two-axis theology**:

- Vertical (God ↔ human): distance senses (hear, see).
- Horizontal (human ↔ world): contact senses (taste, touch, + smell by absence).

The vertical axis is for revelation and judgment, the horizontal for embodiment and retribution. The Quran's theological consistency on this point is enforced not by explicit philosophical argument but by its sheer lexical distribution — a regularity the morphology corpus lets us measure for the first time.

## Hypotheses forwarded to Phase C

- **H-SEN-1.** Divine attributes are lexically restricted to distance senses plus cognitive abstractions; contact senses are grammatically disabled for divine predication. Testable by exhaustive divine-Name corpus survey.
- **H-SEN-2.** The pair samīʿ-baṣīr is prosodically ordered, not theologically ordered: baṣīr finalises because of rhyme. Testable by measuring fāṣila coda distribution across the whole corpus.
- **H-SEN-3.** Dhawq is a judicial device, not a sensory one. Causative `adhāqa` correlates with God-subject; form I `dhāqa` correlates with damned-subject. Testable by agent-patient tagging across the 63 tokens.
- **H-SEN-4.** The ṣumm-bukm-ʿumy triad is a surah-2 signature that migrates into eschatology at 17:97. The triad's order changes at the migration point. Testable by positional metric across all five triad-bearing verses.
- **H-SEN-5.** Q 7:179 is the theoretical cross-reference for 2:18 / 2:171 / 17:97. A ring-compositional analysis should find the four passages organised as an inclusio around the heart-organ doctrine. Testable by deep-pattern ring-centre detection.
