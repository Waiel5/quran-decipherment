# World-history events in the Qurʾān — status against current scholarship

This file takes twenty-five events, peoples, places, and persons that the Qurʾān locates in world history, and reports the state of the historical–archaeological record for each as of current (2026) scholarship. Each entry cites the Arabic of the most load-bearing verse, summarises the material evidence, and ends with a graded verdict.

The grading is descriptive, not polemical. A "CONFIRMED" does not make the Qurʾān "right" in a theological sense; a "UNRESOLVED" does not make it "wrong." It reports how the Qurʾānic claim sits against what spades, inscriptions, and cross-cultural textual witnesses have recovered. Several entries are genuinely in motion — archaeology of the Arabian peninsula has moved faster in the last thirty years than in the previous two centuries combined, and south-Arabian epigraphy in particular has reshaped the ground.

## 1. ʿĀd and Iram dhāt al-ʿimād (Q 89:6–8)

**Arabic.** أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِعَادٍ ۝ إِرَمَ ذَاتِ الْعِمَادِ ۝ الَّتِي لَمْ يُخْلَقْ مِثْلُهَا فِي الْبِلَادِ
"Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with ʿĀd — Iram, of the lofty pillars, the like of which had not been built in any land?"

**Status.** The ʿĀd are otherwise attested only in Arabian oral tradition and pre-Islamic poetry; they have no pre-Islamic epigraphic footprint under that exact name. "Iram" (ʾrm) does appear in a South Arabian context: a Sabaic inscription found at ʿUbar/Shiṣr (Jabal al-Qāra, Dhofar, Oman) mentions ʾrm and related toponyms, and the 1992 Clapp/Zarins expedition identified a caravan entrepôt at Shiṣr that collapsed into a sinkhole — which team leader Nicholas Clapp identified with Qurʾānic Iram/ʿĀd. That identification is disputed; most specialists (Jan Retsö, Kenneth Kitchen) regard Shiṣr as a later frankincense-caravan stop, not the lost city of ʿĀd. The Safaitic and Thamudic inscription corpora do not preserve an ethnonym "ʿĀd" either. Classical tafsīr placed ʿĀd in al-Aḥqāf (south-east Arabia), matching the general Dhofar/Ḥaḍramawt frankincense zone.

**Verdict: CONTESTED.** A real Arabian cultural memory underlies the name — echoed in pre-Islamic poetry independent of the Qurʾān — but no single site or inscription has yet yielded a clean "ʿĀd = X" identification that the field accepts.

## 2. Thamūd and al-Ḥijr (Q 15:80; 7:73–79; 11:61–68; 26:141–158)

**Arabic.** وَلَقَدْ كَذَّبَ أَصْحَابُ الْحِجْرِ الْمُرْسَلِينَ (Q 15:80) — "The people of al-Ḥijr rejected the messengers."

**Status.** Thamūd is among the most archaeologically secure pre-Islamic North Arabian polities. The ethnonym is attested in Neo-Assyrian annals: Sargon II c. 715 BCE lists "Tamudi" among Arab tribes he defeated. It appears again in Greco-Roman sources (Pliny, Ptolemy) as the Thamudēnoi, and the Roman Notitia Dignitatum records an "Equites Saraceni Thamudeni" auxiliary unit. North Arabian rock-inscription corpora (Thamudic A–E, Ḥismaic, Dadanitic) preserve thousands of inscriptions attributable to these groups. Al-Ḥijr — modern Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ / Ḥegra in north-western Saudi Arabia — is a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2008; ninety-four Nabataean monumental tombs cut into sandstone outcrops, dated 1 BCE–75 CE, document it as the Nabataean south-eastern frontier. The Qurʾānic description of them carving houses from the mountains (naḥt al-jibāl — Q 26:149, 7:74) matches the rock-cut architecture visible today.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Thamūd's historicity, al-Ḥijr's location, and the rock-cut architectural practice are all independently attested. The Qurʾān's geographical detail is accurate; the theological framing (divine destruction narrative) is beyond archaeological adjudication.

## 3. Madyan and Shuʿayb (Q 7:85–93; 11:84–95; 29:36–37)

**Arabic.** وَإِلَىٰ مَدْيَنَ أَخَاهُمْ شُعَيْبًا (Q 7:85) — "And to Madyan, their brother Shuʿayb."

**Status.** Madyan = Midian of the Hebrew Bible (Genesis 25, Exodus 2–3, Numbers 22–25). The geographical zone — north-west Arabia, along the Gulf of Aqaba, centred on modern Al-Bidʿ / Maghāʾir Shuʿayb in Tabūk province, Saudi Arabia — has yielded Iron Age (13th–11th c. BCE) "Qurayyah painted ware" sites (Qurayyah, Tayma) associated in scholarship (Peter Parr, Juan Tebes) with Midianite material culture. The prophet Shuʿayb has no extra-Qurʾānic attestation by name; classical identification with Jethro (Yitro, Moses's father-in-law — a Midianite priest, Exodus 3:1) is tafsīr conjecture. The Qurʾān's depiction of Madyan as a trading, measure-cheating polity on a caravan route (Q 11:84–85, 26:181–183) fits what we know of Midianite incense-route involvement.

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** Madyan as a people and region is well-attested archaeologically; Shuʿayb as a specific historical figure is not independently documented.

## 4. Sabaʾ / Sheba and the bursting of the ʿArim dam (Q 27:22–44; 34:15–19)

**Arabic.** فَأَعْرَضُوا فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ (Q 34:16) — "But they turned away, so We sent upon them the flood of the ʿarim."

**Status.** The Sabaean kingdom of south Arabia is one of the best-documented pre-Islamic polities, with thousands of Sabaic inscriptions and continuous occupation from roughly the 9th century BCE to the 6th CE. The Great Dam of Marʾib (Maʾrib) is archaeologically confirmed: a sluice-and-earthen construction first attested epigraphically under the mukarribs c. 8th–7th c. BCE, with multiple recorded repair campaigns — notably under Sharaḥbiʾīl Yaʿfur (c. 450 CE, RES 4069) and the famous Abraha inscription (CIH 541, 548 CE) recording a repair after a major breach. The dam's final catastrophic failure is dated by inscriptions and Arab tradition to the later 6th century CE, probably c. 570–590, after which much of the population of the Marʾib oasis dispersed — matching the Qurʾān's motif of scattered tribes (Q 34:19: "We made them tales and scattered them utterly"). The Qurʾān's Queen-of-Sheba narrative (Q 27) parallels 1 Kings 10 / 2 Chronicles 9 but adds distinctive motifs (the hoopoe, the glass floor, sun-worship).

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** for the kingdom, the dam, the repeated breaches, and the late-antique collapse. The Queen-of-Sheba episode is TRADITION-ONLY — literary parallels exist but the biblical–Qurʾānic queen lacks independent epigraphic attestation by name.

## 5. Pharaoh of the Exodus (Mosaic narrative, Q 7, 10, 20, 26, 28, 40, 43, 79)

**Arabic.** فَالْيَوْمَ نُنَجِّيكَ بِبَدَنِكَ لِتَكُونَ لِمَنْ خَلْفَكَ آيَةً (Q 10:92) — "Today We will save you in your body, that you may be a sign to those after you."

**Status.** Current Egyptological consensus: there is no direct archaeological evidence for a large-scale Israelite exodus from Egypt. The earliest extra-biblical reference to Israel is the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE, reign of Merneptah son of Ramesses II), which mentions Israel as a people-group in Canaan. The two main Pharaoh candidates in the older scholarship are Ramesses II (traditional) and Merneptah (early-date model); both are problematic. Maurice Bucaille's argument (1987) that Merneptah's mummy shows drowning trauma and therefore matches Q 10:92 is rejected by Egyptologists: the mummy's traumatic damage is consistent with post-mortem handling and later robbery, not a pre-mortem event. The Qurʾān's distinctive detail — preservation of the pharaoh's body as a sign — has no specific archaeological confirmation beyond the general fact that royal Egyptian bodies were mummified.

**Verdict: CONTESTED / TRADITION-ONLY.** The Mosaic narrative is attested in Hebrew Bible, Samaritan, and Jewish/Christian late-antique traditions well before the Qurʾān; the underlying historical Exodus lacks independent archaeological support at the scale Scripture describes.

## 6. Solomon's kingdom (Q 27:15–44; 34:12–14; 38:30–40)

**Arabic.** وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا دَاوُودَ وَسُلَيْمَانَ عِلْمًا (Q 27:15) — "We bestowed knowledge on David and Solomon."

**Status.** The archaeological status of a 10th-century BCE united monarchy under Solomon is one of the field's most active debates. The "low-chronology" school (Finkelstein) reads much of the monumental architecture traditionally attributed to Solomon (Megiddo VA–IVB, Hazor X, Gezer) as Omride (9th c. BCE) instead. The "conventional" school (Mazar, Garfinkel) defends a substantial 10th-c. polity. Khirbet Qeiyafa (excavated 2007–2013) has shifted the debate toward accepting a centralised Judahite polity in the 10th century. There is no contemporaneous inscription naming Solomon. The Qurʾānic Solomon is theologically richer than the biblical one — command over winds, jinn, the speech of birds (Q 27:16), the brass-flowing "spring" (Q 34:12) — and the narrative arc parallels later Jewish haggadic expansions (Targum Sheni to Esther, Testament of Solomon).

**Verdict: PARTIAL** — a 10th-c. Israelite monarchy is broadly defensible; Solomon as a named historical figure lacks direct epigraphic confirmation.

## 7. David's kingdom (Q 38:17–26; 2:251; 4:163; 17:55; 21:78–80)

**Arabic.** وَقَتَلَ دَاوُودُ جَالُوتَ (Q 2:251) — "David slew Goliath."

**Status.** The Tel Dan Stele (discovered 1993, dated c. mid-9th century BCE) preserves the Aramaic phrase bytdwd — "House of David" — in an Aramaean king's boast (widely identified with Hazael). This is the earliest extra-biblical reference to the Davidic dynasty and ended the "mythicist" position that David was entirely legendary. The Mesha Stele (Moabite, c. 840 BCE) contains a plausible second reference (bt[d]wd), reconstruction debated. The Qurʾānic David is a prophet-king, ironworker (Q 34:10–11 — "We made iron supple for him"), psalmist (Zabūr, Q 4:163, 17:55). The biblical Goliath story (1 Samuel 17) lacks direct archaeological anchor, though Philistine material culture at Tell eṣ-Ṣāfī / Gath is well-attested.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** for the existence of a Davidic dynasty; PARTIAL for biographical details shared with the Hebrew Bible.

## 8. Roman–Persian war (Q 30:1–6)

**Arabic.** الم ۝ غُلِبَتِ الرُّومُ ۝ فِي أَدْنَى الْأَرْضِ وَهُم مِّن بَعْدِ غَلَبِهِمْ سَيَغْلِبُونَ ۝ فِي بِضْعِ سِنِينَ
"Alif lām mīm. The Romans have been defeated in the nearer land, and they, after their defeat, shall be victorious in a few years."

**Status.** The last great Byzantine–Sasanian war (602–628 CE) is among the best-documented conflicts in late antiquity. Persian forces under Khusrau II captured Antioch (611), Damascus (613), Jerusalem (May 614, with the loss of the True Cross), and Alexandria (619). Heraclius counterattacked from 622, decisively defeated the Persian army at the Battle of Nineveh (December 627), and compelled the return of the True Cross in 630. Q 30 was revealed in the Meccan period (c. 615–620), after the initial Persian victories and before the Byzantine reversal. "In the nearer land" (adnā al-arḍ) is standardly read as referring to Palestine/Syria (geographically "lower" or closest to the Hijaz) or as a wordplay on the Ghor / Jordan rift valley — the lowest land on earth, directly relevant to the 614 Jerusalem capture.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Dates, sequence, and outcome are independently documented in Byzantine (Theophanes, Chronicon Paschale), Armenian (Sebeos), and Syriac chronicle traditions.

## 9. Abraha's elephants (Q 105)

**Arabic.** أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْفِيلِ
"Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the companions of the elephant?"

**Status.** Abraha was the Aksumite-installed / Aksumite-descended ruler of Ḥimyar from the 530s to c. 565–570 CE, independently attested by his Marʾib dam inscription (CIH 541, 548 CE), the Murayghān inscription (Ry 506, 552 CE) recording a northern campaign against Maʿadd, and Byzantine/Syriac sources (Procopius, Book of the Ḥimyarites). A northern expedition by Abraha is thus epigraphically documented; whether one of those campaigns targeted Mecca specifically is debated — the Murayghān inscription reaches northern Najd but does not name Mecca. The traditional date of the "Year of the Elephant" (c. 570 CE, year of the Prophet's birth) sits at the far end of Abraha's reign and may need adjusting — some scholars (Kister, Conrad) argue for an earlier date c. 552.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** for Abraha and his northern Arabian campaigns; PARTIAL for a specific Meccan expedition; CONTESTED for the conventional 570 CE dating.

## 10. Noah's flood (Q 11:25–49; 71:1–28)

**Arabic.** وَقِيلَ يَا أَرْضُ ابْلَعِي مَاءَكِ وَيَا سَمَاءُ أَقْلِعِي (Q 11:44) — "It was said: O earth, swallow your water; and O sky, withhold."

**Status.** A global flood in the biblical/Qurʾānic sense has no geological support. The Mesopotamian flood-story tradition is deep: the Sumerian King List, the Sumerian Flood Story (Eridu Genesis), Atrahasis (Old Babylonian), and Gilgamesh Tablet XI all preserve the same narrative core — divine decision, chosen survivor, ark, mountain landing, bird sent to test waters. Regional flood layers at Ur (Woolley 1929), Shuruppak, and Kish attest real river catastrophes in the Early Dynastic period and are the probable nucleus of the literary tradition. The Qurʾān's narrative sits within this Near Eastern tradition — albeit with distinctive features (Noah's son refusing the ark and drowning, Q 11:42–43; flood limited in some readings to Noah's people rather than universal).

**Verdict: TRADITION-ONLY.** The literary tradition is fully attested; the event as described is not archaeologically recoverable.

## 11. Abraham (Q 2, 6, 14, 19, 21, 26, 29, 37, and more)

**Arabic.** وَإِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا بَلَدًا آمِنًا (Q 2:126) — "When Abraham said: My Lord, make this a secure city."

**Status.** No direct archaeological evidence for Abraham as a historical individual. Genesis 11–25 places him in the Middle Bronze Age II (c. 2000–1800 BCE), departing from Ur of the Chaldeans and travelling through Ḥarrān to Canaan. Ur is excavated (Woolley) and the Middle Bronze material culture is well-understood. The "patriarchal narratives" project (Albright, Speiser) attempted to anchor Abraham in Middle Bronze nomadic culture; most specialists since Van Seters (1975) and Thompson (1974) treat the patriarchal narratives as Iron Age literary compositions, not Middle Bronze memoirs. The Qurʾānic Abraham (ḥanīf, proto-monotheist, builder of the Kaʿba with Ismāʿīl, debater of his idolatrous father) overlaps Jewish haggadic expansions (Book of Jubilees, Apocalypse of Abraham, Midrash Genesis Rabbah) on the break-with-idolatry story.

**Verdict: TRADITION-ONLY.** Literary attestation ancient and continuous; independent archaeological anchoring absent.

## 12. Jesus (Q 3, 4, 5, 19, 43, 61)

**Arabic.** إِنَّمَا الْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ (Q 4:171) — "The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, is the messenger of God."

**Status.** The historicity of Jesus of Nazareth as a 1st-century CE Galilean preacher executed under Pontius Pilate is near-unanimous consensus across secular, Jewish, and Christian scholarship. Earliest non-Christian attestations: Josephus, Antiquities 18.63–64 (Testimonium Flavianum, disputed partial interpolation) and 20.200 (James "brother of the so-called Christ"); Tacitus, Annals 15.44 (c. 116 CE, referring to "Chrestus" executed by Pilate); Suetonius, Life of Claudius 25 (c. 120 CE). The Qurʾān's Jesus narrative diverges from both orthodox Christianity and the canonical Gospels on specific points: the virgin birth is affirmed (Q 3:47, 19:20), but the crucifixion is denied (Q 4:157 — wa-mā qatalūhu wa-mā ṣalabūhu wa-lākin shubbiha lahum) in a way that aligns with some Gnostic Christologies (Basilides in Irenaeus, Adv. Haer. 1.24.4) and Docetic currents. The infancy narrative (clay birds, Q 3:49; the speaking cradle, Q 19:29–33) parallels the Infancy Gospel of Thomas and the Syriac Arabic Infancy Gospel.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** for historical Jesus; specific Qurʾānic details are TRADITION-ONLY (paralleling late-antique apocryphal streams).

## 13. Mary (Q 3, 19)

**Arabic.** وَمَرْيَمَ ابْنَتَ عِمْرَانَ الَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا (Q 66:12) — "And Mary the daughter of ʿImrān, who guarded her chastity."

**Status.** Mary mother of Jesus is attested in the canonical Gospels (Matthew 1–2, Luke 1–2, John 2, 19), the Protoevangelium of James (mid-2nd c. CE), and Syriac-Christian Marian liturgy. The Qurʾān's Mary narrative is more detailed than any canonical source and closely parallels the Protoevangelium — her mother's vow (Q 3:35 / Proto-James 4), her service in the Temple (Q 3:37 / Proto-James 7–8), her being cared for by Zechariah (Q 3:37 / Proto-James 8). She is called bint ʿImrān (daughter of ʿImrān / Amram, the father of Moses in Exodus 6:20), which classical tafsīr resolves as a typological or clan-name usage, and which modern critics have read as a conflation with Miriam, Moses's sister (Numbers 26:59). Mary is the only woman named in the Qurʾān (thirty-four attestations) and has an entire sūra (19) bearing her name.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** for historicity; the specific narrative details are TRADITION-ONLY, paralleling Syriac-Christian apocryphal streams.

## 14. Dhū al-Qarnayn (Q 18:83–98)

**Arabic.** وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَن ذِي الْقَرْنَيْنِ قُلْ سَأَتْلُو عَلَيْكُم مِّنْهُ ذِكْرًا (Q 18:83)
"They ask you about Dhū al-Qarnayn. Say: I shall recite to you a remembrance of him."

**Status.** Most disputed identification in the Qurʾān. Four main candidates:
(a) Alexander the Great, via the Syriac Neṣḥānā / Alexander Legend (c. 629–630 CE according to the Reinink dating; some argue earlier). Kevin van Bladel's 2008 article "The Alexander Legend in the Qurʾān 18:83–102" argues for direct literary dependence — matching motifs include the two horns, the setting-sun spring, the wall against Gog and Magog, and specific vocabulary. The chronological fit (Neṣḥānā c. 629 vs. Q 18 Meccan) is tight and debated; some push the Syriac text earlier, others push Q 18 later.
(b) Cyrus the Great, advanced by modern scholars such as Maulana Azad on the grounds that Cyrus is called "Messiah" in Isaiah 45:1 and appears as a horned figure in Daniel 8 where the ram's two horns = "kings of Media and Persia" — and he is a monotheist conqueror from east to west.
(c) A South Arabian Ḥimyarite king (e.g., al-Ṣaʿb Dhū Marāthid), an identification preserved in early Islamic historiography (Wahb ibn Munabbih).
(d) A composite figure drawing on the Alexander tradition as mediated through Jewish-Syriac-Ethiopic channels.

The "two horns" (qarnayn) epithet is attested in Syriac, Ethiopic, and Jewish depictions of Alexander; on Alexander's Ptolemaic coinage he wears the ram's-horn diadem of Ammon.

**Verdict: UNRESOLVED.** The Alexander-legend hypothesis has the strongest literary evidence; the chronology remains contested.

## 15. Gog and Magog / Yaʾjūj wa-Maʾjūj (Q 18:94; 21:96)

**Arabic.** قَالُوا يَا ذَا الْقَرْنَيْنِ إِنَّ يَأْجُوجَ وَمَأْجُوجَ مُفْسِدُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ (Q 18:94)

**Status.** Gog and Magog enter the biblical tradition in Ezekiel 38–39 (Gog of the land of Magog, prince of Meshech and Tubal) and reappear in Revelation 20. Josephus (Antiquities 1.123) identifies Magog with the Scythians. The Syriac Alexander Legend identifies them as Hunnic / Central-Asian peoples locked behind an iron gate in the Caucasus. Classical Islamic geography (Ibn Khurradādhbih) inherited this identification. The ethnonyms map onto real Central-Asian / Eurasian steppe peoples known by different names across centuries (Scythians, Huns, Khazars, Mongols — the identification shifts).

**Verdict: TRADITION-ONLY.** The names belong to a long literary-apocalyptic tradition; no specific modern ethnic identification is secure.

## 16. Dhū al-Qarnayn's wall (Q 18:93–97)

**Arabic.** آتُونِي زُبَرَ الْحَدِيدِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ (Q 18:96)
"Bring me sheets of iron — until, when he levelled it between the two cliffs."

**Status.** Classical Arab geographers identified the wall with (a) the Caspian Gates / Darband (Derbent) in Dagestan — a massive Sasanian-built fortification system 7th century and earlier, with iron-gated passes across the north-eastern Caucasus chokepoint; (b) the Darial Pass in central Caucasus; or (c) a remote Central-Asian structure. Al-Muqtadir's 9th-c. emissary Sallām al-Tarjumān claimed to have visited the wall (Ibn Khurradādhbih 162–170) and described an iron-and-brass structure with a gate. Some modern writers have proposed the Great Wall of Gorgān (Sasanian, 5th c. CE, ~200 km across north-eastern Iran, brick-lined and garrisoned). None of the candidate sites (Darband, Darial, Gorgān, nor the Chinese Great Wall) corresponds precisely to the Qurʾānic description of a fortification built of iron blocks levelled between two mountain faces and then coated in molten copper (qiṭran).

**Verdict: UNRESOLVED.** Iron-reinforced Caucasian and Caspian fortifications existed; none is a close architectural fit. The description may be literary rather than descriptive.

## 17. Banū Isrāʾīl covenant history (Q 2, 5, 7)

**Arabic.** وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَكُمْ وَرَفَعْنَا فَوْقَكُمُ الطُّورَ (Q 2:63) — "When We took your covenant and raised the Mount above you."

**Status.** The Israelite covenant narrative is attested in the Hebrew Bible (Exodus, Deuteronomy, Joshua) and in the Samaritan Pentateuch; the Dead Sea Scrolls (250 BCE–70 CE) confirm the textual antiquity of these traditions. Archaeologically, the emergence of "Israel" as a distinct highland population in the late 13th–12th c. BCE is supported by survey data (Finkelstein) and the Merneptah Stele mention. The specific covenant episodes (Golden Calf, quail and manna, striking the rock, the twelve springs) are TRADITION-ONLY — literary, not archaeological. The Qurʾān's retelling sometimes follows rabbinic midrash (e.g., the mountain lifted above the Israelites, Q 2:63, parallels b. Shabbat 88a — "the Holy One overturned the mountain upon them like a cask").

**Verdict: PARTIAL / TRADITION-ONLY.** The people and their emergence as a highland polity are attested; specific covenant episodes are literary-traditional.

## 18. Joseph in Egypt (Q 12)

**Arabic.** وَقَالَ الَّذِي اشْتَرَاهُ مِن مِّصْرَ لِامْرَأَتِهِ أَكْرِمِي مَثْوَاهُ (Q 12:21)
"The man from Egypt who bought him said to his wife: treat him honourably."

**Status.** No extra-biblical attestation of the Joseph narrative itself. The broader historical context — Semitic / West-Asian ("Asiatic") officials in Egyptian administration — is well-documented for the Second Intermediate Period (c. 1650–1550 BCE) and into the New Kingdom. Hyksos rulers (15th Dynasty), the Semitic settlement at Tell el-Dabʿa / Avaris (Bietak), and Semitic titulature in Egyptian bureaucracy provide a plausible backdrop. The Qurʾānic titulature is notable: "al-Malik" (the king) rather than "Firʿawn" in Q 12 — arguably a historically accurate distinction, because "Pharaoh" (prʿʿ, "great house") is used as a personal royal title only from the late New Kingdom (c. 1200 BCE) onward, and would be anachronistic for a Middle-Bronze or Second-Intermediate-Period Egyptian monarch. "al-ʿAzīz" (the mighty one) may translate an Egyptian high-official title. This linguistic precision has been noted (Maurice Bucaille, and more cautiously by non-apologetic Egyptologists).

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** The broader cultural milieu is archaeologically plausible; the specific narrative is TRADITION-ONLY; the titulature distinction (malik vs firʿawn) is a notable Qurʾānic precision.

---

## 19. People of the Cave (Q 18:9–26)

**Arabic.** أَمْ حَسِبْتَ أَنَّ أَصْحَابَ الْكَهْفِ وَالرَّقِيمِ كَانُوا مِنْ آيَاتِنَا عَجَبًا (Q 18:9)
"Or did you reckon the people of the cave and al-Raqīm were a wonder of Our signs?"

**Status.** The story of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus is a Syriac-Christian hagiographical tradition of seven young Christian men who hid in a cave during the Decian persecution (c. 250 CE) and awoke roughly two centuries later. The earliest extant witness is Jacob of Sarug's Syriac homily (c. 521 CE). Gregory of Tours transmitted the story westward in the 6th c.; the cave site at Ephesus (modern Selçuk, Turkey) has a late-antique church and catacomb complex identified with the tradition since at least the 6th century. The Qurʾān's version names no place but gives a numeric puzzle ("they will say three — four — five — seven… God knows their number," Q 18:22) and a sleep-duration ("three hundred years, add nine more," Q 18:25, reconciling solar and lunar calendars: 300 solar ≈ 309 lunar). The Arabic al-Raqīm is obscure — variously read as "inscription," "tablet," a toponym, or a proper noun.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** as a pre-Islamic late-antique Christian tradition; the Qurʾānic retelling is TRADITION-ONLY but notable for its numeric hedging.

## 20. Hāmān (Q 28:6–8, 38; 29:39; 40:24, 36–37)

**Arabic.** وَقَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ يَا هَامَانُ ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا (Q 40:36)
"Pharaoh said: Hāmān, build me a tower."

**Status.** Long a stock polemical target: critics from Abraham Geiger (1833) onward have alleged that the Qurʾān has confusedly imported the biblical Haman (an official of the Persian king Ahasuerus / Xerxes in the book of Esther, 5th c. BCE) into Pharaoh's court (13th c. BCE). Maurice Bucaille proposed that hmn reflects an Egyptian religious title — ḥm-nṯr (priest of a god, "servant of the god") — citing a hieroglyphic attestation in Ranke's Personennamen. Most Egyptologists reject the identification: ḥm-nṯr is a common priestly title, not a personal name, and the Qurʾānic Hāmān is clearly a named individual holding political office, building a tower so Pharaoh can "look up" at Moses's God (Q 40:36–37). The Esther-conflation thesis remains the mainstream critical reading; the Bucaille defense is contested.

**Verdict: CONTESTED.** The Esther-conflation reading has majority scholarly support; the Bucaille defense has serious methodological problems but has not been conclusively refuted for all readers.

---

## 21. Sodom and Gomorrah / people of Lot (Q 11:74–83; 15:58–77; 26:160–175)

**Arabic.** فَلَمَّا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا جَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهَا حِجَارَةً (Q 11:82)
"When Our command came, We turned it upside down and rained upon it stones."

**Status.** Tall el-Ḥammām (Jordan Valley, north-east of the Dead Sea) has been excavated since 2005 by Steven Collins under the hypothesis of identification with Sodom. A 2021 paper (Bunch et al. in Scientific Reports) proposed a Middle Bronze Age airburst / cosmic impact destruction event at the site c. 1650 BCE. That paper has been heavily critiqued — subsequent analyses (2022–2023, including by Mark Boslough, specialists in impact cratering) have challenged the shocked-quartz identification and much of the airburst claim. The site shows a genuine Middle Bronze destruction layer; whether from an impact event, earthquake, or military action is contested. Biblical Sodom's location has long been disputed (southern Dead Sea vs. northern Dead Sea vs. entirely legendary). The Qurʾānic description of the city "turned upside down" and stones raining from above is consistent with seismic + tar-seep fire catastrophes known in the Dead Sea tectonic zone.

**Verdict: CONTESTED.** A real destruction event in the Middle Bronze lower Jordan Valley is plausible; identification with a specific Sodom and the airburst mechanism are heavily disputed.

---

## 22. The Ark's resting place al-Jūdī (Q 11:44)

**Arabic.** وَاسْتَوَتْ عَلَى الْجُودِيِّ (Q 11:44) — "And it came to rest upon al-Jūdī."

**Status.** The Qurʾān names al-Jūdī (not Ararat) as the resting point. Jūdī is identified with Cudi Dağı in Şırnak province, south-eastern Turkey, overlooking the Tigris near the Syrian/Iraqi border. This identification is not a late Islamic innovation: the Peshitta (Syriac Old Testament) renders Genesis 8:4 not as "Ararat" but as qardu, and the Targum Onqelos on the same verse gives qardu, matching the region of Corduene / Qardū — the same area as Cudi Dağı. Berossus (3rd-c. BCE Babylonian historian, preserved in Josephus) places the Ark in Corduene (Kurdistan), not on Mount Ararat proper (Ağrı Dağı in far north-east Turkey, which is the later Armenian Christian identification). Josephus (Antiquities 1.93), the Aramaic Targum, Nestorian tradition, and the Qurʾān all converge on the Cudi/Qardū location — the modern Mount Ararat identification is a later Armenian/Western tradition.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** as aligning with the older Near Eastern (Syriac-Jewish-Babylonian) textual tradition; the event itself remains TRADITION-ONLY.

---

## 23. Night Journey — al-Masjid al-Aqṣā (Q 17:1)

**Arabic.** سُبْحَانَ الَّذِي أَسْرَىٰ بِعَبْدِهِ لَيْلًا مِّنَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ إِلَى الْمَسْجِدِ الْأَقْصَى
"Glory to the One who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the furthest mosque."

**Status.** The standard Islamic identification of al-Masjid al-Aqṣā with the Temple Mount compound in Jerusalem is supported by (a) earliest Islamic interpretive tradition, (b) the Umayyad construction of the Dome of the Rock (691/692 under ʿAbd al-Malik) and the al-Aqṣā Mosque proper on the Temple Mount platform, and (c) continuous usage of the name. Revisionist positions (Shoemaker; some Orientalists) have argued that al-Masjid al-Aqṣā originally referred to a site in the Hijaz or to a heavenly sanctuary, not historical Jerusalem — based on reading the Q 17:1 "further mosque" against the background of 7th-c. usage and geography. Archaeologically, the Temple Mount / Herodian platform was a standing, partially ruined sacred precinct in the 7th century after Hadrian's and Titus's demolitions; a Jewish-Christian memorial tradition for the site is continuous through late antiquity.

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** in the mainstream reading; CONTESTED in the revisionist reading.

---

## 24. The Year of the Elephant (Q 105 dating)

**Arabic.** The surah does not itself give a date; traditional Islamic chronology aligns it with the Prophet's birth year (c. 570 CE or 571 CE).

**Status.** See item 9 above. Abraha's northern Arabian campaigns are epigraphically attested (Murayghān inscription, 552 CE). Some scholars (Kister 1965, Conrad 1987) argue the "Year of the Elephant" should be pushed back to c. 552, which would require recalibrating the traditional date of the Prophet's birth. The majority traditional position (c. 570) is not falsifiable against the extant epigraphic record but is no longer the unanimous specialist position.

**Verdict: CONTESTED** on dating; CONFIRMED on the existence of Abraha and his campaigning.

---

## 25. The Kaʿba and Abrahamic Mecca (Q 2:125–127; 3:96–97)

**Arabic.** إِنَّ أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ وُضِعَ لِلنَّاسِ لَلَّذِي بِبَكَّةَ مُبَارَكًا (Q 3:96)
"The first house established for humankind is the one at Bakka, blessed."

**Status.** Mecca's centrality as a pre-Islamic Arabian cult site is attested (a) in classical Arabic poetry and tribal genealogy, (b) in the 7th-c. Islamic sources documenting the Qurayshite mercantile economy, and (c) in the broader pattern of pre-Islamic Arabian pilgrimage / ḥaram sanctuary sites (Najrān, the ḥaram of al-Yaʿmurīya, the cluster of ḥaram sites in the Hijaz). The revisionist case (Patricia Crone, Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam, 1987) challenged the traditional picture of Mecca as a major trading city; her evidence was largely from classical sources lacking Mecca by name. Rehabilitation: later specialists (Gerald Hawting, Harry Munt, more recently Ahmad Al-Jallad's epigraphic work) have partially restored Mecca as a real late-antique Arabian polity, though the economic scale remains debated. Direct archaeology within Mecca is effectively nonexistent due to modern construction. The Abraham-Ismāʿīl foundation of the Kaʿba (Q 2:125–127) is TRADITION-ONLY — no independent attestation of a Bronze-Age Abrahamic construction. The pre-Islamic existence of the Kaʿba as an Arabian sanctuary is not reasonably doubted.

**Verdict: PARTIAL.** Pre-Islamic Kaʿba as an Arabian cult site: CONFIRMED. Abrahamic foundation narrative: TRADITION-ONLY.

# Synoptic tally

Of the twenty-five items examined:

- **CONFIRMED** (archaeologically/historically secure against the Qurʾānic claim): Thamūd/al-Ḥijr (2), Sabaʾ & the dam (4), Roman–Persian war (8), Historical Jesus (12), Mary (13), Seven Sleepers tradition (19), Jūdī / Qardū (22), Night Journey — al-Aqṣā in mainstream reading (23), Kaʿba as pre-Islamic sanctuary (25b).
- **PARTIAL** (core actor/event attested; Qurʾānic specifics go beyond the record): Madyan / Shuʿayb (3), Solomon (6), David (7), Abraha's specific Meccan expedition (9), Banū Isrāʾīl covenant history (17), Joseph's Egyptian setting (18), Abrahamic-foundation Kaʿba narrative (25a).
- **CONTESTED** (scholarly opinion divided; evidence ambiguous or candidates plural): ʿĀd / Iram (1), Pharaoh of the Exodus (5), Hāmān identification (20), Sodom / Tall el-Ḥammām (21), Year-of-the-Elephant dating (24).
- **TRADITION-ONLY** (attested in pre-Islamic literary tradition; no independent archaeology): Noah's flood (10), Abraham (11), Gog & Magog (15).
- **UNRESOLVED**: Dhū al-Qarnayn identification (14), Dhū al-Qarnayn's wall (16).

The overall pattern: where the Qurʾān speaks of events inside the late-antique world its speakers and audience inhabited — Arabia, the eastern Roman and Sasanian empires, northern Mesopotamia, the Levant — it correlates well with independent evidence. Where it speaks of events deep in the Bronze Age or the apocalyptic margins of the world (Noah, Abraham, Gog and Magog, the wall of Dhū al-Qarnayn), the evidence thins to literary tradition — which the Qurʾān shares with the Hebrew Bible, Syriac Christianity, and Jewish midrash.

No item examined clearly contradicts the archaeological record. Several items (Jūdī vs. Ararat; the malik / firʿawn distinction in Yūsuf; the Alif-Lām-Mīm Romans prophecy) preserve details that align with older or independent traditions in ways that are not obvious from a naive reading of the Hebrew Bible alone. Other items (Dhū al-Qarnayn, Hāmān) remain genuinely open and carry the heaviest polemical freight in both directions.

The honest verdict: when the Qurʾān makes testable historical–archaeological claims at the resolution of its own time-horizon (6th–7th c. CE Arabia, Byzantium, Persia), it passes the tests we can run. When it makes claims at deeper time-horizons, it is in a shared literary-traditional space with the Hebrew Bible, late-antique Christian apocrypha, and Jewish midrash, and cannot be independently verified from dirt alone.
