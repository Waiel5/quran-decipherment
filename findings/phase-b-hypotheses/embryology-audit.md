# Quranic Embryology: A Rigorous Audit

**Phase:** B — hypothesis evaluation
**Scope:** Q 23:12–14; Q 22:5; Q 39:6; Q 76:2; Q 75:37–39; Q 96:1–2
**Question:** Does the Quran's description of prenatal development (a) match modern embryology, (b) match the Greco-Roman medical inheritance available in 7th-century Late Antiquity, (c) constitute miraculous foreknowledge, or (d) constitute remarkable but explicable gross-morphological observation?
**Stance:** Skeptical, source-critical, willing to give credit where due.

---

## 1. The Verses in Arabic, with Translation and Lexical Notes

### 1.1 Q 23:12–14 — the "canonical" staging

> وَلَقَدۡ خَلَقۡنَا ٱلۡإِنسَٰنَ مِن سُلَٰلَةٖ مِّن طِينٖ
> ثُمَّ جَعَلۡنَٰهُ نُطۡفَةٗ فِي قَرَارٖ مَّكِينٖ
> ثُمَّ خَلَقۡنَا ٱلنُّطۡفَةَ عَلَقَةٗ فَخَلَقۡنَا ٱلۡعَلَقَةَ مُضۡغَةٗ فَخَلَقۡنَا ٱلۡمُضۡغَةَ عِظَٰمٗا فَكَسَوۡنَا ٱلۡعِظَٰمَ لَحۡمٗا ثُمَّ أَنشَأۡنَٰهُ خَلۡقًا ءَاخَرَۚ فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ أَحۡسَنُ ٱلۡخَٰلِقِينَ

"We created the human from an extract (*sulāla*) of clay. Then We made him *nuṭfa* in a secure lodging. Then We created the *nuṭfa* [as] ʿalaqa, then We created the ʿalaqa [as] muḍgha, then We created the muḍgha [as] bones (*ʿiẓām*), then We clothed the bones with flesh (*laḥm*), then We brought him forth as another creation…"

This is the most explicit ordered list in the Quran: *nuṭfa → ʿalaqa → muḍgha → ʿiẓām → laḥm → "another creation"* (typically glossed as ensoulment / qualitative transition to fetus or full human).

Note the verbal shift. The first three stages are linked by *thumma* ("then") and *fa-khalaqnā* — "we created". The bone→flesh step uses *fa-kasawnā* — "we clothed / covered". The closing "another creation" (*khalqan ākhar*) is a qualitative, not morphological, step.

### 1.2 Q 22:5 — parallel staging, with a legal twist

> خَلَقۡنَٰكُم مِّن تُرَابٖ ثُمَّ مِن نُّطۡفَةٖ ثُمَّ مِنۡ عَلَقَةٖ ثُمَّ مِن مُّضۡغَةٖ مُّخَلَّقَةٖ وَغَيۡرِ مُخَلَّقَةٖ

"…from dust, then from *nuṭfa*, then from *ʿalaqa*, then from *muḍgha* — formed (*mukhallaqa*) and unformed (*ghayri mukhallaqa*) — to make clear to you…"

Here the morphological ordering stops at muḍgha; the "formed / unformed" binary carries legal weight in later fiqh (miscarriage compensation). Bones and flesh are *not* named. This matters: the sequence "bones then flesh" is a feature of Q 23:14 only, not of every embryology passage.

### 1.3 Q 39:6 — "three darknesses"

> يَخۡلُقُكُمۡ فِي بُطُونِ أُمَّهَٰتِكُمۡ خَلۡقٗا مِّنۢ بَعۡدِ خَلۡقٖ فِي ظُلُمَٰتٖ ثَلَٰثٖ

"…He creates you in the wombs of your mothers, a creation after a creation, in three darknesses."

The *ẓulumāt thalāth* ("three darknesses") is pre-modern interpretive Rorschach. Classical mufassirūn (Ibn Kathīr, Qurṭubī) offered multiple readings: (i) belly + womb + placenta/chorion; (ii) belly + womb + amnion; (iii) three successive days / months. The claim that it refers to *amnion, chorion, decidua* (three modern anatomical membranes) is a 20th-century retrofit. No classical tafsīr identifies these three structures.

### 1.4 Q 76:2 — amshāj ("mixed drop")

> إِنَّا خَلَقۡنَا ٱلۡإِنسَٰنَ مِن نُّطۡفَةٍ أَمۡشَاجٖ

"We created the human from a nuṭfa [of] *amshāj* (mixtures)."

*amshāj* is a morphological curiosity: a plural-form adjective qualifying a singular noun. Lexicographers (al-Khalīl, Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*) gloss it as "mixed together", from the verb *mashaja* — "to mingle". Classical mufassirūn overwhelmingly read it as the mingling of male and female sexual fluids (*māʾ al-rajul wa-māʾ al-marʾa*). This is Galenic two-seed theory in native Arabic dress; it is not a description of sperm-ovum fusion in any distinctively modern sense.

### 1.5 Q 75:37–39 — compressed

> أَلَمۡ يَكُ نُطۡفَةٗ مِّن مَّنِيّٖ يُمۡنَىٰ
> ثُمَّ كَانَ عَلَقَةٗ فَخَلَقَ فَسَوَّىٰ
> فَجَعَلَ مِنۡهُ ٱلزَّوۡجَيۡنِ ٱلذَّكَرَ وَٱلۡأُنثَىٰٓ

"Was he not a *nuṭfa* from *maniyy* (semen) emitted? Then he was *ʿalaqa*, then He created and proportioned, and made of him the two kinds — male and female."

Two-stage compression: *nuṭfa → ʿalaqa*, then sex differentiation. Note: *nuṭfa min maniyy yumnā* — "a drop from semen emitted" — anchors *nuṭfa* in a purely seminal context here.

### 1.6 Q 96:1–2 — the earliest revealed embryology datum

> ٱقۡرَأۡ بِٱسۡمِ رَبِّكَ ٱلَّذِي خَلَقَ * خَلَقَ ٱلۡإِنسَٰنَ مِنۡ عَلَقٍ

"Recite in the name of your Lord who created — created the human from *ʿalaq*."

*ʿalaq*, not *ʿalaqa*. This is usually read as a collective noun from the same root (ʿ-L-Q), with the same semantic range: "clinging thing / leech / clot".

## 2. Each Term in Classical Arabic Lexicography

I give the classical senses (al-Khalīl al-Farāhīdī, *Kitāb al-ʿAyn*; Ibn Fāris, *Maqāyīs al-Lugha*; Ibn Manẓūr, *Lisān al-ʿArab*; al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, *Mufradāt*). These are the senses available to a 7th–10th century reader.

### 2.1 *nuṭfa* (نطفة)
Primary sense: a small quantity of clear fluid — literally "a drop" (*qaṭra ṣāfiya*). Used for water trickling from a cistern, for dew, and extended to semen. In Quranic contexts paired with *maniyy* (Q 75:37, 53:46, 56:58) the reference is straightforwardly seminal fluid. It does not specifically denote a cell; the Arabic lexicon contains no concept of *cell*.

### 2.2 *ʿalaqa* (علقة) / *ʿalaq*
Root ʿ-L-Q: "to hang, cling, attach." Classical senses, in approximate frequency: (i) leech (*dūda*), (ii) a clot of congealed blood, (iii) anything clinging / hanging. The lexicographers give all three. Al-Rāghib in *Mufradāt* lists "a piece of congealed blood" (*qiṭʿa min al-dam al-jāmid*) as the embryological sense.

The 20th-century apologetic move (Bucaille, Moore) emphasizes the "leech / clinging" sense because a 3–4-week implanting embryo is often said to resemble a leech clinging to the uterine wall. But the majority classical gloss among mufassirūn is "clot of blood." Both senses are present in the pre-Islamic lexicon. One cannot legitimately select the sense that best matches modern embryology and declare the match miraculous.

### 2.3 *muḍgha* (مضغة)
Root m-ḍ-gh: "to chew." A *muḍgha* is a "small chewed piece / morsel of flesh." Classical mufassirūn unanimously link this to the macroscopic appearance of a small lump of flesh about the size of what the mouth chews (*qadr mā yumḍagh*). Hadith literature (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3208, the "Ibn Masʿūd hadith" on the 40+40+40 days) uses *muḍgha* of the ~80-day embryo. The 20th-century claim that *muḍgha* specifically describes the somite-segmented appearance of a ~28-day embryo (somites as "tooth-marks") is exegetically novel and lexically unforced.

### 2.4 *ʿiẓām* (عظام)
Plural of *ʿaẓm*: "bone." No specialized sense. "Bones" as the ordinary skeletal element.

### 2.5 *laḥm* (لحم)
"Flesh, meat, muscle tissue." No specialized embryological sense. The ordinary word for the soft tissue of an animal's body.

### 2.6 *sulāla* (سلالة)
"An extract, that which is drawn out." Root s-l-l, "to draw out, pull gently." In Q 32:8 *sulāla min māʾ mahīn* ("an extract of despised water") it is paralleled with seminal language. In Q 23:12 the *sulāla min ṭīn* ("extract of clay") refers to the archetypal Adamic creation; the following verse then picks up the individuated *nuṭfa* stage.

### 2.7 *mukhallaqa* / *ghayr mukhallaqa* (Q 22:5)
"Formed / unformed." The *khalq* root here refers to shaping / differentiation. Classical jurisprudence (Ḥanafī, Mālikī) used this distinction to determine when a miscarried fetus acquires legal personhood — a question that presupposes *variability* in morphological development, which is empirically obvious to any midwife.

## 3. Galen's Stages and Their Availability in Late Antiquity

Galen (129–c. 216 CE), *On the Formation of the Foetus* (*De Foetuum Formatione*) and *On Semen* (*De Semine*), articulated a four-stage embryology that became the dominant model in Greek, Syriac, Latin and eventually Arabic medicine:

1. **Semen stage** — "like milk" or "foam"; formed from the mingling of male and female seed (Galen's two-seed theory against Aristotle's one-seed).
2. **Blood stage** — the seed receives blood and becomes a sanguineous mass; Galen uses the word "haima" and speaks of a bloody foetation filling with vessels.
3. **Flesh stage** — the blood becomes unformed flesh; the three principal organs (heart, liver, brain) begin to differentiate.
4. **Articulation / ossification stage** — limbs distinguish, bones form, the fetus takes recognizable shape.

Compare:
- *nuṭfa* ↔ Galen stage 1 (semen / mixed seed). Direct mapping.
- *ʿalaqa* (blood-clot sense) ↔ Galen stage 2 (sanguineous mass). Direct mapping.
- *muḍgha* (chewed lump of flesh) ↔ Galen stage 3 (unformed flesh). Direct mapping.
- *ʿiẓām* then *laḥm* ↔ Galen stage 4 (articulation / ossification) — but with a specifically scriptural twist of putting bone *before* surface flesh, which is not Galen's formulation.

Transmission to 7th-century Arabia:
- Galenic medicine was the dominant medical paradigm of the entire Greek-Roman-Syriac medical world throughout Late Antiquity.
- Syriac-speaking Christian physicians (at Jundishapur, later at the ʿAbbāsid court) transmitted Galenic theory directly.
- The *Book of Medicines* (Syriac, attributed variously), Hippocratic *On the Nature of the Child*, Aëtius of Amida, and Paul of Aegina circulated staged embryologies.
- The *Talmudic* tradition (tractate Niddah) also presents a staged embryology (semen → amorphous matter → cell-like form → limbs) that parallels Galen's via shared Hellenistic medical culture.

The claim that a 7th-century Hijazi audience would be unfamiliar with staged embryology is historically untenable. The details would not be common-man's knowledge; but the *model* was widely available wherever there was a literate Christian, Jewish, or mercantile population, which includes the caravanning Arab world.

## 4. Modern Embryology: Carnegie Stages

The Carnegie Stages (O'Rahilly and Müller) standardize the first 8 weeks of human development across 23 stages. Rough correspondences to Quranic terms (taking each term's most favorable modern reading):

- *nuṭfa* as sperm / zygote / blastocyst → Carnegie 1–5 (days 1–9).
- *nuṭfa amshāj* as zygote with fused parental nuclei → Carnegie 1b.
- *ʿalaqa* as implanting / implanted embryo (leech-like gloss) → Carnegie 5b–9 (days 7–23).
- *muḍgha* as somite-segmented embryo (chewed-morsel gloss) → Carnegie 10–13 (days 22–30).
- *ʿiẓām* as early chondrification of limb skeleton → Carnegie 15–18 (weeks 5–6 onward).
- *laḥm* clothing *ʿiẓām* → appearance of muscle mass overlying cartilaginous models of bone.
- *khalqan ākhar* as fetal-period transition → end of embryonic period, ~week 9 onward.

Key modern fact: bone (ossification) and muscle (myogenesis) develop *concurrently* from different embryonic sources (sclerotome vs myotome, both from the somite), and the process continues well past birth. There is no modern developmental stage at which a fetus is "all bones with no flesh." Cartilaginous models of bone form inside a fetus that already has muscle precursors in place around them.

## 5. Per-Verse / Per-Claim Verdicts

For each claim, one of four labels:
- **MATCHES** — specific, non-obvious detail that aligns with modern embryology.
- **APPROXIMATELY** — rough match to gross morphology; no stronger claim warranted.
- **AMBIGUOUS** — classical language supports multiple readings; modern match is selective.
- **INCONSISTENT WITH MODERN** — literal reading conflicts with modern data.

| Claim | Verdict | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Human comes from a seminal drop (*nuṭfa*) | APPROXIMATELY | True at gross level; available since Aristotle/Hippocrates; Galen two-seed variant fits *amshāj*. |
| Nuṭfa is a "mixture" (*amshāj*) corresponding to sperm-ovum fusion | AMBIGUOUS | Classical reading = male+female fluids (Galenic). Modern reading = sperm+ovum. Both fit the text; modern reading is not lexically forced. |
| Embryo passes through a *ʿalaqa* stage | APPROXIMATELY / AMBIGUOUS | If ʿalaqa = blood-clot: matches ancient/Galenic stage, not distinctively modern. If ʿalaqa = leech/clinging thing: matches implantation shape, but this is a post-hoc sense selection. |
| Embryo passes through a *muḍgha* stage | APPROXIMATELY | Matches macroscopic ~4-week embryo appearance; also matches Galen's unformed-flesh stage. Somite-as-tooth-marks reading is exegetical novelty. |
| The human is ultimately a "new creation" (*khalqan ākhar*) | AMBIGUOUS | Interpreted theologically; not a testable embryological claim. |
| Development occurs in "three darknesses" | AMBIGUOUS / retrofit | Classical tafsīr gave multiple non-anatomical readings; amnion/chorion/decidua identification is 20th-century apologetic. |
| Bones form, then flesh clothes the bones (Q 23:14) | INCONSISTENT WITH MODERN (literal) / APPROXIMATELY (interpretive) | Bone and muscle develop concurrently; cartilaginous limb scaffolds do form before full muscle maturation, but muscle cells are present throughout. Literal reading of successive stages is wrong; a loose "skeletal scaffold appears and tissue envelops it" reading is defensible but not distinctive. |
| Foetal viability varies — "formed / unformed" | APPROXIMATELY | Correct as legal/observational statement; obvious to midwives. |
| Sex differentiation is determined during development | APPROXIMATELY | Q 75:39 affirms sex differentiation without specifying timing or mechanism; trivially true. |

No claim in the list rises to the level of "specific, non-obvious, unavailable-elsewhere fact that could only be known by revelation." Every item either (a) was already in the Galenic / Hippocratic / Talmudic corpus, (b) is gross-morphological description available from observed miscarriages, or (c) requires selective modern sense-picking.

## 6. The "Bones Then Flesh" Problem (Q 23:14)

This is the single Quranic claim most often flagged as embryologically incorrect.

**The text:** *"fa-khalaqnā al-muḍghata ʿiẓāman fa-kasawnā al-ʿiẓāma laḥman"* — "then We created the muḍgha [as] bones, then We clothed the bones with flesh."

**The modern fact:** Muscle precursors (myoblasts) and skeletal precursors (sclerotome cells) both derive from somites and develop in parallel. By the time any recognizable bone-like cartilage appears, muscle masses are already present. There is no "all bones, no flesh" stage. Ossification (actual bone) continues long after birth, well into adolescence.

**Defenses offered by apologists:**
1. "Bones" refers to cartilage scaffolds that form first, and "flesh" to mature muscle which matures later. — Weak: muscle is already present as myoblasts during cartilage formation, and *laḥm* in classical Arabic does not map to "mature muscle mass" as distinct from early muscle cells.
2. The verbs are not strictly temporal; *fa* can indicate logical rather than sequential order. — Philologically marginal. In a parallel *fa-... fa-... fa-...* chain in narrative context, sequential reading is the default.
3. The description is from the perspective of what is visually conspicuous as the embryo develops. — Possible at the level of apologetics, but then the passage loses any claim to technical precision.

**Honest verdict:** Under a plain-sense reading that takes *ʿiẓām* as bones and *laḥm* as flesh in their ordinary classical senses, the sequence is *not* correct by modern developmental biology. Under a metaphorical or loose reading, it's defensible but then unremarkable. You cannot simultaneously claim (a) the text is precisely scientific AND (b) the bones/flesh ordering doesn't mean what it says.

A parsimonious historical explanation: the ordering reflects how a carcass is perceived in traditional butchery and bone-gathering — bones as the revealed frame, flesh as what covers them. It is also notable that the *Mishnah / Talmud* (tractate Niddah 25b and related) has passages discussing the bones of the fetus becoming enveloped, reflecting the same gross-morphology intuition. It is the native pre-scientific intuition of what a skeleton is relative to a body, not a scientific claim.

It is worth noting that apologists have sometimes cited a textual variant or alternative parsing to rescue this. The variant readings in the established *qirāʾāt* tradition (the Seven / Ten) do not materially affect this phrase. The order is fixed in the canonical text.

## 7. Keith Moore's "Developing Human" Inserts — Honest Evaluation

In the 3rd (1982) and subsequent editions of *The Developing Human*, Keith Moore added supplementary material — typically sidebars or appendices — drawing parallels between Quranic staging and the Carnegie stages. He also co-authored with Abdul-Majeed Zindani a volume titled *The Developing Human with Islamic Additions* (1983) and published popular-level articles asserting the Quran's scientific anticipations.

Evaluation:
1. **Academic medical reception.** The inserts were controversial. They were not peer-reviewed embryological claims; they were patron-driven supplementary material (the project was funded by the Saudi-based "King Abdulaziz University Commission on Scientific Signs of the Qur'an and Sunnah"). Later editions of *The Developing Human* progressively reduced or removed the Islamic inserts; current editions do not carry them. This is an important fact that is often omitted in apologetic citations.
2. **Moore's methodology.** Moore accepted specific lexical glosses supplied by his Saudi collaborators (e.g., the somite-as-chewed-marks reading of *muḍgha*, the leech-only reading of *ʿalaqa*) without independently surveying classical Arabic lexicography. He then matched those glosses to the modern stages. This is selection-on-conclusion.
3. **Moore's expertise.** Moore was a respected embryologist and textbook author. He was not a specialist in classical Arabic, in Greek-Syriac medical history, or in the sociology of scientific miracle claims. His endorsement should be weighted accordingly: he was a source for modern embryology (high weight) and a lay reader on Arabic text (low weight).
4. **The Bucaille precedent.** Maurice Bucaille's *La Bible, le Coran et la science* (1976) catalyzed the genre; Bucaille was a gastroenterologist, not an Arabist or historian of science. The Bucailleist style — matching modern science to verses by selective reading — is methodologically the same on both sides of the Moore corpus.
5. **The strongest honest summary.** A qualified expert in modern embryology, reading the Quran through lexical choices supplied by believers who wanted a match, reported that he found the match impressive. This is not strong evidence of miracle; it is evidence of what happens when one side of the translation is controlled.

Moore's testimony is not fraudulent, and the Quran's staging is indeed broadly morphologically coherent. But Moore's endorsement carries less weight than his celebrity in Islamic apologetic literature implies.

## 8. Remarkable Detail vs. Miraculous Foreknowledge

The distinction matters. A fair audit has to separate them.

**Genuinely remarkable features of the Quranic material:**
- The passages are concise, orderly, and morphologically coherent. Compared to, say, Pliny or Aristotle's more digressive embryological passages, the Quranic compression is striking.
- The staged sequence (*nuṭfa → ʿalaqa → muḍgha*) is asserted repeatedly across suras revealed at different times (96, 75, 22, 23, 76, 39), showing stable terminology — a small, consistent lexical kit for a multi-stage process.
- The "formed / unformed" distinction (*mukhallaqa / ghayr mukhallaqa*, Q 22:5) shows accurate observation that some products of conception never become a formed fetus — an empirically defensible observation of miscarriages.
- The embedding of embryology into theological reasoning about resurrection (Q 22:5's *al-baʿth*) is rhetorically powerful and textually integrated.
- The use of *amshāj* — an unusual plural-form adjective on a singular noun — is linguistically interesting, a rare form that draws the listener's attention to the *mixture* concept.

**Features that are NOT evidence of foreknowledge:**
- None of the individual stages are unique to the Quran. Every term has a pre-Islamic or Galenic antecedent.
- The bone-before-flesh ordering is either wrong (literal reading) or unremarkable (loose reading).
- The "three darknesses" match to modern membranes is 20th-century apologetic; no classical mufassir reads it so.
- The identification of *nuṭfa* with a specific cell type (zygote) is a 20th-century lexical decision imposed on a word that had no cellular referent.
- The selective use of the "leech" sense of *ʿalaqa* over the "clot" sense to match modern implantation visuals is post-hoc cherry-picking.

**Net:** The Quran presents a compact, theologically integrated, morphologically coherent embryology that is consistent with what an observant Late Antique Arab with exposure to Hellenistic/Syriac medicine, Jewish halakhic embryology, and pastoral observation of animal and human miscarriages could plausibly compose. It is not consistent with specific foreknowledge of cellular, genetic, or microscopic embryology, because it contains no terms or distinctions that require such knowledge.

The most honest summary: the passages are **impressive for their date, consistent with their Late Antique milieu, and do not rise above that milieu in informational content.**

## 9. What Would Count as Evidence of Foreknowledge?

To sharpen the audit: what would it take to convince a fair skeptic that an ancient text demonstrated embryological foreknowledge?
- A term for a developmental structure that had no Greek, Syriac, Hebrew, or Aramaic antecedent and which denotes a microscopic or molecular feature (blastocyst cavity, neural crest migration, haematopoietic switch).
- A timing claim that contradicts Galen and matches modern data (e.g., that the heart begins beating before limb buds are visible, or that sex is determined at conception but not morphologically visible until week 7).
- A distinction that cuts against contemporaneous folk embryology (e.g., rejecting the two-seed theory in favor of one-seed-plus-passive-receptacle, or vice versa, with a specific observation).
- A numerical claim that matches modern data and not the Galenic / Talmudic data (e.g., a day-count for a specific stage).

The Quran offers none of these. The 40+40+40 day timing found in a Bukhārī hadith (*Ibn Masʿūd*, Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3208) is reasonably close to Hippocratic and Talmudic numbers and does not represent new information.

---

## 10. Summary Verdict

1. The Quran contains a concise, multiply-attested, morphologically coherent sketch of prenatal development using five or six core Arabic terms.
2. Every stage and every term has a Late Antique analog — in Galen, Hippocratic literature, Talmudic tractates, or ordinary pastoral observation of animal and human miscarriages.
3. The "bones then flesh" sequence of Q 23:14 is inconsistent with modern developmental biology if read literally, and unremarkable if read loosely.
4. The "three darknesses" mapping to amnion/chorion/decidua is a 20th-century retrofit with no classical tafsīr support.
5. The selective *ʿalaqa*-as-leech gloss favored by Bucaille and Moore is one of several available classical senses; it is not lexically privileged.
6. Keith Moore's endorsement was made in a context of patronage and selective lexical feed; later editions of his textbook dropped the inserts.
7. The Quran is impressive as 7th-century discourse on embryology; it is not evidence of miraculous foreknowledge.

The honest verdict is neither the dismissive "this is just Galen translated" nor the apologetic "this anticipates modern science." It is in the middle: **an informed 7th-century text whose content is broadly continuous with its medical milieu, whose few striking features are rhetorical rather than informational, and whose literal sequence contains at least one item (bones before flesh) that conflicts with modern data.**
