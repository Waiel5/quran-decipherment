# Sacrifice and Vow Theology in the Qurʾān

**Phase B hypothesis document.** Data source: `data/morphology/quranic-corpus-morphology-0.4.txt`. All verse references verified against the Quranic Arabic Corpus morphology. Roman transliteration uses the Buckwalter scheme in queries; a reader-friendly transliteration is given in the body.

## 1. Per-Word Inventory

### 1.1 Root `*bH` — dhabaḥa "to slaughter (ritually)"

Nine occurrences in three distinct pragmatic fields.

| Ref | Form | Vocalized | Gloss | Speaker/context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2:49 | `yu*ab~iHu` | yudhabbiḥu (Form II) | "slaughters (intensively)" | Pharaoh slaughtering Israelite sons |
| 2:67 | `ta*obaHu` | tadhbaḥū | "you slaughter (cow)" | Moses to his people |
| 2:71 | `*abaHu` | dhabaḥū | "they slaughtered" | Moses' people finally obey |
| 5:3 | `*ubiHa` | dhubiḥa (PASS) | "was slaughtered" | prohibition of altar-sacrifices |
| 14:6 | `yu*ab~iHu` | yudhabbiḥu | "slaughters" | Pharaoh's oppression recalled |
| 27:21 | `>a*obaHa` | adhbaḥannahu | "I shall surely slaughter him" | Solomon threatens hoopoe |
| 28:4 | `yu*ab~iHu` | yudhabbiḥu | "slaughters" | Pharaoh again |
| 37:102 | `>a*obaHu` | adhbaḥu-ka | "I slaughter you" | Abraham to Ishmael |
| 37:107 | `*iboHK` | dhibḥin (noun) | "a slaughter-offering" | ransom for Ishmael |

Form II `yudhabbiḥu` (intensive) is reserved for Pharaoh (3×). The Form I verb is cultic-legal (Moses' cow) or narrative (Abraham, Solomon, prohibition). The noun `dhibḥ` is a **hapax legomenon** at 37:107. This uniqueness matters: the single noun-form of sacrifice in the Qurʾān is the divine ransom for Abraham's son.

### 1.2 Root `qrb` — qurbān "drawn-near offering"

Three attestations of the noun, all in polemical/failure contexts:

| Ref | Context |
|---|---|
| 3:183 | Jewish objection — "until he bring us a qurbān which fire consumes" (rebuked) |
| 5:27 | Cain and Abel — "when they both offered a qurbān" (one rejected, murder follows) |
| 46:28 | Pagans' "gods taken as qurbān" (mediators who abandoned them) |

The verb `qarraba` (5:27:8 `qar~aba`) appears in Form II with the Cain-Abel episode — "they drew near (with) a qurbān". The Qurʾān never labels a normative Muslim rite a qurbān; the noun is markedly **absent from hajj regulation, prayer instruction, and the 108:2/6:162 worship formulae**. This is a significant negative datum.

### 1.3 Root `n*r` (vow-sense only) — nadhara "to vow"

Seven tokens belong to the vow-sense, separated from 316 warner-sense tokens:

| Ref | Form | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 2:270 | `na*aro`, `n~a*orK` | verb + noun — "whatever you spend or vow" (charity law) |
| 3:35 | `na*aro` | "I have vowed to You what is in my womb, liberated" (Mary's mother) |
| 19:26 | `na*aro` | "I have vowed to the Merciful a fast" (Mary's silence-vow) |
| 22:29 | `nu*uwra` | "let them fulfil their vows" (pilgrimage context) |
| 76:7 | `n~a*ori` | "they fulfil the vow" (abrār) |
| 77:6 | `nu*orFA` | "as warning or excuse" (an ambiguous case — often read as warner-sense) |

Two paradigms emerge: (a) vow as **dedication of person** (3:35 the unborn child; 19:26 Mary's own speech), (b) vow as **commitment to divine-directed spending** (2:270, 76:7). Animal-vows are conspicuously absent — in contrast to Biblical Leviticus and pre-Islamic Arabian practice.

### 1.4 Root `hdy` — hady "Hajj-sacrificial animal"

Seven occurrences of the noun `hadoy`:

| Ref | Context |
|---|---|
| 2:196 (×3) | Legislation for pilgrim who cannot reach the Sacred House: send hady; if prevented, fast three days + seven |
| 5:2 | Do not profane God's sacred months, nor the hady, nor the garlanded offerings |
| 5:95 | Penalty-hady for hunting during iḥrām |
| 5:97 | "God made the Kaʿba… and the hady and the garlands… a standing for humanity" |
| 48:25 | The prevented hady at Ḥudaybiyya |

The hady is **always a noun, never a verb**. The word shares its root with *hidāya* (guidance) and *hadiyya* (gift) — semantically "that which is sent toward." The phonetic near-homonym `had-` for "guidance" is theologically exploited by 22:37 ("He subjected them to you so you may magnify God for *what He has guided you*" — `mā hadākum`), puncturing the word-chain hady/hudā in a single verse.

### 1.5 Root `nsk` — nusuk / mansak "rite, act of worship"

Seven occurrences:

| Ref | Form | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 2:128 | manāsikanā | "show us our rites" (Abrahamic prayer) |
| 2:196 | nusukin | "fast or charity or nusuk" (fidya for shaving during iḥrām) |
| 2:200 | manāsikakum | "when you complete your rites" |
| 6:162 | nusukī | "my rite" (paired with ṣalāt) |
| 22:34 | mansakan | "for every nation We have appointed a rite" |
| 22:67 | mansakan | "for every nation We have appointed a rite — which they follow" |
| 22:67 | nāsikū | "performers of the rite" |

`Nusuk` is the Qurʾān's preferred generic term for cultic worship-acts. It is the only sacrifice-term the Qurʾān universalizes ("for every nation a mansak") and the only one paired with individual devotional ownership ("nusukī" — 6:162). It is the most theologically load-bearing sacrifice-root.

### 1.6 Root `nHr` — naḥara "to slaughter (camel, by jugular stab)"

**One** occurrence in the whole Qurʾān: 108:2 `{noHaro` — imperative to the Prophet. The word is hapax-verb. The classical philology distinguishes *dhabḥ* (throat-cut, for sheep/cattle) from *naḥr* (upper-chest stab, for camels). The Qurʾān never uses the latter of anyone besides the Prophet at this single moment. The contrastive rarity is itself a rhetorical signal.

### 1.7 Root `Eqr` — ʿaqara "to hamstring" (anti-sacrifice)

Three occurrences, all against Ṣāliḥ's she-camel: 7:77, 54:29, 91:14. The verb is explicitly **non-cultic** — an act of agrarian violence that the Qurʾān never assimilates to any sacrifice-vocabulary. See §4 below.

### 1.8 Cross-root: `fdy` — fadā "to ransom"

At 37:107 `fadayo-hu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīm` — "We ransomed him with a magnificent slaughter". The verb is from the same root family as *fidya* (Hajj substitute-offering at 2:196). The paradigm is **substitutionary**, not propitiatory.

## 2. Abraham's Sacrifice (Q 37:99–113) — The Paradigm Narrative

The most theologically dense sacrifice narrative in the Qurʾān. The morphology yields a micro-structure:

1. **Request for progeny** (99–100): Abraham's `sayahdīni` ("He will guide me" — root `hdy`) and `hab lī min al-ṣāliḥīn` ("grant me among the righteous"). Already the root `hdy` — which later delivers the hady-offering — is planted.

2. **Dream-vision** (102): `innī arā fī al-manāmi annī adhbaḥu-ka` ("I see in dream that I slaughter you"). The verb is ∫**form I `adhbaḥu`** — not the Pharaonic Form II of intensified slaughter. The Qurʾān reserves Form I dhabḥ for legitimate cultic slaughter.

3. **Submission** (103): `fa-lammā aslamā` — "when they both submitted". The verb is from *slm*, the same root as *muslim*. The narrative redefines sacrifice as mutual submission: **two** subjects submit, not one victim and one priest.

4. **Call-off** (104–105): `nādaynāhu an yā-Ibrāhīmu, qad ṣaddaqta al-ruʾyā` — "O Abraham, you have confirmed the vision". The Arabic emphasizes **truthfulness of the dream-fulfilment** even in aborted act. The sacrifice is reckoned as accomplished on the level of intention.

5. **Explicit test-language** (106): `inna hādhā la-huwa al-balāʾu l-mubīn` — "this is truly the manifest trial". The word *balāʾ* frames the whole sequence as trial-not-requirement. God does not desire the flesh; He tests the heart — a hermeneutic principle later crystallized at 22:37.

6. **Ransom** (107): `wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīm` — "We ransomed him with a magnificent slaughter". Four lexically marked features:
   - **fadā** (ransom/redeem) — substitutionary, not expiatory.
   - **dhibḥ** as noun — the only nominal use of the slaughter-root in the Qurʾān.
   - **ʿaẓīm** (magnificent) — the adjective also describes the Throne (2:255), the Day (18:21), and the Qurʾān itself (15:87). The ransom is not "large" but "magnificent" — aesthetically/metaphysically exalted.
   - **bi-** prefix — the ransom is instrumental: God ransoms Abraham's obedience by providing the physical rite.

The ʿīd al-aḍḥā is commemorative of this event. Yet the Qurʾān never uses the word *uḍḥiya* (festive sacrifice) — post-Quranic legal vocabulary.

**Hypothesis H1**: The Qurʾān's paradigm sacrifice is SUBSTITUTIONARY-COMMEMORATIVE, not propitiatory. God does not demand the son; God tests the surrender of the son and provides His own ram. The Muslim Hajj-sacrifice reenacts the *ransom*, not the *slaughter of a son*. The theological burden of guilt/expiation (prominent in Levitical or Pauline systems) is **absent from the Quranic vocabulary of this scene**.

## 3. Moses' Cow-Sacrifice (Q 2:67–71)

A legally-ordered slaughter framed as trial-of-obedience. Structural notes from the morphology:

- **2:67**: `inna llāha yaʾmurukum an tadhbaḥū baqaratan`. The verb is form-I, the noun *baqara* is feminine indefinite — "a cow, any cow". Obedience would have been completed immediately.
- **2:68-70**: The people ask `maA hiYa` ("what is she?") three successive times, receiving progressively narrower specifications. The morphology shows repeated `ya`^-vocatives and interrogative `maA`, giving the passage a comic-ritual tone.
- **2:71**: `fa-dhabaḥūhā wa-mā kādū yafʿalūn` — "so they slaughtered her, and they nearly did not do so". The verb of slaughter *arrives* in the perfect form, but is immediately qualified by near-failure.

**Hypothesis H2**: Unlike the Abrahamic paradigm where submission precedes the act, the Mosaic episode illustrates the **pathology of ritual legalism**: the more detail the community demands, the harder obedience becomes. Sacrifice here is a *test of trust*, and the near-failure of the Israelites is the point. This supplies the Qurʾānic negative counterpoint to 37:99–113. The entire sura takes its name (*al-Baqara*) from this episode — signalling its diagnostic weight.

Noteworthy anti-typology: Moses' cow is eventually slaughtered after delay; Abraham's son is ransomed before slaughter. The first is obedience-after-hesitation; the second is submission-followed-by-mercy.

## 4. Ṣāliḥ's She-camel — Multiple Retellings (7:73–78, 11:64–68, 26:155–158, 54:27–31, 91:13–14)

The Qurʾān has **five** major retellings of the she-camel narrative. In every single one, the act of killing is called **ʿaqr** (hamstringing) — never dhabḥ, never naḥr, never qurbān, never nusuk, never hady. The morphology confirms:

- 7:77 `Eaqaru wA@ l-nāqata` — "they hamstrung the she-camel"
- 26:157 (same root)
- 54:29 `fa-Eaqara` — a single 3MS verb, climactic and terse
- 91:14 `fa-Eaqaru wA@-hā`

The she-camel is introduced as `naAqapu {ll~ahi` — **"the she-camel of God"** (7:73, 91:13) — and called `'aAyapF` (a sign, 7:73). Her killing is the profaning of a sign, not the mis-performance of a sacrifice. The theological thrust is **iconoclastic** (she is not a victim to be slaughtered but a sign to be kept alive) and **ecological** (she shares water with the community by divine portion, 54:28 `qismatun baynahum`). Her refused co-existence is the sin.

**Hypothesis H3**: The she-camel narrative is the Qurʾān's *anti-sacrifice* story. Against ancient Near Eastern patterns where the slaughter of a sacred animal unlocks divine favor, the Qurʾān reverses the valence: the slaughter of God's sign brings annihilation. This is theologically continuous with 22:37 (the flesh does not reach God) and 5:3 (the prohibition of offerings to stone-altars). The Qurʾān **demythologizes** animal-killing — restricting its ritual valency to hady and nusuk, and de-fanging any theology of blood-merit.

The short sura-versions (54, 91) use no sacrificial vocabulary at all — just the stark verb ʿaqara. This is rhetorically powerful: the text refuses the dignity of cultic language for the deed.

## 5. Cain and Abel (Q 5:27–31) — First Narrative of Accepted/Rejected Sacrifice

Lexical features from the morphology:

- **qarraba qurbānan** (5:27:8–9): form-II verb + cognate accusative. The construction is pleonastic ("they drew-near a drawing-near-offering"), emphasizing the ritual formality.
- **tuqubbila / lam yutaqabbal** (5:27:10, 5:27:14): both passive Form V of *qbl* ("accept"). The agent of acceptance is unnamed in the passive — God's hidden scrutiny.
- **innamā yataqabbalu llāhu mina al-muttaqīn** (5:27:20–24): the explanatory maxim — "God accepts only from the God-conscious". The acceptance criterion is **taqwā**, not the quality of the offering.
- **qatala** (kill, 5:30): Cain kills his brother — the root *qtl* deliberately contrasted with *qrb* ("draw near"). The brothers draw near with an offering; one draws near his brother with murder.
- **ghurāb yabḥathu** (5:31): the crow *scratches/searches* in the ground. The morphology shows `yaboHavu` from root `bHv` (search) — NOT `*bH` (slaughter). Auditory near-homophony plays a macabre role: the unburied body, the scratching bird, and the rejected dhabḥ-world all coalesce acoustically.

**Hypothesis H4**: The Cain-Abel episode establishes the Qurʾān's central criterion for any act-of-drawing-near: **taqwā of the offerer, not the weight/kind of the offering**. This criterion is reiterated at 22:37 ("taqwā from you reaches Him") and made legislative at 6:162. The Qurʾān embeds its theology of sacrifice in the story of its **origin of rejection**, not in the story of its institution. The first narrative of sacrifice in the Qurʾānic canon (by narrative order within sūrat al-Māʾida, the last major sura revealed) is a story of **failure and fratricide**, not of founding-order.

## 6. Mary's Nadhr of Silence (Q 19:26)

`innī nadhartu li-l-raḥmāni ṣawman fa-lan ukallima l-yawma insiyyan` — "I have vowed to the Merciful a fast, so I shall not speak today to any human."

Lexical/morphological features:

- **nadhartu** (19:26:12): first-person perfect of the vow-verb. The vow is autonomous, not mediated through a priest or male guardian.
- **li-l-raḥmāni**: the divine name is specifically *al-Raḥmān* — the Merciful. Mary's vow is to Mercy, not to Power or Judgment.
- **ṣawman**: a fast. But then she glosses it — `fa-lan ukallima` — "so I shall not speak".

The vow **re-categorizes fast**: it is fast-from-speech, not fast-from-food. This is the only Qurʾānic instance of a vow whose substance is *speech-withholding*. Three consequences:

1. Sacrifice is internalized: the offering is not an object but a faculty.
2. The vow is entirely personal — no cultic apparatus, no priest, no altar.
3. The vow is also strategic: it is a ritual device enabling the infant Jesus to speak for her (19:29–30), substituting infant-speech for her own. Sacrifice-of-speech becomes divine-speech.

**Hypothesis H5**: The Qurʾān's Maryam-nadhr is the feminized, interiorized apex of the vow-theology. Her mother's vow (3:35 — dedicating the unborn child, muḥarraran "liberated for service") and her own vow form a two-generation arc in which the vow is always a **dedication of a person**, never an animal immolation. This is culturally radical: the vow-paradigm in the Qurʾān is Marian, not Levantine-pagan.

## 7. The Hajj-Sacrifice (hady)

The hady is legislated, not dramatized. Key passages:

- **2:196**: The core fiqh-verse. *In iḥṣirtum fa-mā ystaysara min al-hady*: if prevented, whatever hady is available. The verse pairs hady with **fidya** (substitute) — fasting, charity, nusuk. The legal triplet *ṣiyām aw ṣadaqa aw nusuk* shows the elasticity: the rite can be substituted by non-slaughter acts.
- **5:2**: hady is placed between the sacred months and the garlanded offerings — liturgical protected objects.
- **5:95**: The compensation for game hunted during iḥrām is a *hadyan bāligha l-Kaʿba* — "a hady reaching the Kaʿba". The sacrifice here is punitive-compensatory, not propitiatory.
- **5:97**: hady is among the "standings for humanity" (*qiyāman li-l-nās*) — it structures social time.
- **48:25**: the hady prevented from the Kaʿba at Ḥudaybiyya — a historical notch, showing that prevention of hady is itself a form of injustice.

The hady is never called a qurbān, never a dhibḥ, and never a mansak-naḥr. It occupies its own semantic slot — "gift-sent-toward". The root **hdy** encodes directionality (to God, to the Kaʿba) without the blood-mysticism of other ancient Near Eastern systems.

**Hypothesis H6**: The hady is best understood as a **sanctified substitute**: the animal stands in for the pilgrim's own body in its journey to the sanctuary. At Ḥudaybiyya (48:25) the Prophet sends 70 hady ahead as emissaries; this captures the logic — the hady is a *delegate body*. 22:37 then strips the delegation of mystical weight: the flesh does not reach God. What reaches is the taqwā of the sender.

## 8. Q 108:2 — `fa-ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar` (Pray + Slaughter)

The shortest sura pairs prayer with sacrifice in a single imperative line:

| Word | Root | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| fa-ṣalli | Slw | imperative form-II from ṣalā |
| li-rabbika | rbb | directional — "for your Lord" |
| wa-nḥar | nHr | imperative form-I, **hapax verb** |

The morphology shows `Sal~i` and `{noHaro` as simple imperatives directed at the Prophet. The word *nḥar* is unique to this line. In classical fiqh the verse is variously read as a command to slaughter camels at ʿīd, to raise the hands in prayer to the throat (naḥr = throat), or to stand erect. The morphological parsimony of the Quranic usage leaves the semantic field open.

What is striking is the **structural asymmetry**: prayer is named by its standard verb (ṣalā), sacrifice is named by a verb that occurs nowhere else in the Qurʾān. If the text wanted "slaughter" in the ordinary sense it had *dhabaḥa* available. The choice of `nḥar` — with its camel-associations and its sole appearance here — suggests a deliberate marking: sacrifice-as-prayer, not sacrifice-as-slaughter.

The sura's triadic structure reinforces this:

- 108:1 — `innā aʿṭaynāka l-kawthar` (We have given you abundance)
- 108:2 — prayer + naḥr (the response to the gift)
- 108:3 — the shāniʾ (enemy) is the one truly cut-off (`al-abtar`)

The gift → response → inversion-of-the-enemy pattern is the sura's architecture. The naḥr is thus a **response-act**, not a propitiation. One does not slaughter *to obtain* favor — one has already received kawthar.

**Hypothesis H7**: 108:2 reverses the normal gift-economy of sacrifice. In most ancient sacrificial systems, the offering is the cause and the divine favor is the effect. Here: the divine favor is cause (v.1 perfect tense `aʿṭaynāka`), the naḥr is effect (v.2 imperative). This is the *post-gift* sacrifice: the naḥr exists to thank, not to induce.

## 9. Q 6:162 — `inna ṣalātī wa-nusukī` (Life-as-Sacrifice Formula)

`qul inna ṣalātī wa-nusukī wa-maḥyāya wa-mamātī li-llāhi rabbi l-ʿālamīn`
"Say: My prayer, my rite, my life, and my death — for God, Lord of the worlds."

The morphology reveals a rhythmic four-noun construction with a single lām-attachment:

| Element | Root | Suffix |
|---|---|---|
| ṣalāt-ī | Slw | my |
| nusuk-ī | nsk | my |
| maḥyā-ya | Hyy | my |
| mamāt-ī | mwt | my |

The four first-person possessive nouns are stacked and resolved at once by the directional phrase `li-llāhi`. The structure dissolves the distinction between prayer-act, sacrifice-act, and life/death. **The formula abolishes the boundary between cultic and existential.**

Note:
- The word is `nusuk` (root nsk) — the universalizing sacrifice-word — not qurbān or dhibḥ. This is deliberate: the formula speaks of the generic rite, not any specific blood-act.
- Life and death are conjugated into the sacrifice-verb's neighborhood. The existential is ritualized; the ritual is totalized.
- `li-llāhi rabbi l-ʿālamīn` — God of the worlds (plural) — universalizes the direction. The offering is not to a tribal deity but to the cosmic Lord.

**Hypothesis H8**: 6:162 is the Qurʾān's most radical *interiorization* of sacrifice. It generalizes from Abraham's moment of submission (37:103 *fa-lammā aslamā*) to a lifelong posture. The Muslim's entire existence is reclassified as nusuk. This is paired with the identical formula in v.163 (`lā sharīka lahu wa-bi-dhālika umirtu wa-anā awwalu l-muslimīn`) — the Prophet styles himself the "first of those who submit". Sacrifice-as-submission is the meaning of *Islam*.

## 10. Synthesis — Nine Hypotheses

1. **H1 — Substitution not propitiation.** 37:107 encodes sacrifice as divine gift returned to the obedient, not the obedient's payment to God.
2. **H2 — The Mosaic cow as diagnostic.** Delayed obedience generates legalistic proliferation; 2:67–71 is a warning about the pathology of literalism.
3. **H3 — The she-camel as anti-sacrifice.** The repeated use of *ʿaqara* (never cultic vocabulary) across five surahs demythologizes animal-killing.
4. **H4 — Taqwā as acceptance-criterion.** Cain-Abel (5:27) + 22:37 together state the hermeneutic: God sees the heart, not the carcass.
5. **H5 — The Marian vow-paradigm.** The vow is dedication of persons, often via abstention; never animal-immolation. The Qurʾānic nadhr is gendered-feminine-interior.
6. **H6 — Hady as delegate body.** The pilgrim's hady stands in for the pilgrim's person; 48:25 (Ḥudaybiyya) illustrates this logic most clearly.
7. **H7 — 108:2 as post-gift response.** The kawthar precedes the naḥr; gratitude precedes sacrifice.
8. **H8 — 6:162 as totalizing formula.** Sacrifice is life-coextensive, not a discrete rite.
9. **H9 (meta)**. The Qurʾān **structurally avoids** the vocabulary of blood-mysticism. *Dhibḥ* is a hapax noun, *naḥara* is a hapax verb, *qurbān* appears only in failure-contexts, *ʿaqr* is reserved for sacrilege. The central generic term is *nusuk* — rite — and the central legislative term is *hady* — that-which-is-sent. The Qurʾān's sacrificial lexicon is constructed by *negation* of ancient Near Eastern cultic specificity and *affirmation* of intentional-relational categories.

## 11. The Integrating Verse — 22:37

`lan yanāla llāha luḥūmuhā wa-lā dimāʾuhā wa-lākin yanāluhu l-taqwā minkum`

"Their flesh will never reach Allah, nor their blood, but the taqwā from you reaches Him."

This is the Qurʾān's own meta-commentary on its sacrificial theology. Four observations:

1. **lan** — the double-negative of absolute futurity. The denial is categorical, not situational.
2. **luḥūm + dimāʾ** — the two elements a blood-cult believes *do* reach the deity are both denied at once.
3. **yanāluhu l-taqwā** — the abstract noun "God-consciousness" is the grammatical subject of *reaching*. An interior state is granted the mobility of a material offering.
4. **minkum** — "from you". The taqwā must originate in the offerer. The vertical motion (reaching God) is strictly indexed to horizontal origination (you).

22:37 thus closes the loop opened by 5:27 (Cain's rejected qurbān) and 37:107 (Abraham's ransomed dhibḥ). Sacrifice, in the Qurʾān, is a spiritual-pedagogical institution whose material form is penultimate. The blood is real; the taqwā is what reaches.
