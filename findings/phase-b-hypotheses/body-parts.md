# Body Parts in the Quran

The Quran's corporeal lexicon is unusually lean — roughly twenty distinct
anatomical nouns — yet it is structurally dense: every body-part noun lands
in a *rhetorical slot* (eschatological, legal, anthropological, or
theophanic) rather than in a biological description. This memo catalogues
every major anatomical lexeme, then pursues seven focal questions commissioned
by the coordinator.

**Total distinct anatomical nouns:** 20. **Total tokens across all roots:**
≈ 484. For comparison: the root *q-l-b* alone accounts for 168 (heart +
heart-verbs), already logged in `phase-c-structures/qalb-theology.md`. Adding
*qalb* to this inventory brings the bodily lexicon to roughly 650 tokens, of
which the heart is ≈ 26 %.

**Structural observations from inventory alone:**

1. **Upper body dominates.** Face, hand, eye, mouth, tongue, head, chest,
   ear account for ~350 tokens. The lower body (foot, leg, knee) is sparse.
2. **Internal organs are all but absent.** Apart from *qalb* and *ṣadr*,
   the Quran names no liver, lungs, kidneys, or stomach-lining. *Baṭn* is
   used for the uterine interior (mothers' bellies) and for whale/earth
   interiors, not as anatomy.
3. **Anatomy of death and judgement.** Skin, bone, throat, and spine all
   concentrate in eschatological verses (resurrection, torment, the final
   gasp). They essentially do not appear in daily-life contexts.
4. **Dual forms predominate for the paired organs.** Eyes, hands, feet,
   ears, lips — the pair is linguistically marked (see §6).

## 2. Wajh Allāh — the Face of God (11 Occurrences)

The face (*wajh*) is the Quran's most frequent body noun after the hand —
74 tokens. Of these, **11 stand in a construct (iḍāfa) with Allah or
Rabb**, forming the classical "Face of God" theologoumenon:

| Verse | Formula | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 2:112 | *aslama wajhahu li-llāh* | Whoever submits his face to God |
| 2:115 | *fa-thamma wajhu llāh* | "there is the Face of God" (direction) |
| 2:272 | *illā btighāʾa wajhi llāh* | spending only for God's Face |
| 3:20 | *aslamtu wajhiya li-llāh* | "I have submitted my face to God" |
| 4:125 | *aslama wajhahu li-llāh* | Abraham's religion |
| 13:22 | *ibtighāʾa wajhi rabbihim* | the patient who seek Rabb's Face |
| 30:38 | *yurīdūna wajha llāh* | those who desire God's Face |
| 30:39 | *turīdūna wajha llāh* | ditto |
| 55:27 | *yabqā wajhu rabbik* | *only the Face of your Lord remains* |
| 76:9 | *li-wajhi llāh* | we feed you *for the Face of God* |
| 92:20 | *ibtighāʾa wajhi rabbihi l-aʿlā* | seeking the Face of his Most High Lord |

Two grammatical families appear. In the first (Q 2:112, 3:20, 4:125), *wajh*
takes a possessive suffix denoting *the human's* face, which is then
"submitted to" (*aslama li-*) God: the face is the part-for-whole totality
of the self. In the second (Q 2:115, 2:272, 13:22, 30:38–39, 55:27, 76:9,
92:20), the iḍāfa is *wajhu llāh* — "God's Face" — either as direction
(Q 2:115) or as intended recipient of pious acts (*ibtighāʾa wajh*) or as
the sole subsistent reality (Q 55:27 *yabqā wajhu rabbik*).

**The classical debate.** Early traditionists (Ibn Ḥanbal, al-Dārimī) read
these verses literalistically: Allah has a *wajh* as an attribute, *bi-lā
kayf* ("without asking how"). The Ashʿarī and Māturīdī kalām schools glossed
*wajh* as *dhāt* (essence) or *riḍā* (pleasure) — so *yabqā wajhu rabbik*
is read as "the essence of your Lord remains" rather than "the face of your
Lord remains." The Muʿtazila were the most aggressive metaphoricisers. The
Ẓāhirīs (Ibn Ḥazm) and the Ḥanbalī traditionalist revival (Ibn Taymiyya,
Ibn al-Qayyim) defended the *tafwīḍ* + affirmation reading: the attribute
is real, its modality is unknown. Modern Salafī theology follows this line.

What the text itself *rhetorically* does: the 11 verses *equally* use the
human *wajh* (submitted) and the divine *wajh* (sought). They are mirror
constructs. The human submits *his* face; he seeks *God's* Face. This
perfectly inverted pair — submit-yours, seek-His — makes *wajh* the single
meeting-point of human and divine in Quranic anthropology. It is arguably
significant that the face-of-God construct is centred on *meeting*, not on
description: no verse describes what *wajhu llāh* *looks like*; every verse
describes what one *does toward it* (submit, seek, spend for).

## 3. Q 41:20–22 — The Skins Testify

*Jild / julūd* (skin) occurs 11 times. Five of those tokens crowd into a
single three-verse passage (Q 41:20, 21, 22 — *julūdu*, *julūdi*, *julūdu*
again across 41:21, 22, with *julūduhum* opening 41:20). The scene:

> Until, when they reach it, their hearing, their sight, and their skins
> will testify against them concerning what they used to do. (41:20)
>
> And they will say to their skins: "Why did you testify against us?"
> They will say: "Allah made us speak, who makes all things speak; He
> created you the first time, and to Him you are returned." (41:21)
>
> You did not hide yourselves lest your hearing, your sight, and your
> skins testify against you — but you thought that Allah did not know much
> of what you do. (41:22)

Three observations.

**(a) The testimony-triad is stable.** Verses 20 and 22 both list *samʿ*
(hearing), *abṣār* (sight), *julūd* (skins) — in the same order. Hearing
and sight are *interior* senses (they enter the person); skin is the
*boundary*. The triad is thus ear → eye → surface, moving outward. The
boundary is what betrays concealment (*tastatirūna* in 41:22, from *satr*,
"to cover").

**(b) Skin speaks only because Allah makes it speak.** The dialogue in
41:21 resolves the theological problem of talking skin: it is not skin that
is inherently sentient, it is Allah who is the *anṭaqa* — the *one who makes
things speak*. The same verb recurs in a tightly parallel doctrine (Q 24:24,
36:65). In Q 24:24, *"their tongues, hands, and feet"* testify; in Q 36:65,
*"We seal their mouths; their hands speak to Us, and their feet testify"*;
here, *skins* speak. The three passages form a triangle where, on the Day,
every organ of *outward expression* — tongue, hand, foot, skin — becomes a
witness against its owner.

**(c) Skin is the organ of *satr* (concealment) inverted.** Everyday
experience: skin covers the interior. Eschatological reversal: skin *reports*
the interior. The verse pivots on this reversal: *you-hid-yourselves from
your-skins lest your-skins testify*. The Quran is playing on the oldest
boundary metaphor — surface conceals depth — and inverting it. This is
continuous with other Quranic inversions of concealment: *yaʿlamu khāʾinata
l-aʿyun* (He knows the treachery of the eyes, Q 40:19); *wa mā takhtalifūna
min sirr* (nothing you hide differs for Him, Q 10:61). The body, in
eschatology, is all surface.

## 4. Yadā Allāh — Q 38:75 and Q 5:64

Of the 120 *yad*-tokens, two are grammatically and theologically load-bearing:

**Q 38:75:** *mā manaʿaka an tasjuda li-mā khalaqtu bi-yadayya* —
"What prevented you from prostrating to what I created with **My two hands**?"
The dual *yaday* + 1SG possessive *-ya* yields *yadayya* ("my two hands") —
spoken by Allah to Iblīs. The object of creation is Adam (masculine, and
the "what," *mā*, grammatically neuter).

**Q 5:64:** *qālati l-yahūdu yadu llāhi maghlūlatun ghullat aydīhim wa-
luʿinū bi-mā qālū bal yadāhu mabsūṭatāni* — "The Jews said, 'Allah's hand
is chained.' May their hands be chained, and let them be cursed for what
they said. Rather, **His two hands are outstretched**." The rebuttal
*yadāhu mabsūṭatāni* (dual) is the climactic affirmation.

**The debate.** The grammar is unambiguously dual in both verses. The
question is whether the dual should be read literally (God has two
attribute-hands) or figuratively (bounty and blessing, kindness and
severity, past and present generosity, etc.).

Traditionist position (Ibn Ḥanbal, later Ibn Taymiyya): the duality is real
as attribute, uninterpreted as modality. The argument is grammatical — if
Allah had meant "power" He could have used the singular *yad* (as He does
elsewhere, *yadu llāhi fawqa aydīhim* Q 48:10). The dual is deliberate.

Metaphorical position (Muʿtazila, most Ashʿarīs for popular teaching):
*yadayya* = bounty and favour; *yadāhu mabsūṭatāni* = "His generosity
flows in two streams," a standard Arabic idiom for unrestricted giving.
Rāzī cites parallels in pre-Islamic poetry.

What the Quran's *own* use does: out of 120 *yad*-tokens, the dual with
divine possessive appears **only in these two verses**. Every other "hand
of God" is singular (*yadu llāhi*, Q 48:10; *biyadihi*, Q 2:245, 3:73, 5:1,
23:88, 36:83, 48:20, 57:29, 67:1). This rarity suggests that *the duality
is semantically loaded*: it does something the singular does not. The fact
that Q 38:75 places the dual at the moment of creating Adam (the sole human
the Quran says was created *by God directly* rather than born or made-to-be)
and that Q 5:64 places it in explicit contrast to the Jewish charge of
divine "chained-ness" suggests an emphatic insistence on *full divine
agency*: "I made him with both of Mine" (not merely commanded him) and
"both Mine are open" (not chained, not single). The dual is less about
*what God has* and more about *how completely He acts*.

## 5. Pharaoh's Threat: Hands and Feet *min khilāf*

The construct *min khilāf* ("from opposite sides") occurs 4 times:

- **Q 5:33** — the *ḥirāba* (banditry) verse: bandits may be killed, or
  crucified, or have "hands and feet severed from opposite sides" (i.e.,
  right hand and left foot), or be exiled.
- **Q 7:124** — Pharaoh to his sorcerer-converts: *"la-uqaṭṭiʿanna aydiyakum
  wa-arjulakum min khilāfin thumma la-uṣallibannakum ajmaʿīn"* — I will cut
  your hands and feet from opposite sides, then crucify you all.
- **Q 20:71** — Pharaoh, same threat, with expansion: *"thumma la-uṣallibannakum
  fī judhūʿi n-nakhli"* (crucify you on palm trunks).
- **Q 26:49** — Pharaoh, same threat.

The three Pharaoh instances form a textbook *mutashābih lafẓī* (similar-in-
wording) triad: the core clause *la-uqaṭṭiʿanna aydiyakum wa-arjulakum
min khilāfin* is **word-for-word identical** in Q 7:124, Q 20:71, Q 26:49.
The variation is in the surrounding clauses:

| | 7:124 | 20:71 | 26:49 |
|---|---|---|---|
| "then I will crucify you all" | ✓ | — | ✓ |
| "…on the palm trunks" | — | ✓ | — |
| "so you will know which of us is severer" | — | ✓ | — |
| "Do you believe before I permit?" | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

The doubling is significant: Pharaoh's threat is canonised **three times**,
with the body-part clause fixed and the surrounding detail modulated. This
is a structural hallmark of *qiṣṣa* (story) repetition in the Quran: the
central emotive line is preserved; peripheral description is varied.

The intertextual echo with Q 5:33 (the *ḥirāba* penalty) is striking:
the same amputation formula that Pharaoh used as *tyrannical terror against
believers* reappears in Medinan law as *legal sanction against those who
wage war against God and His Messenger*. The formula *aydī + arjul +
khilāf* thus carries a deliberately charged intertext: the legal form is
inherited from the rhetorical form of Pharaonic menace, but inverted —
what was the tyrant's threat to the faithful becomes, in shariʿa, the
community's defence against disorder.

## 6. Dual Body Parts — Cross-Reference

The Quran marks the following paired body parts with explicit dual (*muthannā*)
morphology:

**Eyes (ʿaynān / ʿaynayn):** 7 duals — Q 12:84 (*ʿaynāhu* — Jacob's eyes
whiten with grief), Q 15:88 (*lā tamuddanna ʿaynayka*), Q 18:28 (*wa-lā
taʿdu ʿaynāka*), Q 20:131 (parallel), Q 55:50 (*fīhimā ʿaynāni tajriyāni* —
two flowing springs), Q 55:66 (*fīhimā ʿaynāni naḍḍākhatān* — two gushing
springs), Q 90:8 (*a-lam najʿal lahu ʿaynayni* — "did We not make for him
two eyes?"). Note that Q 55:50, 55:66 trade on the *ʿayn* = "eye / spring"
double semantics: Paradise has *two eyes/springs* in each of the paired
gardens.

**Hands (yadān / yadayn):** dozens of duals, the majority in the idiom
*bayna yadayhi* ("before him" / "between his two hands" — Q 2:66, 2:97,
3:3, 5:46, 5:48, 7:57, 10:37, 21:28, 25:27, 27:63, 34:31, 36:9, 41:14,
46:12, 46:30, 72:27). Outside this idiom: Q 5:64 (divine dual), Q 18:57
*qaddamat yadāhu* (what his two hands have advanced), Q 22:10, Q 25:27
(biting the two hands in regret), Q 36:35 (*ʿamilathu aydīhim*, "what their
hands worked" — plural referring to collectivity).

**Feet (rijlān / rijlayn):** Oddly, the Quran does not use an anatomical
dual *rijlān*. The dual *rajulāni* in Q 2:282, 5:23, 18:32, 28:15 refers
to *two men*. Feet are almost always plural *arjul* — used in pairings with
hands (*aydiyakum wa-arjulakum* — wuḍūʾ in Q 5:6; punishment in Q 5:33,
7:124, 20:71, 26:49, 60:12; testimony in Q 24:24, 36:65). This asymmetry
is not linguistically accidental: in Quranic rhetoric, *feet* always pair
with *hands* as the surface of action (doing + walking = the sum of bodily
deed), and the collective *aydī-arjul* serves the wuḍūʾ rite, the *ḥirāba*
penalty, the Pharaoh-triad, and the Day-of-testimony — all of them moments
where the body is *summed*, not halved.

**Ears (udhnān / udhnayn):** only one dual (Q 31:7 *kaʾanna fī udhunayhi
waqrā* — "as if there were a heaviness in his two ears," of the
impenitent). Every other *udhun* appearance is in the plural *ādhān*
(12×) — because the addressee is collective: "those who have ears but do
not hear."

**Lips (shafatān):** Q 90:9 hapax — *wa-lisānan wa-shafatayni* ("and a
tongue, and two lips"). The only dual occurrence of this root; the only
mention of lips in the Quran.

**Kneeling (jāthiya):** three tokens. The most salient is Q 45:28, which
gives Surah al-Jāthiya its name: *tarā kulla ummatin jāthiyatan* ("you
will see every community kneeling" on Judgement). Cognate occurrences in
Q 19:68, 19:72 — the jinn and humans *kneeling* around Hell. Kneeling here
is corporate and eschatological; it is not the hapax-knee-noun the brief
suggested. There is no *rukba* noun in the Quran.

## 7. *Qulūb fī ṣ-ṣudūr* — Nesting in Q 22:46

Q 22:46 is the locus classicus of the Quran's "heart-in-chest" formula:

> *fa-innahā lā taʿmā l-abṣāru wa-lākin taʿmā l-qulūbu llatī fī ṣ-ṣudūr.*
> "Eyes are not blinded; rather, the hearts that are in the chests are
> blinded."

This is the strongest grammatical marker of the *qalb-ṣadr* nesting in the
Quran. The phrase *allatī fī ṣ-ṣudūr* ("which are in the chests") is
grammatically *redundant* — of course hearts are in chests. The redundancy
is the point: it *anatomises* a metaphor. If "heart" here meant only
"intellect" or "faculty of judgement" (as Aristotle's translators
sometimes had it), the anatomical locator would be unnecessary. The verse
insists: the real, located, *thoracic* heart is what is blinded. This
places Q 22:46 in direct dialogue with Q 16:78 (*jaʿala lakum-us-samʿa
wal-abṣāra wal-afʾidata* — "He made for you hearing, sight, and
hearts"), where the interior faculty is listed alongside the exterior
sensory organs.

The same formula *allatī fī ṣ-ṣudūr* occurs at Q 22:46 and recurs at Q
40:19, 57:6, and in anticipatory form at Q 59:13 (*in your chests*). It
is the Quran's signature anti-Platonist move: the soul is not *above* the
body but *within* it, and its chief organ is *thoracic*.

The memo `phase-c-structures/qalb-theology.md` already develops the full
qalb theology (168 root-tokens, 132 noun-tokens, correlations with *ṣadr*,
*fuʾād*, *lubb*). Two additional points specific to this body-parts
analysis:

**(a)** *Ṣadr* (44 noun-tokens) is the Quran's preferred vessel-noun. Allah
*opens* chests (Q 6:125, 7:43, 94:1), *removes envy* from chests (Q 15:47,
7:43), *knows what is in chests* (Q 11:5, 42:24, 57:6). The chest is the
*container of hiddenness*; the heart (qalb) is its *contents*. The two
nouns operate in a strict container-contents pair that mirrors the outer
*julūd*-as-surface / inner faculties nesting from §3.

**(b)** Q 22:46 completes the sensory-circuit: earlier in the same verse,
the text speaks of hearts "that reason" (*qulūbun yaʿqilūna bihā*) and
ears "that hear" (*ādhānun yasmaʿūna bihā*). The final clause redirects
blindness to *hearts-in-chests*. The whole verse thus lists: travel (legs
implicit in *yasīrū fī l-arḍ*), ears, hearts, and eyes — four bodily
sensors, with the heart pointedly anatomised. This is a one-verse
compendium of Quranic embodiment.

## Summary

The Quran's twenty anatomical nouns carry theological weight disproportionate
to their frequency. The face is the meeting-point of submission (human side)
and object-of-seeking (divine side). The hand is quantitatively dominant
(120) but concentrates theologically in two dual constructs (Q 38:75, 5:64).
The skin becomes eschatological witness (Q 41:20–22) by inversion of its
ordinary function of concealment. Pharaoh's threat-triad fixes *aydī-arjul-
khilāf* as a mutashābih formula later inverted in the ḥirāba legislation.
Dual morphology tracks paired organs, with feet as the notable exception
(they pair *lexically* with hands instead). And the *qulūb fī ṣ-ṣudūr*
nesting of Q 22:46 anatomises what might otherwise be purely psychological:
the heart is not only a faculty but a located thoracic organ.

Across all seven focal points, a single grammar emerges: the Quranic body
is *relational* — each part is defined by what it faces, bears, testifies
about, or contains — never by what it is in itself. The body parts are
less organs than *stations* where the human meets, hides from, or is
exposed to God.
