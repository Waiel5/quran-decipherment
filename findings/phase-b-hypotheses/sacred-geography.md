# Sacred Geography of the Quran

## 0. Thesis

The Quran names very few places. That restraint is itself the datum.
Where a modern gazetteer runs to tens of thousands of toponyms, the
entire Quranic place-list fits on a single page. Every named place is
therefore theologically loaded: it has been *selected* out of the whole
ancient-Near-East map to carry a meaning. Mecca is the sacred city,
Medina the community, Sinai the revelation-mountain, Jerusalem the
prophets' meeting-place, Egypt the exile-and-magic-empire, Babel the
hubris, Madyan the borderland-of-repentance, al-Ahqaf / al-Hijr / Iram
the ruins-of-pride. The Quran's geography is theology in space.

This document catalogs every named place with verified morphological
occurrences, ranks them by frequency, cross-references them against
prophet pericopes, and reads the resulting map.

city par excellence of Islam is therefore *named* exactly four times.
Medina by its post-Hijra name appears four times; by its pre-Islamic
name (Yathrib) exactly once. The Masjid al-Aqṣā is named once. The
Masjid al-Ḥarām is named fifteen times. These are the raw data points
around which a theology must account.

The single most striking feature of this table: **seventeen of the
Quran's named places are hapaxes** — they occur exactly once. The
entire geographic vocabulary is tilted toward uniqueness. Madyan and
al-Ṭūr top the list because Moses' biography requires repeated
reference to both; everything else is stingy. Mecca itself is a hapax
(Makka at 48:24), as is Jerusalem (al-Aqṣā at 17:1), as is Badr (3:123)
— the three decisive Muslim sites — each named exactly once.

**Observation.** Moses has the largest named-geographic footprint in
the Quran — four distinct proper-nouns plus *al-arḍ al-muqaddasa* plus
*wādin muqaddas*. His arc literally *moves*: Egypt (exile) → flight →
Madyan (refuge) → Sinai (call) → wandering → Holy Land (promised but
refused: Q 5:21–26). Geography IS the Moses narrative. Contrast
Abraham, whose physical movements (Ur → Ḥarrān → Canaan) are entirely
elided in the Quran — no Ur, no Ḥarrān, only the destination (the
Bayt / Bakka). Contrast further Jesus, whose geography is almost
totally implicit; he is Son-of-Mary, not "of Nazareth" or "of
Bethlehem". And contrast Muḥammad, for whom the geography is
*current*: Makka → Yathrib → back to Makka, with battle-sites
(Badr, Ḥunayn) only as testimonial nodes.

The Quran thus has two geographic modes: (a) the *ruin-markers* — Iram,
al-Aḥqāf, al-Ḥijr, Bābil, al-Rass — named to be visited as warnings
(*fa-sīrū fī al-arḍ*); and (b) the *sacred axis* — al-Bayt, al-Masjid
al-Ḥarām, al-Masjid al-Aqṣā, al-Ṭūr, al-Arḍ al-Muqaddasa, Madyan — the
living theological stations.

## 4. The Isrāʾ / Miʿrāj geography (Q 17:1)

> *Subḥāna lladhī asrā bi-ʿabdihi laylan* **min al-masjid al-ḥarām**
> **ilā al-masjid al-aqṣā** *alladhī bāraknā ḥawlahu li-nuriyahu min
> āyātinā …*

"Glory be to the One who took His servant by night from the Sacred
Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, whose surroundings We have blessed…"

This single verse is the entire Quranic foundation of the Masjid
al-Aqṣā's sanctity. It is the **only** occurrence of the phrase
*al-masjid al-aqṣā* in the Quran. Three structural facts follow:

1. **The two holiest sites in Islam are bound together inside ONE
   verse.** Q 17:1 is the single textual hinge: Mecca at the start,
   Jerusalem at the end, linked by the verb *asrā* ("took by night").
   The Muslim architectural triangle — Haramayn + Aqṣā — derives
   from this verse and the hadith-developed Miʿrāj, not from multiple
   Quranic attestations.
2. **The adjectival logic of al-aqṣā.** The root `qSw` means
   "remotest, farthest". *The farthest mosque* is defined
   **relative to** the speaker's position — i.e., to Mecca. The
   geography is hodological (path-based), not cartographic. The
   comparative form encodes the Night Journey's distance-traversal
   as the very name of its destination.
3. **The three occurrences of the adjective *aqṣā* in the Quran.**
   17:1 (the mosque), 28:20 (a man from *aqṣā al-madīnati yasʿā* —
   the far side of the city, re the Moses-flight from Pharaoh's court),
   36:20 (the same phrase, re the People of the Town / Antioch
   pericope). In all three, *aqṣā* marks the edge-of-the-city from
   which deliverance or warning arrives. Jerusalem is positioned in
   17:1 as the eschatological "edge" from which revelation returns to
   its starting-point (Mecca). The word choice is theological
   topology.

The rarity (1/1) of al-Aqṣā, when compared with the 15 occurrences of
al-Masjid al-Ḥarām, produces an asymmetry that classical exegesis
treats as a *sign* rather than a deficiency: Jerusalem is present
elsewhere through surrogates — *al-arḍ allatī bāraknā* (17:1 cont.,
21:71, 21:81, 34:18), *al-arḍ al-muqaddasa* (5:21) — but never again
under its mosque-name. The one occurrence is load-bearing.

## 5. Bakka vs Makka

| Form | Occurrences | Verse | Environment |
|------|-------------|-------|-------------|
| Makka | 1 | 48:24 | *bi-baṭn Makka* — "in the belly of Makka" — post-Hudaybiyya-treaty, historical-prose context |
| Bakka | 1 | 3:96 | *inna awwala baytin wuḍiʿa li-l-nāsi la-lladhī bi-Bakka* — "verily the first House set up for humankind is the one at Bakka" — primordial-cosmogonic context |

The B↔M interchange is a known Arabic phonological phenomenon
(labialnasal alternation: *lāzib* / *lāzim*; *Bakka* vs *Makka*
parallels Akkadian *bītu* vs Hebrew *bayit*). Classical *tafsīr*
(al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* ad 3:96; al-Zamakhsharī, *Kashshāf*;
al-Rāzī, *Tafsīr al-Kabīr*) treats Bakka as the older, ritual,
valley-sanctuary name of the site — possibly the Kaaba's immediate
vicinity — while Makka becomes the name of the wider city around it.
Some glosses derive Bakka from the root `bkk` "to crowd, to press
together" (the ritual crowding of pilgrims at the House), others from
`bky` "to weep" (humility at the sanctuary). The Quran's pairing is
deliberately chronological: Bakka at 3:96 is the **primordial** name
attached to *the first house*; Makka at 48:24 is the **historical**
name attached to a contemporary treaty. One verse looks back to Adam
and Abraham; the other looks at the present campaign. The B↔M
interchange is thus cosmological vs civic.

The epithet *Umm al-Qurā* ("mother of the towns", 6:92 and 42:7)
constitutes a third nomenclatural register: Mecca as **metropolis**
— literally *metro-polis*, mother-city. Three names (Bakka, Makka,
Umm al-Qurā) for one place, distributed across three theological
frames: primordial, historical, hierarchical.

## 6. Yathrib vs al-Madīna

Yathrib occurs exactly once, in the mouths of the *munāfiqūn*
(hypocrites) at the Battle of the Trench:

> *wa-idh qālat ṭāʾifatun minhum yā ahla Yathriba lā muqāma lakum*
> *fa-rjiʿū* (Q 33:13)

"And when a faction of them said: 'O people of Yathrib, there is no
standing-place for you, so return!'"

The name Yathrib is placed rhetorically — it is what the *enemies*
call the city. Islamic tradition (e.g. Bukhārī, *Kitāb al-Ḥajj*;
al-Samhūdī, *Wafāʾ al-Wafāʾ bi-Akhbār Dār al-Muṣṭafā*, 15th c.)
records that the Prophet renamed the city *Ṭayba* or *Ṭāba* ("the
good") and, in the Quran's own post-Hijra idiom, simply **al-Madīna**
("the city" — with the definite article and no other qualifier, as
if to say: the city, qua city). The four *al-Madīna* occurrences as
a proper noun are all late-Medinan (9:101, 9:120, 33:60, 63:8) and
none overlap with Yathrib-33:13 contextually; 33:13 mocks the name,
and the text then abandons it.

This is a documentable lexical *renaming*: pre-Islamic Yathrib (one
mention, framed as the hostile mouth) → Islamic al-Madīna (four
mentions, all affirmative civic context). The name-transition stages
the Hijra itself in language.

## 7. Mountains — topography of revelation

Three mountains figure in the Quran:

1. **al-Ṭūr** — 10 occurrences, all referring to Mount Sinai. The
   name appears in Moses-legislation contexts (2:63, 2:93 — "We
   raised above you the Mount"; 4:154, 7:171 implied), in the
   burning-bush scene (19:52, 20:80, 28:29, 28:46), and in oath
   formulae (52:1 *wa-l-ṭūri* — the opening oath of Sūrat al-Ṭūr;
   95:2 *wa-ṭūri sīnīna* — the second oath of Sūrat al-Tīn). Two
   specific modifiers appear: **Ṭūr Sīnāʾ** (23:20 — associated with
   the olive tree that yields oil for illumination) and **Ṭūr
   Sīnīn** (95:2 — in rhymed oath). Both are understood by classical
   commentators as variants of the same mountain.

2. **al-Jūdī** — 1 occurrence, 11:44, Noah's ark's resting-place
   ("and it settled on al-Jūdī"). Unlike the biblical Ararat (in
   Armenia/Urartu), al-Jūdī is traditionally located in the Jazīra
   (near Mosul/Cizre), which places the Quranic flood-aftermath in
   the Mesopotamian highlands, not the Caucasian. A conscious
   geographic divergence from Genesis.

3. **al-Ṣafā / al-Marwa** — 1 verse, 2:158. Two hills within Mecca,
   sacralized by the pilgrim's ritual running (*saʿy*) and tied to
   Hagar's search for water in the Abrahamic-Ishmaelite narrative.
   The verse *inna al-ṣafā wa-l-marwa min shaʿāʾiri llāh* makes
   them "signs of Allah" — domesticated ritual topography.

**Topography of revelation.** Each of the three mountains carries a
distinct theological function: Sinai = legislation, al-Jūdī =
post-deluge resumption of humanity, Ṣafā/Marwa = pilgrimage memory.
None is a generic mountain. The Quranic mountain is always
event-laden. Note further that the *unnamed* mountain at 7:143 —
"when his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, He levelled it" —
is deliberately anonymized at the moment of its maximum theophany,
as if to say: no mountain survives its naming into the presence of
God. When God is revealed, the mountain collapses and the name goes
with it.

## 8. Bodies of water

- **al-Baḥrayn** ("the two seas", dual form `baHorayni` from root
  `bHr`) — 5 occurrences, 18:60, 25:53, 27:61, 35:12, 55:19. Three
  of these (25:53, 27:61, 55:19) describe a cosmic phenomenon of
  **two seas meeting** — one sweet and palatable, the other salt
  and bitter — separated by a barrier (*barzakh*) that they cannot
  cross. One (18:60) is the *majmaʿ al-baḥrayn* ("junction of the
  two seas") where Moses meets al-Khiḍr. One (35:12) repeats the
  sweet/salt pair as an argument for divine design. The "two
  seas" motif is therefore both cosmological and narrative.

- **al-Yamm** ("the sea" — from Hebrew/Aramaic *yam*) — 7–8
  occurrences, all in Moses narratives: 7:136 (Pharaoh's army
  drowned), 20:39 (the infant Moses cast into the river), 20:78
  (the sea covers Pharaoh), 20:97 (the Golden Calf cast into the
  sea), 28:7, 28:40, 51:40. The word is a marked loanword — the
  standard Arabic word for sea is *baḥr* — and its deployment is
  Moses-specific. It may signal the Egyptian setting: *yamm* in
  Coptic / late Northwest-Semitic usage referred to both the Nile
  and the Sea of Reeds. The Quran preserves the regional lexeme.

The two water-terms therefore form a complementary pair: *al-baḥr*
(general seas, the two-seas cosmology) vs *al-yamm* (the Moses-sea,
drowning of Pharaoh, casting of the infant and of the Calf). Both
bodies are sites of passage and reversal.

## 9. Caves

- **al-Kahf** — 6 occurrences, all in Sūrat al-Kahf (18:9, 10, 11,
  16, 17, 25). The root `khf` appears ONLY in this surah; it is a
  root-hapax at the surah level. The cave is the sleeping-place of
  the Seven Sleepers (Aṣḥāb al-Kahf), who slept 309 years while
  their city persecuted faith. Al-Kahf is a *time-capsule* cave:
  space that suspends time.
- **al-Raqīm** — 1 occurrence, 18:9 only. The word's meaning is
  disputed: (a) an inscribed tablet describing the sleepers, (b) the
  name of the valley or the dog that accompanied them, (c) a
  place-name. Classical exegesis is split; the majority treat
  it as a secondary place-name associated with the cave. Either
  way it is a Quranic hapax toponym.
- **al-Ghār** — 1 occurrence, 9:40. The cave in which the Prophet
  and Abū Bakr hid from Meccan pursuers during the Hijra, near
  Mecca (Jabal Thawr in later tradition). The Quran does not name
  the mountain; it names only the cave-as-cave. A *flight* cave —
  the opposite function of Kahf's *suspension* cave: where Kahf
  holds time still, Ghār lets time pass until the pursuit dissolves.

**Two caves, two time-functions.** Al-Kahf = stopped clocks
(protagonists emerge centuries later); al-Ghār = moving clocks (the
danger passes). The Quran names exactly two caves and structures
them as opposites.

## 10. Houses of God

| Term | Referent | Occurrences | Notes |
|------|----------|-------------|-------|
| al-Bayt | the Kaaba | ~8 as definite-determinate PN | 2:125, 2:127, 2:158, 3:96, 3:97, 5:97, 8:35, 22:26, 22:29 etc. |
| al-Bayt al-Ḥarām | the Sacred House | 2 | 5:2, 5:97 |
| al-Bayt al-ʿAtīq | the Ancient House | 2 | 22:29, 22:33 |
| al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr | the Frequented House | 1 | 52:4 |
| al-Masjid al-Ḥarām | the Sacred Mosque | 15 | listed above |
| al-Masjid al-Aqṣā | the Farthest Mosque | 1 | 17:1 |

The Kaaba is designated by four distinct epithets — *al-Bayt*,
*al-Bayt al-Ḥarām*, *al-Bayt al-ʿAtīq* ("the ancient one"), and
*al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr* (in one reading the earthly Kaaba, in the
dominant exegetical reading the *heavenly* Kaaba mentioned in the
oath-opening of Sūrat al-Ṭūr, directly above its earthly twin).
This multi-epithet structure is unique to Mecca's sanctuary. No
other place has four names. The onomastic redundancy is itself a
theological marker: the House is too singular to take just one
name.

The verse Q 52:4, within the opening oath-cluster of Sūrat al-Ṭūr,
juxtaposes al-Ṭūr (oath 1) and al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr (oath 3):

> *wa-l-ṭūri* (52:1)
> *wa-kitābin masṭūrin fī raqqin manshūrin* (52:2-3)
> *wa-l-bayti l-maʿmūri* (52:4)
> *wa-l-saqfi l-marfūʿi* (52:5)
> *wa-l-baḥri l-masjūri* (52:6)

Mountain → book → house → roof → sea: the oath-series runs bottom-up
(mountain) then top-down (raised roof) then side-out (sea), tracing
a cosmological coordinate system. The House sits at the center.

## 11. Geographic triangles

### 11.1 The Prophet's triangle: Mecca – Medina – Jerusalem

- **Mecca**: 4 names (Makka 1×; Bakka 1×; Umm al-Qurā 2×) + al-Masjid
  al-Ḥarām 15× + al-Bayt family many times.
- **Medina**: al-Madīna 4× + Yathrib 1×.
- **Jerusalem**: al-Masjid al-Aqṣā 1× + indirect *al-arḍ* references.

The triangle is constructed in ONE verse (17:1, Mecca→Jerusalem) and
held together by the Prophet's biography (Mecca→Medina Hijra, and
the ʿUmra/Conquest return). It is theologically closed: every Muslim
prayer faces one corner (Mecca); every second-order prayer-direction
historically referenced another (Jerusalem — the first *qibla*, per
Q 2:142–144's abrogation); and the community's civic life is rooted
in the third (Medina).

### 11.2 The Moses quadrilateral: Egypt – Sinai – Madyan – Holy Land

- **Egypt (Miṣr)**: 5× — birthplace, exile, Pharaoh's court, Joseph's
  rise.
- **Sinai (al-Ṭūr)**: 10× — burning bush, tablets, covenant.
- **Madyan**: 10× — refuge, marriage, formation.
- **Holy Land (al-arḍ al-muqaddasa)**: 1× (5:21) — destination,
  refused.

This quadrilateral is the life-shape of Moses: born in Egypt, flees
to Madyan, called at Sinai, marches toward the Holy Land, dies in the
wilderness when the Children of Israel refuse entry. No other Quranic
prophet has a four-cornered geographic life. Muḥammad's is
three-cornered (Mecca–Medina–Jerusalem via night-journey); Abraham's
is, within the Quranic text, **zero-cornered** — purely the Bayt, with
no named origin city. The geometric gradient corresponds to
narrative-foregrounding gradient: Moses is the most narrated prophet,
with the most-populated place-list.

### 11.3 The ruin-cities arc (cautionary geography)

Iram → al-Aḥqāf → al-Ḥijr → Madyan → al-Ayka → al-Rass: a scatter
of ruined peoples distributed in a roughly Arabian-peninsular arc,
from south (Iram / ʿĀd in the southern sands) to northwest (Madyan /
the Aqaba-Ḥijāz border). The repetition of the *ahl al-X* formula
(*aṣḥāb al-Ḥijr*, *aṣḥāb al-Rass*, *aṣḥāb al-Aykah*, *aṣḥāb al-qaryah*)
turns these places into a warning-catalog. The theological function
of the catalog: *siyāḥah* (travel) = *ʿibrah* (warning) — "travel
through the land and see the end of those who denied" (6:11, 16:36,
27:69 etc.). Geography becomes didactic.

## 12. Classical prior art

- **Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī (d. 1229 CE)**, *Muʿjam al-Buldān* — 13th-c.
  six-volume geographical encyclopedia. Contains entries for every
  Quranic toponym (and thousands of others), with lexical, historical,
  and literary attestations. For Bakka vs Makka, Yāqūt cites both
  with cross-references; for al-Jūdī he gives the Jazīra location;
  for al-Aḥqāf the southern Arabian sand-country; for Iram the
  debated tribe/city identification. This remains the single most
  comprehensive pre-modern reference.
- **Al-Bakrī (d. 1094 CE)**, *Muʿjam mā Istaʿjam min Asmāʾ al-Bilād
  wa-l-Mawāḍiʿ* — specifically an alphabetic lexicon of place-names
  in the *ḥadīth* and early Arabic literature, often focused on
  Quranic and Jāhilī references.
- **Al-Harbī (d. 899 CE)**, *Kitāb al-Manāsik*, and later
  **Al-Fākihī** / **Al-Azraqī** (*Akhbār Makka*, 9th c.) — dedicated
  works on Meccan sacred geography.
- Modern: F. E. Peters, *Mecca: A Literary History of the Muslim
  Holy Land* (1994); R. Firestone, *Journeys in Holy Lands* (1990);
  U. Rubin, *The Eye of the Beholder* (1995).

## 13. Synthesis: geography as theology

The Quran contains roughly **29 distinct named geographical entities**
(places, mountains, waters, houses, caves, plains) with a combined
occurrence count of approximately **100 tokens** — against a corpus of
~78,000 words. Place-density is therefore roughly **0.13%** — extremely
low. Compare to the Hebrew Bible, where place-density by some estimates
runs >1%. The Quran is a text about God and humanity, not about
geography.

But the places it does name are *exactly* the places its theological
system needs:

1. A sacred center (Mecca, in 4 names).
2. A community (Medina, in 2 names).
3. A horizon (Jerusalem, once).
4. A revelation-mountain (Sinai, 10×).
5. An ark-mountain (al-Jūdī, once).
6. Twin ritual hills (Ṣafā, Marwa — once each).
7. A sea of cosmology (al-Baḥrayn) and a sea of liberation (al-Yamm).
8. Two caves — one of suspension (al-Kahf), one of flight (al-Ghār).
9. A quartet of Moses-stations (Egypt, Sinai, Madyan, Holy Land).
10. A ruin-arc (Iram, Aḥqāf, Ḥijr, Madyan, Ayka, Rass, Bābil).
11. A queen's southern empire (Sabaʾ).
12. Two battle-markers (Badr, Ḥunayn).
13. Two pilgrimage-plains (ʿArafāt, al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām).
14. An earthly house (al-Bayt) with a heavenly twin (al-Bayt
    al-Maʿmūr).

Nothing else. No Damascus, no Baghdad (which did not yet exist),
no Yemen (only Sabaʾ by name), no Persia (though alluded to as
*fārisū* in no named-place form), no Constantinople (only as
"the nearer land" in 30:2–3), no Rome. The Quran's map is a
sacral diagram, not a gazetteer. Its silences are as eloquent as
its inscriptions.

**The Isrāʾ verse (17:1) is the model for the whole map**: one
sentence, two mosques, everything between them blessed. The Quran
treats space the way it treats time — as covered by a divine
signature, not as empty extension. The 29 places ARE the signature.
