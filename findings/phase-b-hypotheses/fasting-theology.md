# Fasting Theology in the Qurʾān: A Root-Level and Legal-Narrative Mapping

Phase B hypothesis document. Root: √ṣ-w-m (Buckwalter: Swm). Tokens: 14.
Data source: `data/morphology/quranic-corpus-morphology-0.4.txt` and `quran-text/quran-min-tashkeel.json`.

---

## 1. Root Inventory and Morphological Profile

The root √ṣ-w-m occurs 14 times in 11 verses. Unlike roots of higher frequency (ṣ-l-w for prayer, ~99x; z-k-w for alms, ~60x), ṣ-w-m is comparatively rare, yet its tokens cluster with remarkable density in legislative passages. The distribution is:

- Q 2:183 (ṣiyām, N-nom)
- Q 2:184 (taṣūmū, V-impf)
- Q 2:185 (yaṣumhu, V-impf)
- Q 2:187 (ṣiyām, N-gen × 1, N-acc × 1)
- Q 2:196 (ṣiyām, N-indef-gen; ṣiyām, N-nom) — Hajj fidya
- Q 4:92 (ṣiyām, N-nom) — homicide kaffāra
- Q 5:89 (ṣiyām, N-nom) — oath kaffāra
- Q 5:95 (ṣiyām, N-indef-acc) — game-hunting-in-iḥrām kaffāra
- Q 19:26 (ṣawm, N-indef-acc) — Mary's vow (hapax masdar)
- Q 33:35 (ṣāʾimīn, N-acc-pcpl MP; ṣāʾimāt, N-acc-pcpl FP) — list of virtues
- Q 58:4 (ṣiyām, N-nom) — ẓihār kaffāra

A critical lexical observation: the Qurʾān uses two masdars. **Ṣiyām** (Form I verbal-noun, 10 tokens) is the institutional/regulative term. **Ṣawm** (Form I masdar, 1 token, 19:26) appears only in Mary's mouth. The distinction is not arbitrary. Classical lexicographers (Lisān al-ʿArab, Rāghib's Mufradāt) note that *ṣawm* is the older, broader abstention — including abstention from speech, motion, or any act — while *ṣiyām* by the time of revelation had specialized toward the legal fasting-from-food. The Qurʾān exploits the lexical split surgically: Mary's fast is not the Muslim fast and the text marks that with a deliberate archaism.

Verb distribution is thin: only two finite forms (2:184, 2:185), both Form I imperfect, both with Ramadan as their direct referent. The action-noun dominates because fasting is presented predominantly as a *ruling* (ḥukm), not an ongoing event.

## 2. The Core Legislation: Q 2:183–187

This five-verse block is the sole source of the Ramadan fast's legal architecture. Its internal progression is worth charting verse-by-verse.

**2:183** opens with the canonical legislative formula: *"Yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū kutiba ʿalaykumu l-ṣiyāmu kamā kutiba ʿalā lladhīna min qablikum laʿallakum tattaqūn"* — "O you who believe, fasting is prescribed upon you as it was prescribed upon those before you, that you may attain taqwā."

Three features are immediately load-bearing:

1. The passive *kutiba* (prescribed/written) places the command outside human agency — a scribal-divine metaphor consistent with *kitāb* as covenantal text.
2. The *kamā kutiba ʿalā lladhīna min qablikum* clause universalizes the practice. The Qurʾān does *not* specify which prior community — no mention of Jews, Christians, Sabeans, or pagan Arabs. The vagueness is theologically deliberate: fasting is presented as a pan-human religious instrument. Classical exegetes (Ṭabarī, Rāzī) adduce that the People of the Book fasted; Muqātil suggests from Adam onward; Qurṭubī notes the Arabs fasted ʿĀshūrāʾ.
3. The terminal clause *laʿallakum tattaqūn* identifies the telos: not purification, not penance, but *taqwā* — protective God-consciousness. Fasting is instrumental to a theological disposition.

**2:184** introduces the mechanics: *ayyāman maʿdūdāt* (numbered/counted days — vague at this stage), then the first set of exceptions: the sick, the traveller, and — most intriguingly — *"those who can (barely) bear it"* (*alladhīna yuṭīqūnahu*). For that third category, a *fidya* (ransom) is specified: feeding a *miskīn*. Tafsīr literature reads this verse together with v.185 as a progressive-revelation pair in which the *fidya* option was narrowed. The final clause, *"but that you fast is better for you, if you knew"* (*wa-an taṣūmū khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūn*), primes the reader for the tightening that follows.

**2:185** is structurally the pivot of the pericope. It identifies the month: *"Shahru Ramaḍāna lladhī unzila fīhi l-Qurʾān"* — "The month of Ramaḍān in which the Qurʾān was sent down." This is the only Qurʾānic naming of Ramadan. The verse then intensifies: *"So whoever of you witnesses the month, let him fast it"* (*fa-man shahida minkumu l-shahra fal-yaṣumhu*). The jussive *yaṣumhu* + the verb *shahida* (to witness, be present) converts optional days to the obligation keyed to physical presence in the month. Travellers and the ill retain their delay-option (*fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhar*) — a compassion-clause made explicit in the next clause: *"God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you"* (*yurīdu llāhu bikumu l-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu l-ʿusr*).

The verse closes with a triple purpose chain: *"that you may complete the number, that you may magnify God (li-tukabbirū llāha) for what He has guided you to, and that you may give thanks."* The *takbīr* reference is ritual anchoring: takbīrāt at the end of fasting (the ʿĪd takbīr) is scripturally grounded here.

**2:186** is syntactically intrusive. It breaks from the legal second-person plural into divine first-person singular: *"And when My servants ask you about Me — then indeed I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls Me."* This is a classical *iltifāt* (pronominal shift). The only verse in the Qurʾān in which "servants" is inflected as *ʿibādī* (My servants) occurring mid-legal-context. Its placement inside the fasting passage suggests a structural purpose: fasting's *telos* is *qurb* (nearness), and the verse inscribes the listener's prayer into the fasting block itself. The fasting legislation is thus not merely regulative but relational — God's nearness is declared while fasting is being explained.

**2:187** supplies the detail the previous verses deferred. Four rulings are delivered:

1. **Night-time permission for marital relations** (*uḥilla lakum laylata l-ṣiyāmi l-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum*) — explicitly revising an earlier stricter practice (the phrase *ʿalima llāhu annakum kuntum takhtānūna anfusakum* implies a retrospective clemency for those who had violated an earlier understanding).
2. **The reciprocal-garment metaphor** (*hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunn*) — a poetic insertion whose theological force is equality-of-entanglement.
3. **Eating and drinking permitted at night** *"until the white thread becomes distinguishable to you from the black thread of dawn"* (*ḥattā yatabayyana lakumu l-khayṭu l-abyaḍu mina l-khayṭi l-aswadi mina l-fajr*) — the most phenomenologically empirical time-marker in Qurʾānic law. *Min al-fajr* was, per the classical reports, added to prevent literalist misapplication (some Companions brought actual threads). The "dawn" here is true dawn (al-fajr al-ṣādiq), not the false dawn.
4. **Completion to night**: *"then complete the fast until the night"* (*thumma atimmū l-ṣiyāma ilā l-layl*). The fast window is thus dawn-to-sunset, defined ostensively rather than by clock hours.

The verse closes with the iʿtikāf clause: *"and do not approach them (sexually) while you are in iʿtikāf (retreat) in the mosques"* (*wa-lā tubāshirūhunna wa-antum ʿākifūna fī l-masājid*). Critically, iʿtikāf is assumed as an existing practice — the Qurʾān does not institute it; it negatively regulates an expected devotional retreat. The classical sunna places iʿtikāf in the last ten nights of Ramadan, seeking Laylat al-Qadr — which bridges this passage to Surah 97.

The passage closes: *"tilka ḥudūdu llāhi fa-lā taqrabūhā"* — "these are God's limits, do not come near them." The negative vocabulary of "do not come near" (*lā taqrabū*) rather than "do not transgress" (*lā taʿtadū*) marks the hudūd as zones with buffer — an asceticism of caution.

The 2:183 *laʿallakum tattaqūn* is echoed at 2:187's close by *laʿallahum yattaqūn* — a ring-composition bracket. The five-verse unit is sealed by taqwā on both sides.

## 3. Laylat al-Qadr (Q 97) and Laylat Mubāraka (Q 44:3)

Surah 97, *al-Qadr*, is five verses and ninety-odd words — one of the shortest suras. Its compression is inversely proportional to its theological cargo.

**97:1** — *"Innā anzalnāhu fī laylati l-qadr"*: "Indeed We sent it down in the Night of Qadr." The pronoun *-hu* has no antecedent in the surah itself; it refers ex-textually to the Qurʾān, making the surah itself a meta-text about the revelation-event.
**97:2** — *"Wa-mā adrāka mā laylatu l-qadr"*: the Qurʾānic interrogative formula introducing the sublime-unknowable.
**97:3** — *"Laylatu l-qadri khayrun min alfi shahr"*: the night is better than a thousand months. A thousand months ≈ 83.3 years, exceeding an average human lifespan. The hyperbolic equation frames a single night as an eschatologically weighted interval.
**97:4** — *"Tanazzalu l-malāʾikatu wa-l-rūḥu fīhā bi-idhni rabbihim min kulli amr"*: The imperfective *tanazzalu* (Form V, connoting gradual/iterative descent) replaces the expected perfective *nazalat*. Angels and "the Spirit" (al-Rūḥ — frequently identified with Gabriel or a supra-angelic being) descend *bi-idhni rabbihim* (by their Lord's permission) *min kulli amr* — ambiguously "for every matter" or "with/from every decree."
**97:5** — *"Salāmun hiya ḥattā maṭlaʿi l-fajr"*: "Peace she is until the rising of the dawn." The feminine pronoun *hiya* refers to the night. The terminus — *maṭlaʿ al-fajr* — matches the dawn marker of 2:187. Laylat al-Qadr thus shares its temporal boundary with the daily fast: both end at *fajr*, creating an exegetical link between the micro-ritual of fasting and the macro-event of revelation. Every fast-dawn is a typological echo of the dawn that closed Laylat al-Qadr.

**Q 44:3–6** parallels Surah 97 thematically. *"Innā anzalnāhu fī laylatin mubārakah"* (44:3) echoes *innā anzalnāhu fī laylati l-qadr* (97:1) with near-identical syntax. 44:4 explicates: *"fīhā yufraqu kullu amrin ḥakīm"* — "in it every wise matter is distinguished/apportioned" — which semantically converges with 97:4's *min kulli amr*. The root *f-r-q* (to distinguish/apportion) supplies the "qadr" (measuring/decreeing) content that Surah 97 names but does not unpack. The dominant classical identification — Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, Ibn Kathīr — treats Laylat Mubāraka (44:3) and Laylat al-Qadr (97:1) as the same night, interpreted respectively from the side of blessing and from the side of decree. A minority (Ikrima) identifies Laylat Mubāraka with the Night of 15 Shaʿbān (Laylat al-Barāʾa), producing a double-night typology, but the Qurʾān's internal lexical echoes favour identification.

The connection with fasting: Ramadan is the month in which the Qurʾān descended (2:185); Laylat al-Qadr falls within Ramadan (universal Muslim position grounded in the 2:185 / 97:1 combined reading). Iʿtikāf in the last ten days (2:187's unregulated practice) is the ritual response to seeking this night. The fasting legislation and the Qadr revelation form a single conceptual cell: the ascetic discipline (fast) frames the eschatological window (Qadr).

## 4. Fasting as Kaffāra (Expiation)

Four distinct expiatory fasting-prescriptions appear, each tiered within a ransom-hierarchy. Their comparative anatomy is revealing.

**Homicide (Q 4:92)**: unintentional killing of a believer requires (i) manumission of a believing slave + (ii) blood-money (*diyya*) to the family. When unable (*fa-man lam yajid*): two consecutive months of fasting (*fa-ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayn*), framed as *tawbatan mina llāh* — "as repentance from God." Two-month consecutive fast marks homicide as the gravest of expiable offences.

**Oath-breaking (Q 5:89)**: the broken deliberate oath triggers tiered kaffāra — (i) feeding ten needy, or (ii) clothing ten, or (iii) manumission of one. When unable: *ṣiyāmu thalāthati ayyām* — three days of fasting. The triad of direct options + a fasting fallback makes this the lightest kaffāra tier.

**Game-hunting in iḥrām (Q 5:95)**: killing game while in pilgrim-sanctity. Restitution: (i) a comparable domestic-animal offering, or (ii) feeding the needy (*kaffāratun ṭaʿāmu masākīn*), or (iii) *ʿadlu dhālika ṣiyāman* — "the equivalent thereof in fasting." The measure-equivalence construction (*ʿadlu dhālika*) is unique to this verse: fasting calibrated to food-equivalent count. The purpose is explicitly retributive: *li-yadhūqa wabāla amrihi* — "that he may taste the gravity of his conduct."

**Ẓihār (Q 58:4)**: the pre-Islamic formula by which a husband treated his wife "as the back" of his mother (an irrevocable declaration of un-relation). The Qurʾān revokes the juridical force of ẓihār (58:2–3) and imposes a tiered kaffāra: (i) manumission of a slave before resumption, (ii) *ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayn* (two consecutive months) if unable, (iii) feeding sixty needy if still unable. Two-month consecutive fast here parallels homicide kaffāra — marking ẓihār as rhetorically grave enough to equate (in kaffāra) with unintentional manslaughter.

**Hajj fidya/tamattuʿ (Q 2:196)**: not strictly kaffāra for a sin, but ransom for hindrance or for performing tamattuʿ: three days fasting during Hajj + seven on return = ten days.

Comparative anatomy:

| Offence | Severity Proxy | Fast Duration | Substitutes for |
|---------|----------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Homicide (unintentional) | Highest | 2 months consecutive | slave + diyya |
| Ẓihār | High | 2 months consecutive | slave |
| Hunting-in-iḥrām | Medium | equivalent in days | offering/feeding |
| Hajj tamattuʿ | Ritual | 10 days (3+7) | sacrificial animal |
| Oath-breaking | Lightest | 3 days | feeding/clothing/manumission |

The architecture: fasting functions as the *universally available* substitute, the penance that requires no asset. It is the equaliser in the kaffāra economy — the poor can always expiate, where the rich must first exhaust their material options. Duration tracks moral weight. The Qurʾān thus positions fasting as both devotional (Ramadan) and juridical (kaffāra), but in neither register does it retain a purely ascetic function: fasting either attains taqwā or re-balances a moral breach.

## 5. Mary's Fast: Ṣawm as Speech-Abstention (Q 19:26)

*"Fa-kulī wa-shrabī wa-qarrī ʿaynā. Fa-immā tarayinna mina l-bashari aḥadan fa-qūlī innī nadhartu li-l-raḥmāni ṣawman fa-lan ukallima l-yawma insiyyā."*

"So eat and drink and cool your eye. And if you see any human, say: 'I have vowed to the All-Merciful a fast, so I will not speak today to any human.'"

Four features make this verse the most theologically interesting ṣawm-token in the corpus:

**1. The lexical marker.** This is the *only* verse using *ṣawm* rather than *ṣiyām*, and the only verse where the root attaches to abstention from something other than food. The semantic range of *ṣawm* in pre-Islamic Arabic included abstention from motion (of horses), from speech, from wind (a still day was called *yawmu ṣawmin*). The Qurʾān here activates the older range — and the preceding imperative *kulī wa-shrabī* ("eat and drink") makes explicit that food and drink are *not* what she abstains from. Mary eats, drinks, and fasts — an apparent contradiction that the lexical archaism resolves.

**2. The dedication formula.** *Nadhartu li-l-raḥmāni ṣawman* — "I have vowed to the Merciful a fast." This is the *only* fasting-token explicitly dedicated (via *li-* construction) to a specific divine name. And the name chosen is *al-Raḥmān*, not Allāh or al-Rabb. Al-Raḥmān is the Meccan-period name most associated with primordial mercy and the Throne. Mary's fast is thus positioned as a pre-Islamic archetype of the fast under mercy — a genealogical precedent for the community's later Ramadan fast (which is also framed under ease, *yusr*, 2:185).

**3. The pragmatic function.** The fast is a conversational alibi — silence is the answer to the scandal of a virgin with an infant. Fasting here is communicative protection: she will let the miracle-child speak for her (19:29–33). The fast institutes a speaker substitution: Mary's voice ceases so that the infant-ʿIsā's first utterance can be the revelation of his own prophetic identity.

**4. The canonical parallel.** The Ramadan fast (2:183) is prescribed *kamā kutiba ʿalā lladhīna min qablikum* — "as it was prescribed upon those before you." Mary's speech-fast is one such prior form — unambiguously so, since she precedes Muḥammad. The Qurʾān thus bracket-identifies: fasting is a pan-temporal category, of which food-abstinence is one manifestation and speech-abstinence another. The Muslim Ramadan fast is not sui generis; it is a particular inflection of a trans-communal ascetic practice.

## 6. Prior Communities and the Universality Thesis (Q 2:183)

The *kamā kutiba ʿalā lladhīna min qablikum* clause is striking for what it does *not* say. Unlike Q 5:44–47 (where specific peoples are named with their scriptures), 2:183 leaves "those before you" undefined. Three interpretive lines have been taken:

- **Maximalist**: all prior prophetic communities fasted; the universality is total.
- **Ahl al-Kitāb only**: Jews and Christians fasted (Yom Kippur, the Lenten fast).
- **Arab-pagan continuity**: the Arabs' ʿĀshūrāʾ fast is absorbed and superseded.

The Qurʾānic silence on specification suggests the maximalist frame is intentional: fasting is constituted as a universal religious practice — a claim about the phenomenology of religion itself, not a legal genealogy. This dovetails with 19:26: Mary is *also* in the "those before you" category, and her fast is the Qurʾān's own illustration of prior-community fasting.

## 7. Ṣāʾimīn / Ṣāʾimāt (Q 33:35): Fasting as Identity

Q 33:35 lists ten paired virtuous categories (masculine/feminine), culminating in a divine promise of *maghfira* and *ajr ʿaẓīm*. The pair *al-ṣāʾimīna wa-l-ṣāʾimāt* sits eighth in the list, between *al-mutaṣaddiqīn* (givers of alms) and *al-ḥāfiẓīn li-furūjihim* (guardians of chastity) — i.e. between material ascetic practice and bodily ascetic practice. The active participle converts fasting from an act (ṣiyām) or event (taṣūmū) into a stable personal attribute (ṣāʾim — "one who fasts"). The list is the Qurʾān's most explicit enumeration of piety-markers, and fasting is the only major ritual-category besides prayer (implicit in *al-qānitīn*) and almsgiving to earn an identity-form in this catalog. Fasting is thus liturgical, juridical *and* identity-constitutive.

## 8. Summary of Findings

The root √ṣ-w-m, despite only 14 tokens, anchors a theological complex of exceptional density. Five interlocking claims emerge:

1. **Lexical bifurcation** (ṣiyām/ṣawm) encodes a typological split between institutional-legal fasting and personal-votive fasting, with the Maryam hapax preserving the older form for a non-food fast.
2. **Q 2:183–187** is ring-composed on *taqwā* and progresses through five legal layers: prescription → exception → identification (Ramadan) → nearness (ʿibādī) → time-boundary and iʿtikāf.
3. **Laylat al-Qadr (Surah 97) and Laylat Mubāraka (Q 44:3)** co-refer. Both end at *maṭlaʿ al-fajr* / *al-fajr*, producing a structural rhyme with the daily fasting dawn.
4. **Kaffāra-fasting** is ordered by moral gravity: 2 consecutive months (homicide, ẓihār) > 10 days (tamattuʿ) > equivalent-count (hunting) > 3 days (oath). Fasting is the kaffāra-of-last-resort — the penance available to all regardless of asset.
5. **Mary's ṣawm li-l-Raḥmān** provides the only dedicated fast and the only speech-fast — a pre-Islamic precedent authorizing the "as prescribed upon those before you" clause.

Fasting in the Qurʾān is thus neither simply pious practice nor simply law: it is a typological category that ranges over time, speech, food, and atonement, bound together by the grammatical discipline of abstention for the sake of a relation (with God, with the harmed, with the self).

## 400-Word Summary

Fasting in the Qurʾān is carried by the root √ṣ-w-m in fourteen occurrences across eleven verses — a surprisingly small textual footprint that nevertheless supports a dense theological architecture. The root splits lexically into two masdars: *ṣiyām* (ten tokens), used for prescribed and expiatory fasting, and *ṣawm* (one hapax at 19:26), reserved for Mary's vow. This split is not stylistic; it encodes a typological distinction between institutional legal fasting and personal votive fasting, with the older form *ṣawm* activated precisely where the fast is not from food.

The core legislation sits in Q 2:183–187, a five-verse pericope ring-composed on *taqwā*. Verse 183 prescribes fasting as universal ("as it was prescribed upon those before you"), leaving the prior communities deliberately unspecified to claim trans-communal status for the practice. Verse 184 presents a lighter initial ruling with a ransom-option; verse 185 tightens the obligation by naming Ramadan as the month of the Qurʾān's descent, exempting only the sick and the traveller. Verse 186 intrudes with divine first-person speech ("I am near") — an *iltifāt* that embeds the theological *telos* of fasting (nearness) inside the legal code. Verse 187 supplies the empirical time-boundaries: the phenomenological "white thread from the black thread of dawn" for *imsāk*, sunset for *ifṭār*, and iʿtikāf as a pre-existing practice whose regulation (not institution) the Qurʾān performs.

Surah 97 (Laylat al-Qadr) and Q 44:3 (Laylat Mubāraka) are intra-Quranically identified through shared vocabulary (*amr*, descent-language) and share the fajr-terminus with the daily fast, binding micro-ritual and macro-event. The angel-descent is durative (*tanazzalu*, not *nazala*), and the night is ranked above 83 years.

Fasting also functions as universal kaffāra. Four durations are prescribed — two consecutive months for homicide (4:92) and ẓihār (58:4), equivalent-count for hunting-in-iḥrām (5:95), three days for oath-breaking (5:89) — with Hajj fidya at ten days (2:196). Fasting is always the last-tier substitute, the penance available to the poorest.

Mary's *ṣawm li-l-raḥmān* (19:26) is the only fast dedicated to a divine name, the only speech-fast, and the only fast preceded by "eat and drink." It is the Qurʾān's own demonstration of the 2:183 clause: a prior-community fast that the Muslim Ramadan fast extends and re-inflects.
