# Paradox Verses: Theologically Challenging Rhetoric in the Quran

**Phase B hypothesis document.** For each verse: Arabic, translation, classical scholar resolution (Rāzī / Zamakhsharī / Qurṭubī where available), modern scholarly treatment, and honest discussion of the residual tension. The goal is not apologetic smoothing but the mapping of where the Quran deliberately *deploys* theological difficulty as rhetorical instrument.

---

## 1. Did God "tire"? — Q 50:38

**Arabic.** وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِن لُّغُوبٍ

"We created the heavens and the earth and what is between them in six days, and no *lughūb* (lassitude / weariness) touched Us."

**Classical.** Rāzī (*Mafātīḥ al-ghayb*) treats 50:38 as explicitly anti-anthropomorphic. He notes that *lughūb* — a rare lexeme, appearing only three times in the Quran (35:35, 50:38 twice implicitly) — is chosen precisely because it is the word rabbinic commentary uses of divine Sabbath rest (*šāḇaṯ*, *nafaš*). Rāzī reads the verse as polemical: the Torah's "God rested on the seventh day" (Gen 2:2) is being corrected *by way of quotation* — the six-day creation is retained, the rest motif is negated. Zamakhsharī (*Kashshāf*) agrees and adds a Muʿtazilī note: fatigue is a defect (*naqṣ*), and the divine is by definition free of *naqṣ*; therefore the verse is not just anti-Jewish polemic but a general proof of *tanzīh* (transcendence). Qurṭubī (*Jāmiʿ*) cites Ibn ʿAbbās's transmission that a Jewish interlocutor in Medina said, "God created the world in six days and rested on the seventh," and this verse was revealed in response.

**Modern.** Gabriel Said Reynolds (*The Qurʾān and Its Biblical Subtext*, 2010) and Angelika Neuwirth (*Der Koran als Text der Spätantike*) treat 50:38 as a paradigm case of Quranic "rewriting" of late antique biblical lore — the six-day structure is preserved as a *common language*, but the Sabbath motif is polemically inverted. Sidney Griffith notes the verse presupposes an audience that knows the Genesis story.

**Tension.** The verse cannot be read as *merely* a statement about God. It is a statement *about* a prior text. If one accepts the Quran as purely self-originating revelation, the anti-rabbinic sharpness of the phrasing becomes puzzling; if one accepts the intertextual reading, the Quran is openly in dialogue with — and correcting — biblical literature. The classical tradition, remarkably, *embraces* this intertextual reading: Qurṭubī's asbāb al-nuzūl is openly polemical. The honest tension is thus not inside the verse but at the methodological meta-level: the Quran presents itself as self-sufficient yet operates inside a conversation whose other side is required for the verse to land.

## 2. Jesus's death — Q 3:55 vs Q 4:157–158

**Arabic (3:55).** إِذْ قَالَ اللَّهُ يَا عِيسَىٰ إِنِّي مُتَوَفِّيكَ وَرَافِعُكَ إِلَيَّ
"When God said: O Jesus, I am *mutawaffīka* and raising you to Me."

**Arabic (4:157–158).** وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَـٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ... بَل رَّفَعَهُ اللَّهُ إِلَيْهِ
"They did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them... rather, God raised him to Himself."

**Classical.** The lexical default of Form V *tawaffā* in the Quran is "to cause to die" (e.g. 4:15, 8:50, 32:11 — the angel of death *yatawaffākum*). Yet 3:55 is read by the majority of classical exegetes against its own lexical grain. Rāzī lists **six** readings in his *Mafātīḥ*: (i) *mutawaffīka* = "causing you to die a real death before raising you"; (ii) a sleep-like state (istidlāl from 6:60 *yatawaffākum bi-l-layl*); (iii) completing/receiving your life's term; (iv) inversion — i.e. *mutawaffīka* is logically later than *rāfiʿuka*, referring to the eschatological second death at the end of Jesus's second coming; (v) removing you from your enemies' reach; (vi) *tafwīḍ* — leaving the matter with God. Rāzī himself inclines to (iv), the inverted-order reading: God raises Jesus alive, he returns at the end of time, then dies. Zamakhsharī treats *mutawaffī* as metaphorical for "complete withdrawal" and pairs it with *rāfiʿ* as a hendiadys. Qurṭubī surveys all six positions and reports that Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan, and others held Jesus did die — for a period (traditions say three hours or seven) — before being raised.

**Modern.** Todd Lawson (*The Crucifixion and the Qur'an*, 2009) argues the Quran's position is deliberately *ambiguous* on the manner of Jesus's departure, emphasizing only the *negation of Jewish triumph* (they did not kill him), not the metaphysics of his state. Reynolds (2010) connects 4:157 to late-antique docetic Christologies (Basilides, Manichaean sources) that are documented in Syriac polemic. Suleiman Mourad has shown that the *raised-alive* consensus is a post-formative development; early tafsīr preserves the plurality.

**Tension.** The two verses are not contradictory but they pull in different directions:
- 3:55 uses a word whose plain sense is death.
- 4:157 denies the specific event of crucifixion-killing.
- 4:158 uses *rafaʿahu* ("raised him") which in classical cosmology implies bodily ascension while alive.
The dominant later Sunnī view (raised alive, will return, then die) is *textually possible* but requires reading against the plain sense of *mutawaffī*. The honest tension: the Quran's wording is strong enough to have sustained **centuries of opposing readings within the same orthodoxy** — the text does not legislate between them. This is theologically noteworthy: the Quran leaves a space where Christian claims are negated (no crucifixion-killing by Jews) but a positive replacement narrative is not fixed.

## 3. Predestination vs free will — Q 74:31 vs Q 18:29

**Arabic (74:31).** ...فَيُضِلُّ اللَّهُ مَن يَشَاءُ وَيَهْدِي مَن يَشَاءُ
"Thus God leads astray whom He wills and guides whom He wills."

**Arabic (18:29).** ...فَمَن شَاءَ فَلْيُؤْمِن وَمَن شَاءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ
"So whoever wills, let him believe; and whoever wills, let him disbelieve."

**Classical.** This is the central kalām fault line.
- **Muʿtazila** (Zamakhsharī): 18:29 is the base principle — humans have *qudra* (real power) over their acts. 74:31 is read as *God wills to guide* the one *who wills to be guided* (human willing logically primary; divine willing responds / ratifies). Zamakhsharī glosses *yushāʾu* via *al-luṭf* (the divine grace of providing the conditions of guidance) — God does not *compel* straying.
- **Ashʿarī** (Rāzī in his later period): 74:31 is the base principle — divine willing is unconditionally prior. 18:29 is *tahaddī* (a challenge / threat), not a grant of autonomy: "go ahead, try" — the human will is real but *kasb* (acquisition), not origination. Rāzī devotes long passages to refuting the Muʿtazilī reading, arguing that human willing is itself created by God.
- **Māturīdī**: a middle path — *mashīʾa* (willing) belongs to the human, but *qudra* (executive power) is created at the moment of the act.
Qurṭubī records all three and notes that *ahl al-sunna* (in his framing, Ashʿarī) reads 18:29 as rhetorical.

**Modern.** Daniel Gimaret (*Théories de l'acte humain en théologie musulmane*, 1980) and Richard Frank's studies of Ashʿarī theology show the kalām debate is genuine and unresolved. Fazlur Rahman (*Major Themes of the Quran*) argues the Quran holds both in productive tension and the attempt to systematize one at the expense of the other distorts the text. Montgomery Watt's *Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam* (1948) traced the issue to very early intra-Muslim disputes (Qadariyya vs Jabriyya) in the first Umayyad century.

**Tension.** The tension is real and acknowledged by the tradition itself. The Quran itself gestures at it: 81:28–29 places the two in successive verses — "for whoever of you wills to go straight / but you do not will unless God wills." The tradition has oscillated rather than resolved, which is itself a theological datum: the Quran's rhetorical strategy may be to *hold* the paradox rather than collapse it. Compare Paul in Romans 9 (potter and clay) vs Romans 10 (call on the name) — monotheisms of revelation tend to produce this doublet.

## 4. Why does God need permission? — Q 10:3

**Arabic.** ...يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ ۖ مَا مِن شَفِيعٍ إِلَّا مِن بَعْدِ إِذْنِهِ
"He manages the affair; there is no intercessor except after His permission."

**Classical.** Rāzī reads the verse as a double negation: against Meccan polytheists who held that their deities interceded *unconditionally* (so that the deities functioned as *co-sovereigns*), and against a reading in which God is *isolated* from the economy of intercession (so that the cosmos would be impersonal). The permission structure is thus **relational pedagogy**, not metaphysical necessity — God needs no permission from Himself; the permission-clause disciplines the *creature's* expectation. Zamakhsharī: *shafāʿa* is language borrowed from Arab political practice (notables interceding with a chief), and the verse negates the Arab presumption that their ancestors' status automatically secured intercession. Qurṭubī quotes Ibn ʿAbbās: the permission-clause makes every act of intercession a *gift* from God, not a *claim* upon God.

**Modern.** Toshihiko Izutsu (*God and Man in the Koran*) treats *idhn* (permission) as part of the Quran's *semantic field of sovereignty* — the term recurs in creation contexts (2:255, 10:3, 13:11) to emphasize that secondary causes operate by delegated authority. William Chittick describes it as the Quran's solution to the problem of providence: creation has real agency, but its agency is *granted*, not autonomous.

**Tension.** Logically, if God is *maximally* sovereign, the permission-clause is redundant. The force of the verse is not logical but *dramatic*: it stages a juridical scene in which intercessors exist but are subordinated. The honest tension: the Quran needs the category of "intercessor" to discipline pagan expectation, and this requires *populating* a realm (angels, prophets) that the strictest monotheism would abolish. The classical resolution — permission as pedagogical — works but concedes that the Quran's monotheism is *not* the austere negation of mediators but a *reconfiguration* of them.

## 5. The "perfected" religion — Q 5:3

**Arabic.** ...الْيَوْمَ أَكْمَلْتُ لَكُمْ دِينَكُمْ وَأَتْمَمْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعْمَتِي وَرَضِيتُ لَكُمُ الْإِسْلَامَ دِينًا
"Today I have perfected for you your religion, completed My favor upon you, and approved Islam as your religion."

**Classical.** Rāzī notes the tension with subsequent revelation: if the religion is *perfected* at the Farewell Pilgrimage, why do later *āyāt* (e.g. 2:281, traditionally the last revealed) follow? Answer: *ikmāl al-dīn* refers to the *completion of the legislative framework* (the core pillars: ḥajj had just been legislated in its final form), not the *closure of the revelation-act*. Zamakhsharī reads *akmaltu* as perfecting the *ritual and moral law*, and ties *raḍītu* (approval) to divine satisfaction with the community's state at that moment. Qurṭubī transmits a famous report that a Jewish questioner said to ʿUmar, "if this verse had been revealed to us, we would have taken that day as a festival" — ʿUmar replied, "we know the day and place of its revelation: it was a Friday, ʿArafa, during the Farewell." Shīʿī tafsīr (not classical Sunnī) ties *akmaltu* to the Ghadīr Khumm designation of ʿAlī.

**Modern.** Andrew Rippin and others in historical-critical mode note that "perfected" in the Quran means *complete for its purpose*, not *closed to further divine activity*; later Muslim theology extends it into a doctrine of the *finality of law*. Jonathan Brown discusses how this verse becomes a proof-text for the *closure of the canon*.

**Tension.** The verse makes a categorical claim that later Muslim jurisprudence has had to interpret flexibly to handle novel situations (banking, bioethics, digital life). If religion is *perfected*, how can there be *ijtihād*? Classical answer: perfection = perfection of *principles*, not of *case-law*. The tension is honest: the word *akmaltu* carries a stronger semantic charge than "I have provided you sufficient principles" — it implies *finished*, *whole*. Interpretive traditions have softened this to make room for ongoing jurisprudence.

## 6. Seven heavens — literal or metaphorical? — Q 67:3, 71:15, 78:12

**Arabic (67:3).** الَّذِي خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ طِبَاقًا
"He who created seven heavens in layers."

**Classical.** Rāzī, writing with access to Ptolemaic astronomy via Ibn Sīnā and the *falāsifa*, identifies the seven heavens with the seven celestial spheres of the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn — a harmonization with Greek astronomy. Zamakhsharī takes a more literal path: seven physically layered domes (*ṭibāq*). Qurṭubī reports hadith material (the *mi'rāj* narratives) describing the seven heavens as discrete *realms* inhabited by prophets, not astronomical spheres. Ibn ʿArabī and the Sufi tradition read the seven as *cosmic degrees of manifestation*, not physical strata.

**Modern.** Most modern Muslim scholars (e.g. Nidhal Guessoum, *Islam's Quantum Question*) argue that *seven heavens* must be read symbolically or as *kalām* language, not literal cosmology — modern astronomy has no seven-layer structure, and attempting to map the seven heavens onto e.g. the atmospheric layers (troposphere, stratosphere...) is, in Guessoum's view, concordist apologetics of the wrong kind. Historical-critical scholarship (Kevin van Bladel on Syriac cosmology, Reynolds) traces the seven-heavens motif to late-antique merkavah and Syriac Christian texts.

**Tension.** If read literally, the Quran's cosmology is late-antique, not scientific. If read metaphorically, one concedes that its cosmological language is *accommodated* to its original audience. Classical tradition already split on this (Rāzī's Ptolemaic harmonization is a concession to Greek learning; Qurṭubī's merkavah-style cosmology is not). The honest tension: maintaining *both* that the Quran is timeless and that its cosmological imagery is pre-modern requires interpretive flexibility that not all readers accept. Modern Muslim thought is genuinely divided.

## 7. The Satanic Verses tradition — Q 22:52

**Arabic.** وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا مِن قَبْلِكَ مِن رَّسُولٍ وَلَا نَبِيٍّ إِلَّا إِذَا تَمَنَّىٰ أَلْقَى الشَّيْطَانُ فِي أُمْنِيَّتِهِ فَيَنسَخُ اللَّهُ مَا يُلْقِي الشَّيْطَانُ ثُمَّ يُحْكِمُ اللَّهُ آيَاتِهِ
"We have not sent before you any messenger or prophet but that when he *recited / desired* (*tamannā*), Satan cast into his *recitation / desire* (*umniyya*); but God abrogates what Satan casts, then God confirms His verses."

**Classical.** al-Ṭabarī (d. 310/923) in his *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* reports the *gharānīq* tradition in multiple isnāds: the Prophet, reciting Sūrat al-Najm (53) and reaching "have you seen al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā and Manāt the third," had Satan cast on his tongue the words "these are the exalted *gharānīq* (cranes) whose intercession is to be hoped for." The Meccans prostrated with him. 22:52 was then revealed as consolation and explanation. Ibn Isḥāq, al-Wāqidī, and multiple early sources record this. The *classical* reception is mixed: al-Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ (d. 544/1149) in *al-Shifā* rejects the tradition on the grounds of *ʿiṣma al-anbiyāʾ* (prophetic inerrancy in transmission). Ibn Kathīr (d. 774/1373) rejects it as weakly transmitted. Rāzī rejects it decisively, treating 22:52 as a general principle about Satan's attempts to interfere with *all* prophetic reception, not a specific Meccan incident. Zamakhsharī (Muʿtazilī, who should be hostile to such a tradition) surprisingly allows a version of it, reading *tamannā* as "desire" (Satan inserting a desire in the Prophet's heart, not actual verses onto his tongue). Qurṭubī transmits the tradition and multiple objections.

**Modern.** Shahab Ahmed's posthumous *Before Orthodoxy: The Satanic Verses in Early Islam* (2017) is the definitive modern study: he documents that the tradition was *widely accepted* by the first three centuries of Muslim scholars and that its rejection is a specifically post-4th-century (ʿiṣma-driven) development. Uri Rubin, Patricia Crone, and others treat it as probably historical. Sean Anthony argues 22:52 was revealed specifically in response to *some* such incident. Salman Rushdie's 1988 novel made the tradition globally controversial, which further entrenched modern Muslim rejection.

**Tension.** If the tradition is authentic:
- The Quran itself (22:52) acknowledges *satanic interference in prophetic recitation*, though the verses were subsequently abrogated.
- The doctrine of *ʿiṣma* in its strong form is post-Qurʾānic and in tension with the plain reading of 22:52.
If the tradition is inauthentic:
- Why does 22:52 exist at all, in the form it takes? The verse seems to presuppose *some* such phenomenon.
The honest tension is that 22:52 is not a *denial* of satanic interference; it is a *framing* of it. Classical orthodoxy has moved toward reading 22:52 as minimal (Satan *tries* to interfere; God *always* corrects before any verse becomes canonical), but the Arabic *alqā* (cast) is strong and concrete.

## 8. Adam's sin — Q 20:121

**Arabic.** ...وَعَصَىٰ آدَمُ رَبَّهُ فَغَوَىٰ
"And Adam disobeyed his Lord and erred."

**Classical.** Rāzī faces a direct conflict with *ʿiṣma al-anbiyāʾ*. His resolutions: (i) Adam's *ʿiṣma* began with prophetic appointment, which postdates the Eden incident (*qabla al-nubuwwa*); (ii) *ʿaṣā* here means *tark al-awlā* (abandoning the more preferable), not sin proper; (iii) *ghawā* is reinterpreted from "erred / went astray" to "was disappointed / frustrated in his aim," since the Arabic verb has that secondary sense. Zamakhsharī is more direct: he reads the verse plainly — Adam disobeyed — and does not flinch. Qurṭubī compiles and surveys.

**Modern.** Amina Wadud and others note that the Quran's Adam story *lacks* the doctrine of original sin — Adam is forgiven in the text itself (2:37, 20:122) — and that the *ʿiṣma* doctrine is a theological overlay that creates the very tension it then has to resolve. Fazlur Rahman reads 20:121 as evidence the Quran presents prophets as fully human, including capable of error.

**Tension.** The plainest reading — Adam disobeyed — is theologically inconvenient for a later doctrine that demanded prophetic inerrancy. Classical tafsīr's solution is to introduce technical category distinctions (*tark al-awlā*, pre-prophethood). These are *available* readings of the Arabic but they are not the first readings. The honest tension: the Quran seems content with prophets who *ṣā*; the tradition is not.

## 9. Divorce waiting period — Q 2:228

**Arabic.** وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ
"Divorced women wait, for themselves, three *qurūʾ*."

**Classical.** The controversy is on *qurūʾ*: does it mean menstrual periods (*ḥayḍ*) or periods of purity between menstruation (*ṭuhr*)? This is a rare Arabic "contranym" (*aḍdād*).
- **Ḥanafī / Ḥanbalī**: *qurūʾ* = menstruation. The waiting period is thus ~3 full cycles.
- **Mālikī / Shāfiʿī**: *qurūʾ* = periods of purity. The waiting period is shorter (~1.5–2 months).
Rāzī surveys both in detail and inclines to the *ṭuhr* reading based on grammatical gender (*thalātha* as masculine-numbered noun aligns with *ṭuhr*, which is masculine, rather than *ḥayḍ*, which is feminine). Zamakhsharī follows the Ḥanafī side. Qurṭubī extensively presents both.

**Modern.** Kecia Ali (*Marriage and Slavery in Early Islam*) and Karen Bauer examine how the *qurūʾ* debate shaped Muslim family law. Modern Muslim family codes (Egyptian, Moroccan, etc.) typically settle the ambiguity by statute.

**Tension.** The verse is *lexically ambiguous* at the level of legal precision — unusual for legal material. Classical jurists could not agree, and the disagreement was consequential (timing of remarriage, paternity determination). The honest tension: the Quran is sometimes legally *less* specific than the juristic tradition would prefer, and classical jurists had to generate specificity from ambiguous text.

## 10. Marry up to four — Q 4:3 vs Q 4:129

**Arabic (4:3).** ...فَانكِحُوا مَا طَابَ لَكُم مِّنَ النِّسَاءِ مَثْنَىٰ وَثُلَاثَ وَرُبَاعَ ۖ فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا فَوَاحِدَةً
"Marry of women who please you — two, three, or four; but if you fear you will not be just, then one."

**Arabic (4:129).** وَلَن تَسْتَطِيعُوا أَن تَعْدِلُوا بَيْنَ النِّسَاءِ وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ
"You will never be able to be just between women, however much you try."

**Classical.** Classical jurists read 4:3 and 4:129 as governing *different kinds* of justice.
- 4:3: justice in *material* matters (lodging, nights, maintenance) — *achievable*, hence polygamy permitted conditionally.
- 4:129: justice in *inclination of the heart* — *unachievable*, hence not demanded.
Rāzī states this distinction explicitly and adds that 4:129 is followed by "do not incline wholly" — i.e. internal preference is pardoned, external equality is required. Zamakhsharī and Qurṭubī follow. No classical jurist reads 4:129 as abrogating 4:3.

**Modern.** Muḥammad ʿAbduh and Rashīd Riḍā (late 19th / early 20th c.) argued that 4:129 effectively *de-legislates* polygamy: since justice is impossible and 4:3 conditions polygamy on justice, the *realistic* application is monogamy. Fazlur Rahman supports this as the *teleological* reading. Amina Wadud (*Qur'an and Woman*) and asmaʾ barlas (*Believing Women in Islam*) read 4:3 in its asbāb context (orphan-girl justice) as a *narrowing*, not expansion, of pre-Islamic polygamy. Tunisian family code (1956) bans polygamy outright, citing this reading. Most other Muslim legal codes permit polygamy under conditions.

**Tension.** The surface contradiction (permit up to four / say you can't be just) is genuine, not contrived. Classical resolution partitions "justice" into achievable and unachievable components; modernist resolution reads the two verses together as implying preference for monogamy. Neither is forced by the text; both are *available*. The honest tension: the Quran's own rhetoric pulls *against* its own permission — an unusual legal structure.

## 11. Over it are nineteen — Q 74:30

**Arabic.** عَلَيْهَا تِسْعَةَ عَشَرَ
"Over it are nineteen."

**Classical.** The immediate referent (*ʿalayhā*) is *Saqar*, hell, mentioned in the preceding verses. Classical tafsīr reads the nineteen as the *angelic guards* of hell. 74:31 makes the verse's purpose explicit: "We have made their number only as a trial for those who disbelieve — that the People of the Book may be certain, and those who believe may increase in faith, and that neither those given the Book nor the believers may doubt." Rāzī notes the number generates anecdotes of early opposition: the Meccan Abū Jahl is said to have mocked, "muhammad's God has only nineteen angels; we are more than that." Zamakhsharī emphasizes the *sign-value*: the specific number is a *challenge* (*fitna*) — its role is to discriminate reactions, not to yield a theology of hellkeeper ratios. Qurṭubī compiles traditions.

**Modern.** Rashad Khalifa (*The Computer Speaks: God's Message to the World*, 1981) made 19 the anchor of a numerological code claiming the Quran is structured on multiples of 19. Khalifa's claims have been critiqued internally (forced counts, exclusions); Bilal Philips and others have refuted the code in detail. Mainstream academic Quranic studies (Andrew Rippin, Stefan Wild) treat the 19 as a late-antique numerical motif (compare the 19-year Metonic cycle; the number's significance in Bahāʾī and earlier esoteric traditions). The number *may* function as an apocalyptic sign whose force the original audience grasped more directly than we do.

**Tension.** The Quran *itself* declares the number is a *fitna* — a trial. Verse 74:31's purpose clause ("that the People of the Book may be certain") strongly suggests the original audience had a reason to recognize the number. Whether that reason was cosmological (19 stations of the divine throne in Jewish/Christian apocalyptic), calendrical (Metonic cycle), or something else is not fixed by the text. Khalifa's numerological use is textually thin; but the verse's own self-description as a *sign* invites *some* kind of semiotic reading.

## 12. The Throne Verse — Q 2:255

**Arabic.** ...وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ
"His *kursī* (throne / footstool) encompasses the heavens and the earth."

**Classical.** Two kalām problems: (i) is the *kursī* a real object? (ii) does *istiwā ʿalā al-ʿarsh* (God's "ascending / settling" on the throne, 20:5, 7:54, etc.) imply divine spatiality? On (i): Rāzī distinguishes *kursī* (footstool, smaller, created) from *ʿarsh* (throne, greater, created) — both are real created entities but do not *contain* God. On (ii), the classical positions are:
- **Mujassima / Ḥanbalī literalist**: God sits on the throne in a real-but-unspecified manner.
- **Ashʿarī**: *bilā kayf* ("without asking how") — affirm the text, negate the modality.
- **Muʿtazilī / philosophical**: *istiwā* is metaphorical for sovereignty / dominion.
Imām Mālik's famous line: "*istiwāʾ* is known, the *how* is unknown, belief in it is obligatory, and asking about it is innovation." Zamakhsharī (Muʿtazilī) is decisive: *kursī* encompassing heavens and earth is *metaphor for limitless dominion*. Qurṭubī catalogs positions.

**Modern.** Bin Bāz and Salafī discourse leans toward literalism-with-*bilā-kayf*. Academic scholarship (Binyamin Abrahamov's *Anthropomorphism and Interpretation*) traces the debate as a central axis of Islamic theology.

**Tension.** The Quran uses *bodily* language of God (hands, face, throne, descent). The theology of absolute transcendence (*laysa ka-mithlihi shayʾ*, 42:11) pulls the other way. Classical Sunnī orthodoxy settled on *bilā kayf* as a *procedural* refusal to resolve — affirm the word, refuse the picture. The honest tension: the Quran speaks of God in a *register* that requires constant interpretive caution; *ta'wīl* (figurative) and *tafwīḍ* (deferring meaning) are both live options and classical scholars chose different ones.

## 13. God "finds" Job patient — Q 38:44

**Arabic.** ...إِنَّا وَجَدْنَاهُ صَابِرًا ۚ نِّعْمَ الْعَبْدُ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ
"Indeed, We found him patient — what an excellent servant, truly he turns [to God]."

**Classical.** The problem: *wajadnāhu* (we found him) implies *discovery*, which presupposes *non-prior-knowledge* — but God is omniscient. Rāzī addresses directly: *wajada* here is *idiom of approval*, not discovery. Analogous to Arabic idioms where "we found X praiseworthy" = "X proved to be praiseworthy (to Us)." The verb marks *relational assessment*, not epistemic update. Zamakhsharī: *wajada* = "we attested him to be" — a *declaration*, not a *learning*. Qurṭubī collects.

**Modern.** Commentators like Muhammad Asad translate accordingly ("We found him full of patience") and gloss in notes.

**Tension.** Rāzī's solution works — Arabic *wajada* has this semantic range — but the phrasing *does* sit in the Quran alongside verses that seem to imply testing as *revelation of what humans do* (e.g. 47:31: "We shall certainly test you until We *know* — *ḥattā naʿlama* — who strive and are patient"). The *ḥattā naʿlama* language is more directly problematic than *wajadnāhu*. Classical tafsīr resolves these too (divine *ʿilm* is pre-eternal; the "knowing" language refers to *manifestation* — making known to others, or actualizing the known). The honest tension: the Quran speaks of divine knowledge in a *dynamic* register (testing, finding, revealing) alongside a *static* register (preserved tablet, *lawḥ maḥfūẓ*). The tradition harmonizes but the surface tension is real.

## 14. "Do not say 'uff' to your parents" — Q 17:23

**Arabic.** ...فَلَا تَقُل لَّهُمَا أُفٍّ وَلَا تَنْهَرْهُمَا وَقُل لَّهُمَا قَوْلًا كَرِيمًا
"Do not say to them *uff* nor chide them, but speak to them a generous word."

**Classical.** *Uff* is the smallest emotive utterance in Arabic — a syllable of mild irritation. Rāzī uses this verse as a paradigm of *minimum from which maximum follows* (*qiyās al-awlā* — "a fortiori reasoning"): if even *uff* is forbidden, every stronger negative expression is forbidden *a fortiori*. The verse is the *canonical* proof-text in *uṣūl al-fiqh* for this inferential mode. Zamakhsharī treats *uff* as the minimal admissible sign of impatience. Qurṭubī compiles legal applications.

**Modern.** Widely cited in Muslim family ethics. Linguistically: *uff* appears three times in the Quran (17:23, 21:67, 46:17), always marking minimal negative affect.

**Tension.** Not theologically problematic in itself; included here because it is the classical tradition's *paradigm* for rhetorical minimality as legal maximality. The verse trains a reading practice: *below the surface* of a small word is a *rule* encompassing much more.

## 15. Why forbid what God permitted? — Q 66:1 and the Maria al-Qibṭiyya incident

**Arabic.** يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ ۖ تَبْتَغِي مَرْضَاتَ أَزْوَاجِكَ
"O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has permitted you, seeking the approval of your wives?"

**Classical asbāb al-nuzūl.** Two main traditions, both in Ṭabarī and Qurṭubī:
- **Honey incident**: the Prophet drank honey at Zaynab bt. Jahsh's; ʿĀʾisha and Ḥafṣa conspired to say he smelled unpleasant; he vowed to refrain; 66:1 was revealed.
- **Maria al-Qibṭiyya incident**: the Prophet was with Maria (his Coptic concubine) in Ḥafṣa's chamber on her day; Ḥafṣa returned; tensions erupted; the Prophet vowed to refrain from Maria; 66:1 revealed.
Rāzī discusses both and inclines to the honey reading. Zamakhsharī gives both. Qurṭubī extensively analyzes the grammar and the asbāb.

**Modern.** Muhammad Haykal and other modern biographers tend to prefer the honey version as more dignified. Western scholars (Maxime Rodinson, William Montgomery Watt) accept the Maria tradition as earlier and probably historical.

**Tension.** Several levels:
(i) *God chides the Prophet* — the Quran preserves material in which its own messenger is corrected, which is a remarkable feature of the text (contrast with canonical gospels' portrayal of Jesus, or with idealized prophetic biographies).
(ii) The *forbidden-permitted* language implies the Prophet had authority to forbid himself something God had allowed — a *self-ḥarām* — and God overrides. This has implications for the scope of prophetic authority over legislation.
(iii) The Maria version preserves material embarrassing to later piety (the Prophet balancing wives and concubines; divine intervention in domestic politics). The existence of such material in the canonical Quran is itself *evidence of textual honesty* — a forger would have excised. This is a classic *criterion of embarrassment* point in modern historical-critical study.
The classical tradition reads 66:1 as *qudwa* (exemplary) — demonstrating the Prophet's humanity, the corrective force of revelation, and the principle that pious motive does not override divine license.

## Cross-cutting observations

1. **Classical tradition consistently holds tensions open.** Rāzī, Zamakhsharī, Qurṭubī do not pretend unanimity; they catalog disagreement. The *illusion* of a single "classical position" is a later artifact.
2. **The Quran deploys difficulty rhetorically.** Verses like 74:30 explicitly label themselves as *fitna* — as tests that discriminate reactions. The paradox is *functional*, not a bug.
3. **Major tensions sit at the interfaces** — between Quran and prior scripture (50:38, 3:55), between Quran and later doctrine (20:121, 22:52), between Quran and Quran (4:3 / 4:129, 74:31 / 18:29). The *interface* is where theological work occurs.
4. **Modern scholarship often recovers early plurality** that medieval orthodoxy subsequently narrowed (Shahab Ahmed on Satanic Verses; Suleiman Mourad on Jesus's death; Kecia Ali on marriage).
5. **Honest reading requires resisting both apologetic smoothing and polemical sharpening.** The verses *do* create theological problems; the tradition *has* addressed them with intellectual seriousness; residual tensions *remain*.
