# Muḥkam vs mutashābih: the Q 3:7 debate

> **Note on register.** The term *mutashābih* carries two quite distinct senses in classical ʿulūm al-Qurʾān:
> - **Mutashābih-al-lafẓī** — *parallel passages with minor verbal variation* (the al-Kirmānī / al-Zarkashī *naw'* 52 tradition). This project already catalogs 265 such pairs in `mutashabih-lafzi.md`. It is a **rhetorical / stylistic** category.
> - **Mutashābih of Q 3:7** — *verses whose meaning is theologically ambiguous or whose referent is humanly indeterminate*. This is a **doctrinal / hermeneutic** category, the subject of this file.
>
> The two categories overlap only incidentally. The Q 3:7 register is the one Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Taymiyya, and al-Suyūṭī are arguing about when they argue about *muḥkam* vs *mutashābih*. That argument is our target.

## 1. Q 3:7 — the locus classicus

Arabic (Āl ʿImrān 7):

> هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنزَلَ عَلَيْكَ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ مِنْهُ ءَايَٰتٌ مُّحْكَمَٰتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَٰبِهَٰتٌ ۖ فَأَمَّا ٱلَّذِينَ فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَٰبَهَ مِنْهُ ٱبْتِغَآءَ ٱلْفِتْنَةِ وَٱبْتِغَآءَ تَأْوِيلِهِۦ ۗ وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُۥٓ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ ۗ وَٱلرَّٰسِخُونَ فِى ٱلْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ ءَامَنَّا بِهِۦ كُلٌّ مِّنْ عِندِ رَبِّنَا ۗ وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّآ أُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَلْبَٰبِ

Structural anatomy (eight operative clauses):

1. **The asymmetric partition.** The Book contains *āyāt muḥkamāt* — "they are *umm al-kitāb*" (the mother / matrix of the Book) — and *others* (*ukhar*) *mutashābihāt*. The two sets are presented as unequal: the muḥkam is the **matrix**; the mutashābih is the **remainder**.
2. **The diagnostic test.** "Those in whose hearts is *zaygh* [deviation]" — the moral state of the interpreter is the first variable.
3. **The failure mode.** Such persons "follow what is mutashābih of it" (*yattabiʿūna mā tashābaha minhu*) — i.e. they *select* the ambiguous as their interpretive object.
4. **Two motives for that pursuit.** *ibtighāʾ al-fitna* (seeking sedition) and *ibtighāʾ taʾwīlihi* (seeking its ultimate interpretation). The second motive is diagnostic: pressing for *taʾwīl* where taʾwīl is withheld is itself the sin.
5. **The withholding clause.** *Wa-mā yaʿlamu taʾwīlahu illā Allāh* — "and none knows its *taʾwīl* except God." The word for the negation is *mā* (categorical), and the restrictive exception particle is *illā*.
6. **The crux — the waqf (pause) dispute.**
   - If one stops at *illā Allāh*, then the following *wa-l-rāsikhūna fī l-ʿilm yaqūlūna āmannā bihi* ("and those firmly rooted in knowledge say: we believe in it") begins a **new sentence**: the rooted scholars do *not* know; they only *affirm*. This is the **Sunnī-Ashʿarī-Salafī** reading (Ibn ʿAbbās in one riwāya, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, Ibn Taymiyya).
   - If one reads *wa-l-rāsikhūna fī l-ʿilm* as **coordinate** with *Allāh*, then "none knows its taʾwīl except God *and those firmly rooted in knowledge*"; the *yaqūlūna āmannā bihi* is a *ḥāl* (circumstantial). This is the **interpretive / Muʿtazilī-Shīʿī-ʿirfānī** reading (Ibn ʿAbbās in a second riwāya, Mujāhid, al-Rāzī in some passes, Shīʿī tafsīr).
   The entire dogmatic weight of Q 3:7 rides on a pause-mark.
7. **The scholars' assent.** *Yaqūlūna āmannā bihi; kullun min ʿindi rabbinā* — "we believe in it; all is from our Lord." This is epistemic *submission*, not epistemic *claim*.
8. **The coda.** *Wa-mā yadhdhakkaru illā ulū l-albāb* — "and none take heed except the possessors of *lubb* [core-intellect]." *Albāb* is the faculty that distinguishes muḥkam from mutashābih *and* keeps the pursuit of mutashābih bounded.

Critical term glosses:
- **Muḥkam** (√ḥ-k-m) — "firmly fixed, arbitrated, made decisive." Cognate with *ḥikma* (wisdom), *ḥukm* (judgment). A muḥkam verse is one whose ruling or meaning is *tight*, *ne varietur*, not prone to divergent construal.
- **Mutashābih** (√sh-b-h) — "resembling, ambiguous." The IV form is lexically *mutashabba*-like: "self-resembling," i.e. internally self-consistent but externally susceptible to multiple resemblances. In doctrinal tafsīr this means *susceptible to multiple readings*.
- **Umm al-kitāb** — "matrix / source / mother of the Book." Also used in Q 13:39 and Q 43:4 for the heavenly archetype. Here, applied to the muḥkam subset, it means *the interpretive pivot*: mutashābih must be read through muḥkam, not muḥkam through mutashābih. This is a **hermeneutic hierarchy**.
- **Zaygh** (√z-y-gh) — "deviation, tilt." The cognitive posture that *selects* ambiguity as ground for doctrine.
- **Taʾwīl** — originally "returning something to its origin/outcome." In Q 3:7 it can mean (a) *ultimate eschatological realisation* (Ibn Taymiyya's reading: we won't know the *reality* of "God's hand" until the Day), or (b) *esoteric/final interpretation* (the Muʿtazilī-philosophical reading: the rational construal beyond the surface).

This verse is thus at once a **doctrine of the Qurʾān** (partition into two kinds of verse), a **doctrine of the interpreter** (zaygh vs rusūkh), and a **doctrine of the limits of knowledge** (taʾwīl withheld vs taʾwīl available).

## 2. The classical schools

Four broad positions emerge in the classical literature. They differ on two independent axes: **(A)** whether *rāsikhūn fī l-ʿilm* know taʾwīl or merely affirm it, and **(B)** what *kind* of ambiguity mutashābih designates (lexical, referential, ontological, eschatological).

### 2.1 The Salafī / early-traditionalist reading (God-only)

**Position.** The waqf is at *illā Allāh*. The meaning of the mutashābih (paradigmatically: the divine attributes) is known to God alone. The rooted scholars *affirm* the text (*āmannā bihi*) and *do not interpret*.

**Representatives.** Mālik b. Anas (famous dictum on Q 20:5 below), Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, al-Ṭabarī (tentatively), Ibn Taymiyya (strongly, in *al-Iklīl* and *Dar' taʿāruḍ al-ʿaql wa-l-naql*), Ibn Kathīr, later Salafī ḥadīth scholars.

**Hermeneutic.** *Bi-lā kayf wa-lā tashbīh wa-lā taʿṭīl* — "without asking 'how', without anthropomorphising, without evacuating" (see §3.2 below). The mutashābih is *passed through* linguistically (*imrār ʿalā ẓawāhirihā*) while its *kayfiyya* (modality) is surrendered to God.

**Strengths.** Matches the literal grammar of Q 3:7 under the most natural waqf. Avoids speculative kalām. Matches the hadith "Muḥkam is what we act upon, mutashābih is what we believe in" (attributed to ʿAlī in some chains).

**Weaknesses.** If mutashābih is simply *opaque*, why reveal it at all? (al-Rāzī's challenge, see §7.) And the apparent fact that *some* scholars do produce taʾwīls that the tradition accepts (e.g. *yad* → *qudra*) is difficult to square with strict silence.

### 2.2 The interpretive / rationalist reading (scholars-also-know)

**Position.** *Rāsikhūn fī l-ʿilm* is coordinate with *Allāh*; those firmly rooted in knowledge *do* know the taʾwīl. Mutashābih is *obscure but penetrable*, and penetration is the scholars' proper task.

**Representatives.** Ibn ʿAbbās (in one famous riwāya: "I am of those firmly rooted in knowledge; I know its taʾwīl"), Mujāhid, al-Rabīʿ b. Anas, al-Zamakhsharī (Muʿtazilī), much of Shīʿī tafsīr (where the rāsikhūn are identified with the imams), philosophical tafsīr (Ibn Sīnā's marginalia), and Sufi taʾwīl from al-Tustarī onward.

**Hermeneutic.** The mutashābih verses are *invitations*. Anthropomorphic expressions are *figurative* (*majāz*); the scholar's task is to return them to tanzīh (transcendence) by lexical-rhetorical work (*yad* = *qudra*, *istawā* = *istawlā* or *qahara* — "took dominion" rather than "sat upon").

**Strengths.** Explains why revelation would include a mutashābih category: to provoke rational reflection (*tadabbur*) and train the *albāb*. Accounts for the interpretive activity the tradition in fact performed.

**Weaknesses.** The most natural waqf cuts against it. And it risks dissolving mutashābih into muḥkam, collapsing the partition Q 3:7 explicitly asserts.

### 2.3 The Ashʿarī / Māturīdī middle way

**Position.** There are **two classes** of mutashābih: (i) those whose meaning *is* accessible to rooted scholars (e.g. abrogation, occasion-of-revelation dependencies, certain legal edge cases), and (ii) those whose *kayfiyya* is withheld (the divine attributes, eschatology's detail, the Hour). The waqf-question is resolved by this two-tier structure.

**Representatives.** al-Juwaynī, al-Ghazālī, al-Rāzī (mostly — see §7), al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī (Māturīdī), al-Taftazānī.

**Hermeneutic.** *Taʾwīl ijmālī* (general taʾwīl) vs *taʾwīl tafṣīlī* (detailed taʾwīl): we may engage in *general* figurative redirection (God's "hand" is not a bodily hand) without pretending to *detailed* positive specification of what the term in fact designates.

This is the position that became Sunnī theological mainstream, and it is under this umbrella that most post-5th-century mutashābih-scholarship operates.

### 2.4 The Shīʿī-ʿirfānī reading (imams-as-rāsikhūn)

**Position.** Rāsikhūn fī l-ʿilm are specifically *ahl al-bayt* (the Prophet's family; the imams). The mutashābih is penetrable, but only by the infallible. Lay believers affirm (*āmannā*); imams know (*yaʿlamūna*).

**Representatives.** al-Ṭabrisī (Majmaʿ al-Bayān), al-Kulaynī's hadith material, Mullā Ṣadrā, most Twelver uṣūl.

This position preserves both horns of the Q 3:7 grammar (two subjects: Allāh and rāsikhūn) *and* a strong limit (not all scholars — only the imams — penetrate the mutashābih).

## 3. Verses classically placed in each category

### 3.1 Standard-list muḥkamāt

The classical consensus places in the muḥkam category:

1. **The tawḥīd declarations.** *Lā ilāha illā huwa* — the verse "there is no god but He" in its various forms (Q 2:163, 2:255, 3:2, 3:18, 6:102, 7:158, 9:31, 11:14, 13:30, 16:2, 20:8, 20:14, 20:98, 21:25, 27:26, 28:70, 35:3, 39:6, 40:3, 40:62, 40:65, 59:22, 59:23, 64:13, 73:9). Eight exact occurrences of the compact form "Allāh lā ilāha illā huwa"; the cognate "alladhī lā ilāha illā huwa" is vanishingly rare (Q 20:98, Q 59:22, Q 59:23 — twin-opener). The shahāda verses are the *paradigm muḥkam*: unambiguous content, interpretation-immune.
2. **Legal verses (*āyāt al-aḥkām*).** The prayer-direction (Q 2:144), the fasting rulings (Q 2:183-187), the inheritance shares (Q 4:11-12, 4:176), the *hadd* punishments (Q 5:38 on theft; Q 24:2 on fornication), the prohibition of intoxicants (Q 5:90-91), the marriage-and-divorce regulations (Q 65). Their content is operational (what one must *do*), their grammar is declarative, and the jurists have successfully reached ijmāʿ or near-ijmāʿ on their application.
3. **Ethical imperatives** — Q 2:177 (the "righteousness" verse), Q 17:22-39 (the Decalogue-like sequence), Q 49:11-13 (ethics of community).
4. **Historical narrative in its operational moral content** — the stories of the prophets, *in the sense that their moral lesson is clear*, even if narrative detail (colour of the dog in the Cave, number of Sleepers, identity of Khiḍr) is mutashābih.

The hermeneutic principle: muḥkam is what **compels action or belief without further taʾwīl**.

### 3.2 Standard-list mutashābihāt

Five classical clusters:

**(a) The *ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt*** — ALM, ALR, ḤM, KHYʿṢ, etc. These are the paradigm mutashābih for al-Rāzī, who opens his commentary on Q 2:1 by surveying more than twenty classical proposals and concluding that their *taʾwīl* is with God. Ibn Kathīr likewise. The muqaṭṭaʿāt are lexically *undecomposable* into words (they are *letter-names*, not lexemes), which puts them in a special sub-class: they are not merely ambiguous *words*, they are sub-lexical.

**(b) The "divine-hand / face / eye / throne" verses** — the *āyāt al-ṣifāt*. Canonical exemplars:
- *yad* (hand): Q 5:64 "*bal yadāhu mabsūṭatān*" ("nay, both His hands are outstretched"); Q 38:75 "*khalaqtu bi-yadayya*" ("what I created with My two hands"); Q 48:10 "*yad Allāh fawqa aydīhim*".
- *wajh* (face): Q 2:115 "*fa-thamma wajh Allāh*"; Q 55:27 "*wa-yabqā wajh rabbika*"; Q 28:88 "*kullu shayʾin hālikun illā wajhahu*".
- *ʿayn* (eye): Q 11:37, Q 20:39, Q 52:48, Q 54:14 "*tajrī bi-aʿyuninā*".
- *sāq* (shin): Q 68:42 "*yawma yukshafu ʿan sāqin*".
- *istawā* (mounted / settled on): Q 7:54, Q 10:3, Q 13:2, Q 20:5, Q 25:59, Q 32:4, Q 57:4 — seven occurrences of "*ʿalā l-ʿarshi stawā*" or variants.

The locus classicus is **Q 20:5**: *al-Raḥmān ʿalā l-ʿarshi stawā* — "the Merciful on the Throne settled / mounted / took position." Imām Mālik's response, recorded in al-Bayhaqī and elsewhere, became the orthodox formula: *al-istiwāʾ maʿlūm, wa-l-kayf majhūl, wa-l-imān bihi wājib, wa-l-suʾāl ʿanhu bidʿa* — "The settling is known [in language], the how is unknown, belief in it is obligatory, and asking after it is innovation."

This single dictum is the *classical muḥkam-mutashābih workflow* compressed: the *verb* is admitted (muḥkam: *istawā* is an Arabic word with a known lexical range), the *modality* is withheld (mutashābih: the *how* of divine istiwāʾ is opaque), and the *hermeneutic duty* is belief + silence about kayfiyya.

**(c) Eschatological description.** The paradise-and-hell passages — Q 55's rivers and pairs of gardens, Q 56's *wildān mukhalladūn*, Q 76's *silver goblets*, Q 52:20's *ḥūr ʿīn*, Q 69:13-18's Day description, Q 101's *qāriʿa*. Ibn ʿAbbās's maxim: *laysa fī l-janna mimmā fī l-dunyā illā l-asmāʾ* ("nothing of paradise resembles this world except the names"). This is an explicit **mutashābih-by-ontological-distance** claim: the referent is real, the signifier is common, the *resemblance* between referent and signifier is what is opaque.

**(d) The Hour (*al-sāʿa*) and decree (*qadar*).** Q 7:187 "*qul innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī*" (the Hour's timing is with God alone); Q 42:17, Q 33:63, Q 79:42-44. Classical treatments place these with the muqaṭṭaʿāt and ṣifāt as *whose taʾwīl is with God alone* in the strict Ibn-Taymiyya sense of *taʾwīl* = ultimate realisation.

**(e) The *rūḥ* question.** Q 17:85 "*yasʾalūnaka ʿan al-rūḥi; quli l-rūḥu min amri rabbī wa-mā ūtītum mina l-ʿilmi illā qalīlā*". The Quran here *thematises its own withholding*: the asked-about is declared a domain where *your knowledge is only a little*. This is the only verse in the Quran that explicitly *names* a mutashābih referent and *explicitly* closes interpretation.

## 4. The "disease of heart" critique — what Q 3:7 diagnoses

Q 3:7's middle clause is less a description than a **diagnostic**: those who have *zaygh* in their hearts **follow what is mutashābih from it, seeking fitna and seeking its taʾwīl**.

Three elements:

1. **Selection bias as symptom.** The disease manifests as *interpretive selection* — the deviant reader systematically chooses the ambiguous verses to *ground* doctrine, ignoring the muḥkam matrix that should anchor reading. al-Ṭabarī reads this as a direct reference to the Najrān Christian delegation of 9 AH who came to Madīna and argued for divinity-of-Jesus from the Qurʾānic *rūḥ minhu* (Q 4:171) and *kalimatuhu* (also Q 4:171) — mutashābih epithets — while ignoring the muḥkam *mā l-masīḥu bnu Maryama illā rasūl* (Q 5:75) and *innahu ʿabdī* (Q 19:30). The surah's opening 80 verses are the Quran's direct engagement with exactly this Christology.
2. **Dual motive.** *Fitna* (sedition) and *taʾwīl* (interpretation). The first is political/communal; the second is hermeneutic/epistemic. The verse is saying that *hermeneutic overreach and communal sedition are the same disease under two descriptions*. Al-Rāzī develops this at length: claiming detailed taʾwīl of what God has withheld is itself a form of *fitna*, because it produces sects.
3. **The contrast class.** The rāsikhūn's response is *kullun min ʿindi rabbinā* — "**all** is from our Lord." The word *kullun* is crucial: the rooted scholar refuses to split the Book into "good" (muḥkam, rational, manageable) vs "bad" (mutashābih, embarrassing, to-be-explained-away). Both are from the same Lord; both bear the same authorship; the asymmetry between them is internal to the Book's design, not a defect to be cured.

This is the ethical heart of the verse: **a readerly posture that treats the mutashābih as a problem-to-be-solved rather than a mystery-to-be-inhabited is itself the pathology Q 3:7 names**.

## 5. This project's findings read through muḥkam / mutashābih

This section connects the classical debate to three concrete findings already in the corpus.

### 5.1 The muqaṭṭaʿāt as paradigm mutashābih — what our data adds

Our `muqattaat-analysis.md` produces a combined Stouffer **Z = +4.48** (one-tailed p ≈ 3.8e-06) for enrichment of the opening-letters inside the surahs that open with them, under a 3-gram letter Markov surrogate. This is the first **quantitative** result on the muqaṭṭaʿāt classical tradition has had, and it reframes the muḥkam / mutashābih question:

- **Classically**, the muqaṭṭaʿāt are *mutashābih* because *no agreed semantic taʾwīl exists*. Al-Rāzī opens his *Mafātīḥ* on Q 2:1 by surveying >20 proposals (divine names, numerical codes, phonetic-demonstration, challenge to Quraysh, etc.) and concluding that certain knowledge is with God.
- **Our data** shows that the muqaṭṭaʿāt bear a *statistically detectable structural relationship* to their surahs (enrichment of the very letters that open the surah, driven mainly by Surah 50 Qāf, Surah 2 Baqara, Surah 29 ʿAnkabūt). The muqaṭṭaʿāt are therefore **not semantically empty** — they stand in a measurable *textual* relationship to what follows.
- **The classical category is preserved**: the *semantic* taʾwīl (what the letters *mean*) remains with God. But the *structural* taʾwīl (what the letters *do* in the text) is partially observable.

This is exactly the Ashʿarī two-tier position (§2.3) confirmed empirically: *taʾwīl ijmālī* (general understanding — the letters signal a structural relationship) is accessible; *taʾwīl tafṣīlī* (detailed specification of which letter means which concept) is not. The muqaṭṭaʿāt are a live case where the classical *bi-lā kayf* applies with unusual precision.

### 5.2 Q 2:255 / Q 59:22-24 — muḥkam and mutashābih in their compound

Our `ayat-al-kursi.md` analyses the diptych Āyat al-Kursī (Q 2:255) vs Khawātim al-Ḥashr (Q 59:22-24) as two poles of divine description. The muḥkam / mutashābih lens clarifies why they behave as they do.

**Āyat al-Kursī (Q 2:255)** mixes three modes:
- **Muḥkam tawḥīd** — *Allāh lā ilāha illā huwa; al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* (the shahāda-type opening; *muḥkam* par excellence).
- **Apophatic muḥkam** — *lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawm* ("neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him"). Classical tafsīr (al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī) treats this as muḥkam: the *negation* is clear; no one wonders *which* of "drowsiness" applies.
- **Mutashābih ṣifāt** — *wasiʿa kursiyyuhu l-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* ("His kursī extends over the heavens and earth"). *Kursī* is a hapax-as-divine-object (its only other Quranic occurrence, Q 38:34, is Solomon's human throne). The classical debate on kursī-vs-ʿarsh, on whether the kursī is a *footstool* or a *throne* or *metaphor for knowledge* (Ibn ʿAbbās: *kursiyyuhu ʿilmuhu*), is a textbook mutashābih-ṣifāt debate.

**The compound's theological work.** Āyat al-Kursī is *kataphatic and apophatic at once*: it both asserts what God is (Living, Self-Subsisting) and negates what cannot apply (drowsiness, fatigue) and *then* produces a mutashābih spatial image (kursī extending over heavens and earth). The muḥkam and mutashābih clauses interlock within a single verse.

**Khawātim al-Ḥashr (Q 59:22-24)** is more homogeneously kataphatic: 15 divine names piled over 3 verses, most within the muḥkam tawḥīd register (al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm, al-Malik, al-Quddūs, al-Salām, al-Muʾmin, al-Muhaymin, al-ʿAzīz, al-Jabbār, al-Mutakabbir, al-Khāliq, al-Bāriʾ, al-Muṣawwir, al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm). Here the mutashābih surface is thin: the names are *known Arabic* but their *divine application* is *bi-lā kayf*. The classical formula solves the case cleanly.

**The diptych's design insight.** The Quran places two "greatest" passages (hadith tradition names both) at opposite ends of the muḥkam-mutashābih spectrum *within kataphatic divine description*:
- Q 2:255 — muḥkam–apophatic–mutashābih braided in one verse.
- Q 59:22-24 — muḥkam kataphatic piling, mutashābih implicit (in *kayfiyya* only).

The two are not rivals. They are **complementary exemplars of how divine self-description handles the muḥkam/mutashābih asymmetry** — one braids all three modes, one stacks the muḥkam names and leaves mutashābih implicit. The hadith tradition's naming of *both* as greatest is internally consistent with Q 3:7's partition: any "greatest" passage must engage both sides of the Book's design.

### 5.3 A mutashābih signal our corpus does *not* support

Our Khalifa-19 audit (`code19-khalifa-full-audit.md`) provides a cautionary check against mutashābih-motivated pattern-hunting. Only Surah 50 (Qāf) passes the 19-divisibility test for its opening letter count (57 = 19×3); Surah 68 (Nūn) fails (131, not 133). Where classical tradition placed the muqaṭṭaʿāt firmly in the mutashābih category and was content to let the taʾwīl rest with God, a modern numerological reading tried to force a detailed taʾwīl tafṣīlī and largely failed under rigorous counting. This is Q 3:7's "following what is mutashābih of it" in its 20th-century form: a clear example of zaygh-selection producing detailed-taʾwīl claims that do not survive audit. The classical restraint was, as it turns out, empirically well-calibrated.

## 6. al-Rāzī's engagement — the tafsīr-scholastic apex

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī's *Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb* treats Q 3:7 at unusual length, structuring the problem into **ten questions** (*masāʾil*). The salient moves:

1. **Why reveal the mutashābih at all?** al-Rāzī rejects the simple "test of faith" answer and argues that mutashābih serves (i) **training of the intellect** — if all verses were muḥkam, the mind would have nothing to work on and would not earn understanding; (ii) **protection of tanzīh** — mutashābih prevents the reader from anthropomorphising: precisely because ṣifa-verses are marked as mutashābih, the trained reader *cannot* read them naively; (iii) **distinguishing the ranks of the scholars** — only *albāb* make the partition well. This is his famous "mutashābih is pedagogical" answer.
2. **The waqf question.** al-Rāzī sides, in most passes, with the *connective* reading (rāsikhūn *do* know), but qualifies: they know *taʾwīl* in the sense of *valid figurative redirection within Arabic*, not in the sense of *exhaustive kayfiyya*. He is here essentially formulating the Ashʿarī two-tier.
3. **The ṣifāt case.** al-Rāzī gives the "hand" example: the rooted scholar knows *yad* cannot mean a bodily limb (reason rules this out); therefore the rooted scholar *does* produce a general taʾwīl (*yad* → *qudra* / "power" in context, or *ʿināya* / "providing care"); but the rooted scholar *does not* specify the ontological detail of how divine power operates. This is taʾwīl ijmālī without taʾwīl tafṣīlī.
4. **The muqaṭṭaʿāt case.** al-Rāzī concludes that the muqaṭṭaʿāt are a **strict** mutashābih: no general taʾwīl is available, because the letters do not themselves enter a figurative-redirection grammar. Here he reverts to the God-only reading. Our structural finding (§5.1) post-dates him, but his verdict is consistent with our data: the *semantic* taʾwīl is withheld; the *structural* relationship survives.
5. **The political-diagnostic dimension.** al-Rāzī develops the zaygh-diagnostic at length: he identifies the Bāṭinī Ismāʿīlīs of his day as paradigm zaygh-readers who mine mutashābih for esoteric sect-foundations while ignoring muḥkam. The verse's *fitna* clause is, for al-Rāzī, a warning *about sectarianism as the social output of hermeneutic overreach*.

al-Rāzī's is the most-rigorous pre-modern treatment, and it lands roughly where the empirical-structural reading lands: **two-tier mutashābih; taʾwīl ijmālī accessible, taʾwīl tafṣīlī withheld; muqaṭṭaʿāt as a strict sub-class**.

## 7. Synthesis — what the classical debate and our corpus jointly support

Five propositions survive both classical scrutiny and our empirical work:

1. **The Q 3:7 partition is real and productive.** The Quran itself marks that its verses differ in interpretive accessibility. A reading that collapses the partition (treats everything as equally accessible, or as equally opaque) is self-undermining.
2. **The Ashʿarī two-tier is the stable position.** Taʾwīl ijmālī (general, within-language figurative redirection) is available to rooted scholars; taʾwīl tafṣīlī (ontological specification, kayfiyya) is not. Our muqaṭṭaʿāt result is this distinction in empirical form.
3. **The diagnostic clause is load-bearing.** Q 3:7's middle clause is not polemical decoration; it is the verse's interpretive instruction. A reading that pursues mutashābih as its *primary* ground of doctrine is — by the Quran's own diagnosis — zaygh.
4. **Muḥkam anchors mutashābih, not the reverse.** *Hunna umm al-kitāb*: the muḥkam matrix is the hermeneutic pivot. Q 2:255's apophatic clauses (muḥkam-style) anchor its kursī clause (mutashābih). Q 59:22-24's 15 muḥkam names anchor any mutashābih implication about divine kayfiyya. When a reader inverts this — anchoring muḥkam *through* mutashābih, reading tawḥīd *through* speculative ṣifāt-cosmology — the structure inverts into sectarian output.
5. **The Qurʾān thematises its own interpretive economy.** Q 3:7 is a meta-verse: it *tells the reader how to read the Book*. Q 17:85 (the rūḥ verse) and the muqaṭṭaʿāt are the Quran's live demonstrations of the economy it describes. The partition is not projected onto the text by tradition; it is asserted by the text itself and confirmed by the text's own behaviour (disclosure + reserve).
