# Weapons and Warfare in the Qurʾān — Vocabulary, Distribution, Theology

All counts are token counts unless otherwise noted. All verse references follow the Ḥafṣ/Kūfan numbering.

## 1. Per-weapon inventory: the absence of the warrior's kit

The single most striking datum is a set of *silences*. The Qurʾān, despite its reputation, does not speak a warrior's vocabulary of weapons. Taking the classical Arabian kit-list one root at a time:

| Weapon (gloss)           | Root          | Token count | Surahs | Notes |
|--------------------------|---------------|:-----------:|:------:|-------|
| sayf (sword)             | s-y-f         | **0**       | —      | absent entirely |
| tirs (shield)            | t-r-s         | **0**       | —      | absent entirely |
| rumḥ (spear/lance)       | r-m-ḥ         | 1           | 1      | Q 5:94 (rimāḥ): pilgrims hunting game |
| sahm (arrow)             | s-h-m         | 1           | 1      | Q 37:141 — but means "drew lots" (Jonah) |
| qaws (bow)               | q-w-s         | 1           | 1      | Q 53:9 — distance idiom, "two bow-lengths" |
| aslihah (weapons/arms)   | s-l-ḥ         | 4           | 1      | all in Q 4:102, combat-prayer verse |
| labūs (mail-shirt)       | l-b-s         | 1 (in sense)| 1      | Q 21:80 — David's craft |
| sābighāt (flowing mail)  | s-b-gh        | 1 (in sense)| 1      | Q 34:11 — David's craft |
| ḥadīd (iron)             | ḥ-d-d         | 6           | 5      | once for David's iron (34:10), otherwise punitive/cosmic |
| dirʿ / miḥjān / saʿd     | d-r-ʿ etc.    | **0**       | —      | classical armour/shield lexemes absent |

This is not the lexicon of a martial epic. A single verse (Q 4:102) carries the entire weight of the word "weapons" (`asliḥah`), and even there the context is liturgical: how to pray in congregation when an enemy is nearby. Four of the five weapons most characteristic of the Arabian warrior — sword, shield, lance-banner, dagger — are either missing or reduced to a single metaphorical or incidental token.

A methodological subtlety: the user-supplied prompt cited `miswad` ("iron-armour, Q 34:10"). The root `m-s-d` occurs in the Qurʾān exactly once, at Q 111:5 (`min masadin`), and there denotes the twisted-fibre neck-rope around Abū Lahab's wife. The iron and mail of David's craft at 34:10–11 are carried by two different roots: `ḥ-d-d` (ḥadīd = iron) and `s-b-gh` (sābighāt = long/flowing mail), with the shaping imperative `qaddir fī al-sard` — "measure the links in the weave". We therefore correct the item in section 8 below.

## 2. The root q-t-l: the real engine of Qurʾānic warfare

Where specific-weapon nouns are almost absent, verbs of killing and fighting are abundant. The root `q-t-l` yields **170 tokens across 33 surahs and 122 verses** (`root-stats.csv`). The distribution is strongly Medinan: 133 of the 170 tokens fall in Medinan sūras (78%), 37 in Meccan — the inverse of nearly every other theological root.

Top-loaded sūras (tokens of q-t-l):

| Sūra | Tokens | Character |
|------|:------:|-----------|
| Q 2  Baqarah    | 31 | legal infrastructure (2:190–194, 2:178 qiṣāṣ, 2:216–218) |
| Q 4  Nisāʾ      | 25 | rules of engagement, no-fault killing, hypocrites |
| Q 3  Āl ʿImrān  | 21 | Badr, Uḥud, martyrdom doctrine |
| Q 9  Tawbah     | 13 | treaty abrogation, Ḥunayn, jizya |
| Q 5  Māʾidah    | 13 | sanctity of life (5:32), Cain & Abel |
| Q 28 Qaṣaṣ      | 7  | Moses and the Egyptian (killing narrative) |
| Q 8  Anfāl      | 6  | Badr, spoils, rules |
| Q 33 Aḥzāb      | 6  | Trench / ḥijāb / hypocrites |
| Q 17 Isrāʾ      | 5  | do not kill children (17:31), sanctity (17:33) |

The semantic fan-out of q-t-l in the Qurʾān is wider than "war-killing". It covers: (1) homicide as capital crime (2:178, 4:92–93, 17:33); (2) infanticide prohibition (6:137, 6:151, 17:31, 81:8); (3) Israelite internal killing (2:54, 2:72, 2:87, 2:91, 4:155, 5:70); (4) Cain killing Abel (5:27–32) — the first human violence, used to state that *man nafsan bi-ghayri nafs ... fa-ka-annamā qatala l-nāsa jamīʿan*; (5) the Pharaoh-threat against Moses' followers (repeated in Q 7, Q 20, Q 28, Q 40 with `yuqattilu abnāʾakum`); (6) pagan self-killing / "killing oneself" in dispute (2:54); (7) and then the legal-war corpus in 2, 8, 9, 47, 48. The root is therefore indexed to *lethal action as such*, with combat as one region of its field.

Note the morphological shape: `q-t-l` in combat contexts is almost always Form III (`qātala` → `qitāl`) — a reciprocal stem meaning *to fight-with*, implying mutual engagement. The Form I `qatala` (to kill) predominates in non-combat settings. This grammatical differentiation carries doctrine: *qitāl* is, by form, relational, bilateral; *qatl* is unilateral. The legal verses deploy Form III repeatedly (`wa-qātilū`, `yuqātilūnakum`), while the prohibition against murder uses Form I.

## 3. Root j-h-d: 41 tokens, and "holy war" is the smaller meaning

The root `j-h-d` (to strive, to exert effort) occurs **41 times across 19 surahs**. Distribution: 11 Meccan tokens, 30 Medinan. Densest site: **Q 9 with 11 tokens** (an exceptional concentration — Q 9 alone holds 27% of the root).

Other loci: Q 29 (4), Q 4 (3), Q 5 (3), Q 8 (3), Q 25 (2), Q 22 (2), Q 16 (2); and one each in Q 2, 3, 6, 24, 29, 31, 35, 47, 49, 60, 61, 66.

Meccan `jihād` is almost never combat. Key Meccan tokens:
- **Q 25:52** — *fa-lā tuṭiʿi l-kāfirīna wa-jāhid-hum bi-hī jihādan kabīran* — "strive against them *with it* [the Qurʾān] a great striving". The instrument is the recited text, not a weapon.
- **Q 29:6, 29:8, 31:14–15** — "and we commanded man concerning his parents ... *but if they strive with thee* (`jāhadāka`) to associate with Me what thou hast no knowledge of, obey them not". Here *jāhada* means "pressure/compel" with the parents as the active *mujāhidūn* — against the believer.
- **Q 29:69** — *wa-lladhīna jāhadū fīnā la-nahdiyannahum subulanā* — "those who strive in Us, We shall guide them to Our ways". Pure interior/ascetic register.
- **Q 22:78** — *wa-jāhidū fī llāhi ḥaqqa jihādih* — "strive in God the right striving".

Only in Medinan Q 9 does *jihād* become systematically paired with q-t-l (9:24, 9:44, 9:73, 9:81, 9:86) and with "goods and selves" (`bi-amwālihim wa-anfusihim`). Even here, striving-with-wealth is listed first, implying that *financial contribution* to the community's defence is itself a category of jihād — not merely armed combat. The modern gloss "holy war" flattens a root whose Qurʾānic semantic arc runs: parental pressure → Qurʾānic persuasion → ascetic effort → financial sacrifice → migration (`hijra`) → physical fighting. The last is the densest Medinan usage but not the root's lexical core.

## 4. Named battles: the Qurʾān's minimalist battle-geography

Four historical engagements of the Medinan community are referenced. A fifth, Uḥud, is never named.

### Badr (year 2/624) — Q 3:123
> *wa-laqad naṣarakumu llāhu bi-Badrin wa-antum adhillah* — "God aided you at Badr while you were contemptible (few/weak)."

Single toponymic reference. The verse's emphasis is *adhillah* — "lowly, outnumbered" — an inversion of martial glory. Q 8 (Anfāl) narrates Badr without naming it, with an angelic-reinforcement motif (8:9, 8:12 "I am with you, so confirm those who believe; I shall cast terror into the hearts of the disbelievers") and a spoils ethic.

### Uḥud (year 3/625) — Q 3:140–155 (un-named)
The Qurʾān never utters the word "Uḥud". Q 3:140–144 diagnoses a wound: *in yamsaskum qarḥun fa-qad massa l-qawma qarḥun mithluh* — "if a wound touched you, a like wound has touched the people" (3:140). 3:144 then speaks the famous counter-rumour: *wa-mā Muḥammadun illā rasūl* — "Muḥammad is only a messenger; messengers have passed away before him; if then he dies or is killed (`qutila`), will you turn back on your heels?" 3:152–153 analyses the tactical collapse: *idh tuḥissūnahum bi-idhnih* — "when you were cutting them down by His leave" — until *fashiltum* ("you failed") and *tanāzaʿtum fī l-amr* ("you disputed over the command"). The theological frame is *mudāwalah* — "We rotate these days among men" (3:140) — i.e., battle outcomes are didactic, not triumphal.

### Ḥunayn (year 8/630) — Q 9:25–27
> *wa-yawma Ḥunaynin idh aʿjabatkum kathratukum* — "And on the day of Ḥunayn, when your multitude amazed you, it availed you nothing, and the earth, vast as it is, became straitened for you, then you turned back in retreat."

Ḥunayn is named precisely because it inverts Badr: at Badr, few triumphed in weakness; at Ḥunayn, many nearly lost in self-congratulation. 9:26 then sends down invisible `junūdan lam tarawhā` — "troops you did not see" — as the ground for what happened. The pedagogical symmetry with Badr is structural.

### al-Aḥzāb (the Trench / "Confederates", year 5/627) — Q 33:9–27
The densest battle pericope. Features:
- 33:9 — *innahu jāʾatkum junūdun* — "hosts came upon you" — with double mention of angelic `junūdan lam tarawhā` and a sent wind (*rīḥan*).
- 33:10 — *wa-idh zāghati l-abṣāru wa-balaghati l-qulūbu l-ḥanājir* — "when eyes swerved and hearts reached the throats", a vivid battle-fear idiom.
- 33:22 — the believer's contrasting response: *hādhā mā waʿadanā llāhu wa-rasūluh* — "this is what God and His messenger promised us."
- 33:25 — *wa-raddaallāhu lladhīna kafarū bi-ghayẓihim lam yanālū khayran, wa-kafā llāhu l-muʾminīna l-qitāl* — "God turned back the disbelievers in their rage; they attained no good; and God sufficed the believers against fighting."
- 33:26 — the Banū Qurayẓah episode, where the disbelievers' Jewish allies are brought down from their *ṣayāṣī* (fortresses) and a portion is killed, a portion taken captive (`farīqan taqtulūna wa-taʾsirūna farīqā`).

**Observation:** across all four named/implied battles, weapons are invisible and divine agency is foregrounded. Wind, angels, terror (`ruʿb`), tranquillity (`sakīnah`), multiplication/straitening of space — these do the narrative work. No sword is drawn in any of the battle verses.

## 5. The legal warfare corpus

Five pericopes carry the bulk of the Qurʾān's juridical framework for combat. Each sits in a Medinan sūra.

### Q 2:190–194 — the foundational permission
> *wa-qātilū fī sabīli llāhi lladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū* — "and fight in the way of God those who fight you, but do not transgress. God does not love transgressors." (2:190)

Five verses establish: (i) combat against combatants only; (ii) prohibition on *iʿtidāʾ* (aggression beyond legitimate response); (iii) fighting permitted in the Sacred Mosque precinct only if attacked there first; (iv) cessation upon enemy cessation (`fa-in intahaw fa-inna llāha ghafūrun raḥīm`); (v) retaliation in *proportion*: `fa-manī ʿtadā ʿalaykum fa-ʿtadū ʿalayhi bi-mithli mā ʿtadā ʿalaykum`.

The pericope's structure is chiastic: outer verses limit transgression; inner verse 193 states the war-aim (`ḥattā lā takūna fitnah`, "until there is no persecution") and its termination-condition.

### Q 8:60–66 — preparation and peace-option
Q 8:60 is the most militarised verse in the corpus (*wa-aʿiddū lahum mā staṭaʿtum min quwwatin wa-min ribāṭi l-khayl*) — but note two features. First, the immediate purpose is deterrence: *turhibūna bihī ʿaduwwa llāh* — "thereby to daunt the enemy of God". Second, the very next verse (8:61) reverses: *wa-in janaḥū lil-silmi fa-jnaḥ lahā wa-tawakkal ʿalā llāh* — "and if they incline to peace, incline thou to it and trust in God." The peace-option follows *immediately* the preparation-for-war.

8:65–66 then discusses numerical endurance ratios (20 vs. 200; 100 vs. 1000, then revised to 100 vs. 200; 1000 vs. 2000) — a unique Qurʾānic abrogation-within-a-surah that lowers the expected ratio because *God knew there was weakness in you*.

### Q 9:5, 9:29 — the so-called "sword verses"
Q 9:5 (`fa-qtulū l-mushrikīna ḥaythu wajadtumūhum`) is situated entirely inside a treaty-abrogation pericope (9:1–15) specifying the Makkan polytheists who had broken the Ḥudaybiyyah terms. It includes: the four sacred months as grace period; clemency upon repentance (`fa-in tābū wa-aqāmū l-ṣalāh`); asylum granted to a polytheist who seeks it to hear God's word (9:6) — a remarkable clause adjacent to the "kill" verse. Q 9:29 extends combat-permission to "People of the Book" who have violated covenant, with the jizya outcome rather than elimination.

### Q 47:4 — the prisoner ethic and war's term
> *fa-idhā laqītumu lladhīna kafarū fa-ḍarba l-riqābi ḥattā idhā athkhantumūhum fa-shuddū l-wathāq, fa-immā mannan baʿdu wa-immā fidāʾan, ḥattā taḍaʿa l-ḥarbu awzārahā*

"When you meet those who disbelieve, strike the necks, until when you have subdued them, bind the bonds firmly. Then either *mannan* (freeing as favour) *or fidāʾ* (ransom), until war lays down its burdens."

Three features: (i) *ḍarba l-riqāb* is phrased as a masdar ("a striking of necks") not an open-ended imperative; (ii) POWs receive a binary of release-or-ransom — not execution or enslavement; (iii) the telos `ḥattā taḍaʿa l-ḥarbu awzārahā` announces war's *termination*, not its normalisation. This is one of only 6 genuine `Hrb` tokens (cf. §1).

### Q 48:15–17 — the exemption clause
The post-Ḥudaybiyyah pericope specifies: those left behind seeking to join now only for *maghānim* (spoils) are barred; the *aʿrāb* must now face a *qawmun ulī baʾsin shadīd* ("a people of severe might"); but the blind, lame, and sick bear no guilt. This is the Qurʾānic precursor to canonical exemption-doctrine (non-combatants).

## 6. The "Fight in the way of Allah" formula

The phrase **`fī sabīli llāh`** (literally "in the path of God") occurs **45 times across 43 verses**. It is the master-frame that binds the whole corpus together. Its collocations:

| Co-occurring lexeme        | Verses | Notes |
|----------------------------|:------:|-------|
| q-t-l (fight / be killed)  | 19     | the martial sub-frame |
| j-h-d (strive)             | 11     | the broader striving sub-frame |
| anfaqa (spend)             | ≈10    | financial sub-frame (e.g. 2:195, 2:261, 2:262, 8:60) |
| hājara (migrate)           | ≈6     | 2:218, 4:100, 8:72, 9:20 |
| qaʿada (sit back)          | ≈3     | 4:95, criticising non-participation |

*Less than half* of `fī sabīl Allāh` occurrences pair with combat. The largest collocation group is giving/spending, not fighting. This materially reshapes the "holy war" reception: the formulaic centre of gravity is *voluntary costly action oriented toward God*, of which armed combat is the most dangerous subtype.

Within the combat subset, "those killed in the path of God" (2:154, 3:169, 3:195, 4:74, 47:4) are explicitly *not dead*: `wa-lā taqūlū li-man yuqtalu fī sabīli llāhi amwāt, bal aḥyāʾ wa-lākin lā tashʿurūn` (2:154). The martyr theology is embedded in the formula, not in a separate `shahīd`-specific idiom.

## 7. Prisoners of war — Q 8:67–70 and Q 47:4

Q 8:67–70 is a critical corrective passage delivered after Badr:
- 8:67 — *mā kāna li-nabiyyin an yakūna lahū asrā ḥattā yuthkhina fī l-arḍ* — "it is not for a prophet to take captives until he has subdued the land." An implicit rebuke: taking captives *before* decisive battle is a misordering.
- 8:67 concludes: *turīdūna ʿaraḍa l-dunyā wa-llāhu yurīdu l-ākhirah* — "you desire the goods of this world; God desires the hereafter." The rebuke is on motive.
- 8:68 — were it not for a prior divine decree, punishment would have touched them for the ransom they took.
- 8:69 — the ransom-spoils are now lawful (*kulū mimmā ghanimtum ḥalālan ṭayyibā*); 8:70 addresses the captives directly: "if God knows any good in your hearts, He will give you better than what was taken from you."

The POW provisions of 47:4 — *mannan ... fidāʾan* — rule out execution-as-default. Classical jurisprudence disagreed on whether a fifth option (enslavement) was implicit; the Qurʾānic text offers only two: release or ransom. Crucially, the chapter closes on 47:22 with a rhetorical question: *fa-hal ʿasaytum in tawallaytum an tufsidū fī l-arḍ* — "are you perhaps, if you take command, going to corrupt the earth?" — a warning *against* the abuse of martial authority.

## 8. David's armour-craft — Q 21:80, 34:10–11

Q 21:80 — *wa-ʿallamnāhu ṣanʿata labūsin lakum li-tuḥṣinakum min baʾsikum fa-hal antum shākirūn* — "We taught him the craft of mail for you, to fortify you from your [own] violence. Are you then grateful?"

Two observations. First, the telos: `li-tuḥṣinakum min ba'sikum` — "to protect you from *your own* might/violence". The purpose of the armour is *defensive and internal to the community*: the enemy being defended against is us. Second, the verse closes with an accusatory rhetorical gratitude-question (`fa-hal antum shākirūn`), implying the craft is under-appreciated.

Q 34:10–11 — *yā jibālu awwibī maʿahū wa-l-ṭayr, wa-alannā lahu l-ḥadīd. anī ʿmal sābighātin wa-qaddir fī l-sard* — "O mountains, echo with him, and the birds! And We softened for him iron. *Make long-flowing mail, and measure the links in the weaving*." Three technical Qurʾānic hapaxes cluster here: `alannā` (we softened), `sābighāt` (plural f. "amply flowing" — i.e., long-body armour), and `sard` (links/welded rings). No other verse uses these weaving-for-war terms.

David is thus the Qurʾān's only *artisan of war-gear*, and the gear is always *armour* (defensive) — never a sword (offensive). Combined with his Psalter (`zabūr`) and his mountain-echoing (`awwibī maʿahu`), David emerges as a composite sage-craftsman: scripture, song, metallurgy, law. The militarised elements of his reign are transformed into artisanal/doctrinal ones.

### Correction on "miswad"

The prompt glossed `miswad` as "iron-armour Q 34:10". The root `m-s-d` yields the Qurʾān's *only* occurrence at Q 111:5 (`ḥablun min masad`), describing Abū Lahab's wife's fibre neck-rope — the diametrical opposite of armour: a hangman's-style twist. At 34:10 the actual root is `ḥ-d-d` (`al-ḥadīd`, iron), and at 34:11 the armour-lexeme is `s-b-gh` (`sābighāt`). We record this as a lexical correction rather than an interpretive one.

## 9. The Ṣāliḥ she-camel — Q 7:73, 11:64, 26:155, 54:27, 91:13 — the anti-warfare sign

Across five surahs the Qurʾān returns to a single episode: the tribe Thamūd demands a sign; God sends a *nāqatu llāh* ("she-camel of God") whose water-right alternates with theirs (26:155 `lahā shirbun wa-lakum shirbu yawmin maʿlūm`); Thamūd *hamstring* (`ʿaqr`) the camel (`fa-ʿaqarū l-nāqah`); God destroys Thamūd.

The refrain is exact:
- Q 7:73 — *hādhihī nāqatu llāhi lakum āyatan fa-dharūhā taʾkul fī arḍi llāhi wa-lā tamassūhā bi-sūʾin* — "do not touch her with harm"
- Q 11:64 — identical prohibition
- Q 26:155–157 — water-alternation clause + same prohibition + `fa-ʿaqarūhā fa-aṣbaḥū nādimīn`
- Q 54:27–29 — *innā mursilū l-nāqati fitnatan lahum* — the she-camel *as a test*
- Q 91:13–14 — condensed: *nāqata llāhi wa-suqyāhā* / *fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-ʿaqarūhā*

Three observations on its relation to warfare:
1. **The sign is a living non-combatant.** Thamūd's crime is not rebellion or impiety in general, but a *specific act of violence against a creature under divine protection*.
2. **The destructive act is `ʿaqr` (hamstringing)** — a uniquely Arabian pre-Islamic sacrificial-cum-humiliation act. Its use here semantically *codes* unilateral, asymmetric violence against a non-resisting subject.
3. **Thamūd are destroyed not by military force** but by `ṣayḥah` (a cry, 11:67) or `ṣāʿiqah` (a thunderbolt, 41:13) or `ṭāghiyah` (an overwhelming event, 69:5) or `damdamah` (a crushing, 91:14). Divine response to anti-sign violence is *meteorological*, never martial.

The she-camel cycle therefore functions as a theological control case: it shows what Qurʾānic ethics look like when weaponry is absent on both sides. It is the mirror of the battle narratives: whereas Badr and al-Aḥzāb present divine wind/angels *defending* a community, the she-camel narrative presents divine *seismic* destruction *punishing* a community. In both cases human weaponry is absent; divine agency is primary. The anti-warfare reading is not that the Qurʾān is pacifist, but that weaponry has no autonomous narrative role: even in the five great battle stories, the decisive causation is non-human.

## 10. Synthesis: a warfare rhetoric without a weapons lexicon

Pulling the eight threads together:

1. **The Qurʾān speaks of *action* and *actor*, not of *implement*.** q-t-l (170), j-h-d (41), n-ṣ-r (158, help/victory), ṣ-b-r (103, patient endurance), b-ʾ-s (73, might) — these are the numerical giants. sayf, tirs, dirʿ — zero.

2. **`jund` / `junūd` is the true war-noun (29 tokens).** And more often than not the troops are invisible (9:26, 33:9, 48:4/7) or Pharaoh's (28:6/8/39/40, 26:95, 37:173) or punitive (36:28, 36:75).

3. **Form III of q-t-l (mutual fighting, qitāl) dominates the combat register**, encoding reciprocity rather than one-sided violence.

4. **`jihād`'s Meccan register is overwhelmingly non-martial.** It is the Qurʾān that forces interior effort, the recitation that struggles against disbelief, the parents who *jāhidū* a child to apostatise. Medinan usage adds a combat-specific sub-sense without eliminating the broader field.

5. **Named battles narrate divine agency, not martial prowess.** Wind, angels, tranquillity, terror, straitening/expanding space — all non-human causes. Uḥud, the community's painful loss, is never named at all.

6. **The legal corpus is saturated with *limits*.** "Do not transgress" (2:190). "If they incline to peace, incline to it" (8:61). "Strike the necks" (47:4) — immediately qualified by *mannan aw fidāʾ*. Four sacred months (9:5). Asylum for hearers (9:6). Exemptions for the weak (48:17).

7. **`fī sabīl Allāh`** is the master-frame: 45 occurrences across combat, spending, migration, striving — combat is one region, not the centre.

8. **David, the one prophet-warrior, is rendered as an armour-smith, a psalmist, and a judge.** His *defensive* craftsmanship (`li-tuḥṣinakum min ba'sikum`) is set against a community whose *own might* is the threat.

9. **The Ṣāliḥ she-camel names a non-negotiable category: the sign-creature under divine protection.** Its destruction is the Qurʾānic archetype of anti-civilisational violence — and the response is not war but cosmic event.

The Qurʾān's warfare rhetoric is thus *peculiarly de-instrumentalised*: there is no sword to swing, no shield to raise, no bow to draw. What there is, is a densely juridicised and theologised grammar of *action-under-limit*, framed by *fī sabīl Allāh* and terminated (47:4) by war's own laying-down of its burdens. Read against the classical Arabian poetic corpus — whose sword-and-spear lexicon is vast — the Qurʾān's weapons-silence is deliberate and structurally significant.
