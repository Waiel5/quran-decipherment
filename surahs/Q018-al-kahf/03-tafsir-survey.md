## 1. Surah-level openings: what each mufassir says about Q 18 as a whole

### 1.1 al-Qurṭubī's opening (`qurtubi-jami-ahkam.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18 opening)

al-Qurṭubī establishes:

1. **Meccan by consensus** (وهي مكية في قول جميع المفسرين). He notes a minority view that the surah's opening was Medinan (down to v. 8 *juruzan*), but rejects this as "the first is more correct" (والأول أصح).

2. **Anas b. Mālik fadāʾil**: "Whoever recites it is given a light between the heaven and the earth, and is shielded thereby from the trial of the grave" (من قرأ بها أعطي نورا بين السماء والأرض ووقي بها فتنة القبر) — citing it from Anas via Isḥāq b. ʿAbdullāh b. Abī Farwa. This isnād is later graded weak (al-Albānī).

3. **Friday-recitation tradition**: cites *al-Dārimī* via Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī — "Whoever recites Sūrat al-Kahf on Friday-night, light shines for him between him and the Ancient House [the Kaʿba]" (من قرأ سورة الكهف ليلة الجمعة أضاء له من النور فيما بينه وبين البيت العتيق).

4. **Ten-verses-Dajjāl-protection** (THE major canonical fadāʾil): from **Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim** via Abū al-Dardāʾ — "Whoever memorizes ten verses from the **first** of Sūrat al-Kahf will be protected from the Dajjāl" (من حفظ عشر آيات من أول سورة الكهف عصم من الدجال). al-Qurṭubī immediately notes the textual variant: "and in [another] narration: 'from the **last** of al-Kahf'" (وفي رواية" من آخر الكهف"). He further notes that Muslim records the Nawwās b. Samʿān al-Kilābī tradition — "whoever encounters [the Dajjāl], let him recite over him the openings of Sūrat al-Kahf" (فمن أدركه - يعني الدجال - فليقرأ عليه فواتح سورة الكهف).

5. **Samura b. Jundab tradition** (cited via al-Thaʿlabī): "whoever memorizes ten verses of Sūrat al-Kahf, the trial of the Dajjāl will not harm him" (من قرأ عشر آيات من سورة الكهف حفظا لم تضره فتنة الدجال). And: "whoever recites the entire surah enters Paradise" (ومن قرأ السورة كلها دخل الجنة).

al-Qurṭubī does NOT critique these chains; he transmits them with positive force, as is standard for fadāʾil literature.

### 1.2 al-Rāzī's opening (`razi-mafatih-al-ghayb.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18:1 commentary)

al-Rāzī's distinctive contribution is an **inter-surah comparative**: Q 18 is the third of the five *al-ḥamdu lillāh*-opening surahs (Q 1, 6, 18, 34, 35). al-Rāzī ranks them by theological scope (verbatim extract):

> فقوله في أول سورة الفاتحة: رب العالمين إشارة إلى التربية العامة في حق كل العالمين، ويدخل فيه التربية الروحانية للملائكة والإنس والجن والشياطين والتربية الجسمانية الحاصلة في السموات والأرضين، فكان المذكور في أول سورة الكهف نوعا من أنواع ما ذكره في أول الفاتحة.

"His statement in the opening of Sūrat al-Fātiḥa — 'Lord of the worlds' — refers to general nurture covering all worlds, including spiritual nurture of angels, humans, jinn, and demons, and corporeal nurture in the heavens and the earths. So what is mentioned at the opening of Sūrat al-Kahf is *one type* of what is mentioned in the opening of al-Fātiḥa."

For al-Rāzī, Q 18's opening focuses on **tarbiya rūḥāniyya** (spiritual cultivation through the Book sent to the servant), a *species* under al-Fātiḥa's *general* nurture. Q 6 al-Anʿām is creation-of-heavens-and-earth (cosmological-scope), Q 34 Sabaʾ is *who-owns-what-is-in-them*, Q 35 Fāṭir is creation-as-origination. The five *ḥamd*-openers are read as five facets of the al-Fātiḥa's general doxology.

al-Rāzī's overall claim: Q 18's *maqṣūd* (purpose) is **spiritual cultivation through revealed Scripture**, with the four narratives functioning as illustrative cases of God's *raḥma* (mercy) and *ʿilm* (knowledge) operating on different scales — youthful believers (cave), grown-prosperity (gardens), prophetic-confrontation-with-mystery (Mūsā/Khaḍir), kingly-power-with-eschatological-frame (Dhū al-Qarnayn). Each narrative is a *spiritual-cultivation case-study*.

### 1.3 al-Ṭabarī's asbāb al-nuzūl

al-Ṭabarī (`tabari-jami-bayan.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18 opening) preserves the canonical asbāb: *Quraysh* sent al-Naḍr b. al-Ḥārith and ʿUqba b. Abī Muʿayṭ to the Jewish rabbis of Madīna to verify the Prophet's claims. The rabbis instructed Quraysh to ask the Prophet about three things:
1. The youth in ancient times who departed from their people — the cave-companions.
2. A wandering man who reached the east-and-west of the earth — Dhū al-Qarnayn.
3. The spirit (*al-rūḥ*).

The Quran answered the first two questions in Q 18 (cave-companions vv. 9-26; Dhū al-Qarnayn vv. 83-101) and the third in Q 17:85 (*qul al-rūḥu min amri rabbī*) — placed in the *previous* surah of the mushaf-order. The asbāb is the foundational structural clue for why Q 18 contains specifically these narratives and not others.

The cave-companions narrative is identified by classical exegesis with the **Christian Seven Sleepers of Ephesus** tradition. al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr all cite the Najrān Christian elders' version as the immediate pre-Islamic context. The Quranic refusal to specify the *number* of companions (vv. 22, 25 — "three with their dog the fourth"/"five with their dog the sixth"/"seven with their dog the eighth"; "they remained in their cave three hundred years and increased nine") is read as a *rebuke* of the Christian disagreement over numbers.

### 1.4 al-Biqāʿī's *naẓm* claim (`biqai-nazm-al-durar.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18 opening)

al-Biqāʿī's *Naẓm al-Durar* applies his sequential-coherence (*munāsaba*) method to Q 18. His claim: **the four narratives are arranged as four ordered tests of the believer's faith under the four classical fitan**:

1. **Companions of the Cave = fitnat al-dīn** (trial of religion) — the youths preserve their religion against polytheist persecution.
2. **The two gardens = fitnat al-māl** (trial of wealth) — the rich man's pride leads to destruction.
3. **Mūsā and al-Khaḍir = fitnat al-ʿilm** (trial of knowledge) — Mūsā the prophet must accept that he is not the most-knowledgeable; *ʿilm ladunī* exists beyond what he knows.
4. **Dhū al-Qarnayn = fitnat al-mulk** (trial of power) — the king who reaches the ends of the earth and yet builds barriers and acknowledges *ḥattā idhā jāʾa waʿdu rabbī* (the eschaton).

al-Biqāʿī's four-fitan reading is one of the most-cited classical structural readings of Q 18; modern Muslim devotional literature (e.g., the Friday-recitation discipline) frequently cites this as the *meaning* of the Friday tradition — the surah covers the four fitan a Muslim must navigate.

al-Biqāʿī's *tartīb* (sequencing) is: opening frame asserts the *al-kitāb* (revelation as anti-fitna), four narratives instantiate the four fitan, closing frame asserts the *al-qul innamā anā basharun* (Prophetic exemplar). The narratives' lengths (18+13+23+16 = 70 verses) form the *content-core* (vv. 9-101); the discursive-frame verses (vv. 1-8 + 27-31 + 45-59 + 102-110 = 38 verses) form the *commentary* on the cores. Total: 70+38 = 108 verses + 2 transitional (vv. 26 closure + v. 102 opener) = 110 verses.

### 1.5 al-Zamakhsharī's *Kashshāf* on Q 18:1 (the *ʿiwajan / qayyiman*)

al-Zamakhsharī (`zamakhshari-kashshaf` Q18:1-2) treats the *wa-lam yajʿal lahu ʿiwajan • qayyiman* as a rhetorical-pivot example. The *ʿiwaj* (crookedness) is **negated**, then *qayyim* (straight) is **asserted positively**. The same content stated in two complementary syntactic forms is, for al-Zamakhsharī, the canonical example of the *iqāʿ* doctrine (al-Sakkākī, *Miftāḥ al-ʿulūm*) — that the Quran's *fawāṣil* are constructed via complementary-pair completions.

The *qayyiman* is grammatically a *ḥāl* (circumstantial accusative) qualifying *al-kitāb*; al-Rāzī (`razi-mafatih-al-ghayb.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18:2) endorses al-Zamakhsharī's reading and adds that the placement of *qayyiman* AT THE BEGINNING of v. 2 (not at the end of v. 1) is itself a structural signal: the assertion of straightness is given its own metrical-prosodic position.

### 1.6 Ibn Kathīr (`ibn-kathir-tafsir-quran.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18 opening)

Ibn Kathīr's distinctive contribution to Q 18 commentary is the **fadāʾil-collection**. He records, in his Q 18 opening:
- The Anas b. Mālik tradition (light between heaven and earth + protection from grave-trial).
- The Abū al-Dardāʾ tradition (first ten verses → protection from Dajjāl; **Muslim**'s text).
- The Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī Friday-recitation tradition (al-Dārimī's text).
- Commentary on each narrative's relationship to the asbāb of Quraysh's three questions.

Ibn Kathīr does NOT critique the chains; like al-Qurṭubī, he transmits them positively as standard fadāʾil practice. Modern critical-hadith assessment (al-Albānī, *Silsilat al-Aḥādīth al-Ṣaḥīḥa* and *Silsilat al-Aḥādīth al-Ḍaʿīfa*) grades the Anas-light-between-heaven-and-earth tradition as ḍaʿīf (weak), but the Abū al-Dardāʾ ten-verses tradition as **ṣaḥīḥ** (sound, in Muslim).

## 2. The four narratives — exegetical positions

### 2.1 Aṣḥāb al-Kahf (vv. 9-26): the Christian-Najrān parallel

**Position 1** (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī majority): the cave-companions are the historical Christian Seven Sleepers of Ephesus, retold as a Quranic reproof to Christian disagreement over their number. The Quran's refusal to specify the number (3+1, 5+1, 7+1) is *itself* the lesson: the historical detail is irrelevant; the moral (faith preserved through divine protection) is the point.

**Position 2** (al-Rāzī, Q18:9-26): the cave-companions are not specifically the Ephesus group; they are *generic exemplars* of youth-preserving-religion. al-Rāzī treats the proper-noun (*aṣḥāb al-kahf*) as descriptive ("companions of [the] cave") rather than referring to a specific historical group. This is a more conservative reading.

**The number-dispute (vv. 22, 25)**: classical disagreement is exhaustive on whether the *correct* number is 3, 5, or 7 + the dog. al-Suyūṭī (`suyuti-durr-manthur.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18:22) catalogues the positions — Ibn ʿAbbās favored 7+1 (the position later adopted by majority Sunnī tradition); some early Companions held 3+1 or 5+1. The Quran's *rabbī aʿlamu bi-ʿiddatihim* (v. 22) is the *final decisive text*: God knows the number; humans should not dispute it.

**The In shāʾa Allāh discipline (vv. 23-24)**: this verse-pair is the foundational text for the *In shāʾa Allāh* discipline in Islamic jurisprudence. al-Qurṭubī (Q18:23-24 masʾala) treats the verses as establishing two rules:
1. Do not say "I will do X tomorrow" without "if God wills".
2. The asbāb is preserved: the Prophet was asked the three questions and said "I will tell you tomorrow"; the revelation was delayed 15 days as a rebuke for the omission of *In shāʾa Allāh*. (This asbāb is itself one of the most-cited fragments of Q 18's classical reception.)

**al-Raqīm**: the proper noun *al-raqīm* (v. 9) is a corpus-hapax. al-Ṭabarī Q18:9 catalogues four positions on its identity:
1. The cave's name (its mountain).
2. The valley where the cave was located.
3. An inscription with the youths' names and dates.
4. A separate event entirely (a different cave-narrative).

al-Ṭabarī himself favors position 3 (an inscription); al-Rāzī (Q18:9) favors position 1 or 2; al-Qurṭubī catalogs without choosing. The verse leaves *al-raqīm* deliberately underspecified, and the classical disagreement reflects this textual openness.

### 2.2 The two gardens (vv. 32-44): the wealth-vs-faith parable

**Classical reading** (al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr): the parable is a generic moral about pride-of-wealth, not a specific historical reference. The *rajulan* (two men) are deliberately unnamed.

**al-Rāzī's treatment** (Q18:32-44): the parable is a **direct illustration** of the al-Fātiḥa's *rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — the rich man fails because he does not see God as Lord-of-Worlds (treats his garden as if it were *his*). The believing companion's correction (*lawlā idh dakhalta jannataka qulta mā shāʾa Allāh* — v. 39) is the explicit *In shāʾa Allāh* discipline echoing the cave-companions' earlier discipline.

**The sub-narrative pattern**: pride → confidence in eternal-possession → destruction → regret → return to belief. This is the same arc as Q 68:17-33 (the garden-owners parable) — al-Biqāʿī (Q18:32 *munāsaba*) treats Q 18:32-44 and Q 68:17-33 as the corpus's two complementary garden-pride parables.

### 2.3 Mūsā and al-Khaḍir (vv. 60-82): the *ʿilm ladunī* test

This is **the most-extensively-commented passage of Q 18**. The classical agreements:

1. **The unnamed servant is al-Khaḍir** — Bukhārī #122 + #3261 (Saʿīd b. Jubayr ← Ibn ʿAbbās ← Ubayy b. Kaʿb) is the foundational hadith identifying him by name. Bukhārī #3262 (Abū Hurayra) gives the etymology of the name (from *xDr*, "verdure").

2. **The Mūsā in question is the Israelite Mūsā** — Bukhārī #122 records Saʿīd b. Jubayr correcting Nawf al-Bakālī's claim that this was a *different* Mūsā: "The enemy of God [Nawf] has lied. Ubayy b. Kaʿb told us..."

3. **Each of the three trials has a hidden cause**: 
   - The boat: a king behind them seizing every ship; defective boat = preserved boat.
   - The boy: would burden righteous parents with rebellion-and-disbelief; killing-now spares parents future grief.
   - The wall: under it is a treasure for two orphan boys; wall-collapse-now would expose the treasure too early.

**The theological dispute — was al-Khaḍir a prophet?**

- **Position A** (majority Sunnī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr): al-Khaḍir was a *prophet* — the killing of the boy and the holing of the boat would otherwise be sins, but as *waḥy*-recipient acting on direct divine instruction, his actions are not sinful. This is the most theologically-economic position.

- **Position B** (some Sufi readings, al-Rāzī cautiously sympathetic): al-Khaḍir was a *walī* (saint) with *al-ʿilm al-ladunī* — knowledge from God's presence directly accessible to those whom He chooses. al-Rāzī (Q18:65 commentary) reads *ʿabdan min ʿibādinā ātaynāhu raḥmatan min ʿindinā wa-ʿallamnāhu min ladunnā ʿilman* as the foundational text for *ʿilm ladunī* — knowledge given without a prophetic-message-bearing function. This is the Sufi position from al-Tustarī through Ibn ʿArabī.

- **Position C** (some philosophers, e.g., al-Qushayrī): al-Khaḍir was an *angel* in human form. This is a minority position.

**The Mūsā/Khaḍir dynamic** is the corpus's most-extensive *single* didactic narrative. Three trials (vv. 71-74-77) → three objections (v. 71b; v. 74b; v. 77b) → three explanations (vv. 79-82). The structure is canonical: each of the three trials is the same *test of patience* in different domains (property, life, courtesy). al-Biqāʿī reads this as a single *fitnat al-ʿilm* — Mūsā the Prophet must accept that propheticly-revealed-knowledge is not the only kind of knowledge. The al-Rāzī reading: al-Khaḍir is a *partial-prophet* (waḥy-receiver-without-mission) and the lesson is the *epistemic humility* of the prophetic class.

### 2.4 Dhū al-Qarnayn (vv. 83-101): the geographic-eschatological narrative

**Identity**: classical exegesis is divided. The major positions:
1. **Alexander the Great** — the dominant position by the 4th-5th AH centuries (al-Ṭabarī cites this attribution but does not endorse it definitively).
2. **A pre-Islamic Yemenite king** — al-Ṭabarsī (Shīʿī, *Majmaʿ al-bayān*) supports this.
3. **A different ancient king** (Cyrus the Great, in modern reconstruction; not a classical position).
4. **Generic exemplar** (al-Rāzī's preferred reading, Q18:83 commentary): the proper-noun *Dhū al-Qarnayn* ("the Two-Horned") is descriptive not historical — describing an extraordinarily-powerful king who reaches the ends of the earth.

**The three journeys** (west-then-east-then-between-the-two-barriers) form a canonical *dharʿa* (geographic survey) structure that classical exegesis treats as the proto-eschatological-cosmology. The west-journey reaches *ʿayn ḥamiʾa* (a muddy spring); the east-journey reaches a people without shelter; the between-journey reaches the Yājūj-Mājūj barrier.

**Yājūj wa-Mājūj** are unanimously identified in classical tafsīr as a specific people (al-Ṭabarī cites genealogies tracing them to Japheth, son of Noah) but the geographic location of the barrier is disputed. al-Qurṭubī Q18:94 catalogues classical positions ranging from the Caucasus mountains to a wholly-mythological barrier in the *unseen-earth*.

The eschatological clause (v. 99 — *yamūju fī baʿḍ wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūr*) is read by all classical mufassirūn as a transition from the historical-Dhū-al-Qarnayn to the universal Last Day. The barrier holds *until* the Day of Judgement; this is the surah's most-explicit eschatological-historical hinge.

## 3. The opening verses (Q 18:1-8) — exegetical positions

### 3.1 *al-ḥamdu lillāh* + *ʿiwajan* / *qayyiman*

al-Zamakhsharī's *iqāʿ* reading and al-Rāzī's five-*ḥamd*-openers comparative are the two most-distinctive classical readings. Both treat the opening as a *miniature* of al-Fātiḥa, with Q 18 specifying the *spiritual-Book-revealed-to-servant* dimension.

al-Biqāʿī (Q18:1): the opening *al-ḥamdu lillāh* is the *frame-statement* of the surah's *maqṣūd* — the *kitāb* (Book) is the antidote to fitna. The four narratives that follow are four illustrations of this frame.

### 3.2 *fa-laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka ʿalā āthārihim asafan* (v. 6)

The Prophet's grief at unbelievers' rejection. This verse-pair is one of the corpus's two most-explicit references to the Prophet's emotional distress (the other is Q 26:3, identical phrasing: *laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka allā yakūnū muʾminīn*). al-Rāzī (Q18:6): the *bākhiʿun* hyperbole — "killing yourself in grief" — establishes the depth of the Prophet's compassion for the unbelievers; the surah's narrative reassurance follows in v. 9 with *am ḥasibta* ("do you consider the cave-companions a wonder?") — i.e., the historical record shows this is not a unique or insurmountable rejection.

## 4. The closing (Q 18:102-110)

### 4.1 *qul law kāna al-baḥru midādan li-kalimāti rabbī* (v. 109)

al-Ṭabarī (Q18:109): this verse is a *rejoinder* to the Najrān Christians' challenge — they had asked the Prophet about specific historical events; the Quran answers by asserting the *inexhaustibility* of God's words. Specific answers like the cave and Dhū al-Qarnayn are mere drops from an infinite sea.

al-Rāzī (Q18:109): the *baḥr-as-ink* parable is a meta-commentary on the *qaṣaṣ* genre — narrative is a finite human form, but the divine knowledge from which narratives are drawn is inexhaustible. This connects Q 18:109 to Q 18:65 (the Khaḍir as one given *ʿilman* from *ladun-nā*) — both verses establish *al-ʿilm al-ilāhī* as boundless compared to human forms.

### 4.2 *qul innamā anā basharun mithlukum* (v. 110)

The closing *qul innamā anā basharun mithlukum* is paralleled by Q 41:6 (*qul innamā anā basharun mithlukum yūḥā ilayya*). Both verses use the identical *basharun mithlukum* + *yūḥā* construction. Q 18:110 closes with the *aḥadan* fāṣila (mirroring v. 26); Q 41:6 with *fa-staqīmū ilayhi wa-staghfirūhu* (different fāṣila).

al-Biqāʿī (Q18:110): the closing *aḥadan* is a *naẓm-ring-closure* mirroring v. 26's *aḥadan*. The four-narrative arc is enclosed within this fāṣila ring; the closing verse's monotheistic imperative is the *positive* counterpart to the cave-companions' opening prayer.

## 6. al-Biqāʿī on the Q 17 → Q 18 → Q 19 *tanāsub*

al-Biqāʿī (`biqai-nazm-al-durar.openiti.raw.txt`, Q18 opening + Q19 opening): 
- **Q 17 → Q 18**: Q 17 al-Isrāʾ closes (v. 110-111) on *qul ud-ʿū Allāh aw ud-ʿū al-Raḥmān* + *al-ḥamdu lillāh alladhī lam yattakhidh waladā*; Q 18 opens *al-ḥamdu lillāh alladhī anzala ʿalā ʿabdihi al-kitāb*. The transition is from *al-ḥamd-as-monotheistic-affirmation* (Q 17:111) to *al-ḥamd-as-revelation-affirmation* (Q 18:1) — the same fāṣila-key (*al-ḥamdu lillāh*) bridging two surahs.
- **Q 18 → Q 19**: Q 18 closes on *qul innamā anā basharun mithlukum* + monotheistic-imperative; Q 19 opens *kāf-hā-yāʾ-ʿayn-ṣād* (muqaṭṭaʿāt) + *dhikru raḥmati rabbika ʿabdahu Zakariyyā* — opening of the Zakariyyā-and-Yaḥyā narrative. The transition is from *Prophetic-exemplar* (Q 18:110) to *prophetic-narrative* (Q 19:1ff.).

The empirical correlate: Q 17-18 canonical-adjacency cost = 0.0279 (rank 86/113, cheap); Q 18-19 = 0.0193 (rank 92/113, cheaper). al-Biqāʿī's *naẓm* reading explicates the *low cost*: the same *al-ḥamd*-key bridges Q 17 and Q 18; the same *prophetic*-genre bridges Q 18 and Q 19.

## 8. One-paragraph synthesis

Q 18's classical reception is dominated by **fadāʾil traditions** (Friday-recitation, ten-verses-Dajjāl-protection) and **four-narrative exegetical disagreements** (cave-companions number; al-Khaḍir's prophet-or-walī status; Dhū al-Qarnayn's identity; Yājūj-Mājūj's location). al-Biqāʿī's *four-fitan* reading (faith, wealth, knowledge, power) is the most-cited classical structural framework, and modern devotional literature aligns the Friday-recitation discipline with this four-fitan typology. al-Rāzī's five-*ḥamd*-openers comparative places Q 18's *maqṣūd* as **spiritual cultivation through revealed Scripture** (a species under al-Fātiḥa's general nurture), with the four narratives as illustrative case-studies of God's mercy and knowledge across different scales (youth, prosperity, prophethood, kingship). The Mūsā-Khaḍir narrative is the most-theologically-loaded passage, with the *ʿilm ladunī* doctrine grounded in v. 65; the prophet-or-walī classification of al-Khaḍir is the major lasting dispute. al-Zamakhsharī's *iqāʿ* reading of *ʿiwajan / qayyiman* in vv. 1-2 is the surah's clearest classical example of complementary-pair fāṣila construction. The empirical project finds Q 18's *aḥadan*-fāṣila ring (v. 26 ↔ v. 110) at law-strength as a single-word-fāṣila ring-closure, and the four-narrative spacing geometry as a Bonferroni-3-passing palindromic-expansion at p = 0.008 ([[h-new-268-kahf-four-narratives|H-NEW-268]]).
