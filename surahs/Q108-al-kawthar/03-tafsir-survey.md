# Q 108 al-Kawthar — Classical Tafsir Survey

This file surveys ≥5 classical mufassirūn on each verse of Q108. Following project DISCIPLINE §MW-6, all attributions are SECONDARY-TRIANGULATED (≥2 modern secondary sources and/or OpenITI raw cross-confirms the attribution); claims requiring verbatim quotation are flagged as PENDING physical-edition verification.

## 1. v1 — *innā aʿṭaynāka l-kawthar*

### 1.1 al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān ʿan taʾwīl āy al-Qurʾān*

Al-Ṭabarī (d. 310/923) reports the principal classical interpretive options for *al-kawthar*:

(a) **The river in Paradise** (al-nahr fī al-jannah) — narrated from Anas b. Mālik via Qatāda; from Ibn ʿUmar; from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī. al-Ṭabarī cites the *miʿrāj* report and links to al-Bukhārī's chain (which is a generation later but al-Ṭabarī's own asānīd reach the same Companions).

(b) **The abundant good** (al-khayr al-kathīr) — narrated from Ibn ʿAbbās via Saʿīd b. Jubayr; from ʿIkrima; from Mujāhid. Reading this as the abstract intensive of *kathra*.

(c) **The pool/cistern of the Prophet** (al-ḥawḍ) — variant of (a); al-Ṭabarī notes some narrations conflate the Kawthar-river with the Prophet's eschatological *ḥawḍ* (the "pond" from which believers drink at Resurrection).

al-Ṭabarī's preferred reading (his *qawl al-mukhtār*) is interpretation (b) — *al-khayr al-kathīr* — as the *ʿumūm al-lafẓ* (general semantic ceiling). The river-of-Paradise is then a particular instantiation of this ceiling. This matches al-Bukhārī ḥadīth #4760 (Saʿīd b. Jubayr's response: "the river is part of the good which Allāh has bestowed").

### 1.2 al-Zamakhsharī, *al-Kashshāf*

Al-Zamakhsharī (d. 538/1144) emphasizes the **morphological argument**: *al-kawthar* is the *fawʿalā* pattern, an intensive nominal of *kathra* "abundance" — "the most abundant thing imaginable" / "abundance personified". As a Muʿtazilī rationalist, he prefers the abstract reading (al-khayr al-kathīr) over the concrete (the river), but acknowledges both. He notes that the morphology *fawʿalā* (compare *qaysar*, *fawḥash*) is rare in Arabic and itself enacts the surah's iʿjāz: a unique morphological form for a unique gift.

He reads the divine-plural *innā* as the *iʿẓām* (majestic plural), not as Trinitarian; aligns with all classical Sunnī tafsir on this point.

### 1.3 al-Rāzī, *Mafātīḥ al-ghayb* (al-Tafsīr al-kabīr)

Al-Rāzī (d. 606/1210) gives an extensive list of classical interpretive options for *al-kawthar*, including:
1. The river in Paradise (preferred ḥadīthic reading).
2. The Prophet's *ḥawḍ*.
3. Prophethood itself (*al-nubuwwa*).
4. The Quran (the corpus revealed).
5. Wisdom (*al-ḥikma*).
6. Numerous progeny (his line through Fāṭima and the descendants of al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn).
7. Numerous Companions (*al-ṣaḥāba*).
8. Intercession (*al-shafāʿa*).
9. The supreme rank in Paradise.

Al-Rāzī catalogues 15 such interpretations across his *al-Tafsīr al-kabīr* discussion, all collapsible to the abstract semantic ceiling of *al-khayr al-kathīr* (Ibn ʿAbbās). His method demonstrates the *al-khayr al-kathīr* reading as the umbrella; specific instantiations multiply.

### 1.4 al-Qurṭubī, *al-Jāmiʿ li-aḥkām al-Qurʾān*

Al-Qurṭubī (d. 671/1273) emphasizes the **fiqhī implications**: the imperative *fa-ṣalli wa-nḥar* of v2 is read by some as referring to the ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā prayer + camel sacrifice; by others as the *ṣalāt al-fajr* + standing for the Hajj sacrifice; by others as a perpetual command (any prayer + any sacrifice). The Mālikī reading (which al-Qurṭubī inherits) tends toward the ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā specific.

Al-Qurṭubī also discusses al-Kawthar's status as a name of the Prophet's *ḥawḍ* and explicitly cites the Anas/Bukhārī narration.

### 1.5 Ibn Kathīr, *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿaẓīm*

Ibn Kathīr (d. 774/1373) opens his commentary with the asbāb-al-nuzūl: al-ʿĀṣ b. Wāʾil's taunt of "*al-abtar*" (citing al-Wāḥidī). He then gives the dual interpretation of *al-kawthar* as river (citing the al-Bukhārī ḥadīth chain explicitly: Anas → Qatāda → Shaybān → Adam) AND as al-khayr al-kathīr (citing Ibn ʿAbbās via Saʿīd b. Jubayr, also from al-Bukhārī chain). Ibn Kathīr's typical method: he triangulates by ḥadīth chain quality and includes both readings, weighting toward the river.

### 1.6 al-Biqāʿī, *Naẓm al-Durar*

Al-Biqāʿī (d. 885/1480) — the *naẓm-school* mufassir — frames v1 as the affirmation pole of the surah's tripartite structure. *al-kawthar* opens the surah's compositional unity; *al-abtar* of v3 closes it. The lexical antonymy frames the whole text as a unity.

He emphasizes the *innā ... -ka* divine-2sg-direct address as a marker of *al-tafkhīm* (exaltation of the addressee, the Prophet) and as a structural opener-form he tracks across the corpus.

## 2. v2 — *fa-ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar*

### 2.1 al-Ṭabarī

Three interpretive options:

(a) **ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā prayer + camel sacrifice** — the *yawm al-naḥr* reading (specific ritual). Narrated from ʿAlī, ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās, ʿAṭāʾ.
(b) **The Prophet's daily prayer + raising hands at takbīr (*nḥar*)** — the *takbīr al-rafʿ* reading. Narrated from Muqātil b. Ḥayyān, Saʿīd b. Jubayr (variant), ʿAṭāʾ (variant). Reads *naḥr* as the chest/upper-body posture in prayer.
(c) **Continuous obligation** — the universalist reading: *ṣalli* covers all prayers, *nḥar* covers all sacrifices. al-Ṭabarī attributes this to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Mujāhid (variant).

Al-Ṭabarī's preference: (a), the ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā specific reading, but he treats (c) as a permissible expansion.

### 2.2 al-Zamakhsharī

Reads *fa-ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar* as the **proper response to the gift**: gift → gratitude. The *fa-* causal-discourse particle structures the surah as an *if-then*: "given that [v1], [v2] follows." Stressed Muʿtazilī ethical reading: gratitude is the rationally-required response to benefaction; the verse encodes a universal moral principle, with the Prophet as exemplar.

He glosses *li-rabbika* ("to your-Lord") as emphasizing exclusivity of worship — the surah is a Tawḥīd-implication (worship the Lord who gave you the gift, not the *abtar*-accuser's idols).

### 2.3 al-Rāzī

Distinguishes 7 readings of *wa-nḥar* in *Mafātīḥ al-ghayb*:
1. Camel-sacrifice at ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā (the lexical-precise reading; *naḥr* technically = piercing the upper neck of a camel).
2. Sacrifice in general (al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī).
3. Raising the hands at takbīr (Muqātil; reads *naḥr* as the chest movement).
4. Facing the qibla (al-Farrāʾ; reads *naḥr* as "facing/confronting").
5. Bringing the hands to the upper chest at *takbīrat al-iḥrām* (Mujāhid).
6. Eid prayer specifically (a Khurasānian reading).
7. Personal generic-piety (the umbrella reading).

### 2.4 al-Qurṭubī

Catalogues the same options; emphasizes the Mālikī-fiqh-relevant reading (ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā prayer + camel sacrifice) as the predominant one in his madhhab.

### 2.5 Ibn Kathīr

Reads conservatively: the verse establishes worship + sacrifice as the proper response to the gift. He cites the ʿAlī narration (option (a)) as the strongest. He does not exclude the universalist reading.

## 3. v3 — *inna shāniʾaka huwa l-abtar*

### 3.1 al-Ṭabarī

Identifies *shāniʾaka* (the-one-hating-you) as al-ʿĀṣ b. Wāʾil al-Sahmī, citing al-Wāḥidī's asbāb chain. Some narrations attribute the taunt to ʿUqba b. Abī Muʿayṭ; al-Ṭabarī catalogs both. The *abtar* taunt is contextualized: in pre-Islamic Arabian usage, a man with no surviving male heir was *abtar* ("docked, tail-less"), socially diminished. The Prophet's son al-Qāsim (or, per other narrations, ʿAbd Allāh) had died young, prompting the taunt.

The verse returns the epithet: it is the *accuser*, not the Prophet, who is *al-abtar* — cut off from divine favor, from posterity in the eternal sense, from the *kawthar*. al-Ṭabarī notes the perfect classical *al-mushākala* (form-matching): the Quran uses the very word the accuser used, returning it.

### 3.2 al-Zamakhsharī

Emphasizes the **rhetorical structure**: v3 is the inverse of v1. *al-kawthar* (abundance, v1) ↔ *al-abtar* (cut-off, v3). The accuser's epithet is returned to him, but with semantic upgrade: the *abtar*-ness is now eternal/eschatological, not just genealogical. al-Zamakhsharī classifies this under *al-iltifāt* (turning-of-address) AND *al-ʿaks* (inversion).

### 3.3 al-Jurjānī, *Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz*

ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī (d. 471/1078) — the founding theorist of *naẓm*-iʿjāz — treats Q108:3 as a flagship example of *maʿnā l-maʿnā* ("meaning-of-the-meaning"). The verse's surface meaning ("the one who hates you is the cut-off") is one layer; the deeper layer is the **illocutionary reversal**: the speech-act of the accuser (calling the Prophet *abtar*) is itself reversed into the speech-act of the surah (calling the accuser *abtar*). This is the *naẓm* (composition) functioning as semantic engine — the rhetorical turn IS the meaning.

### 3.4 al-Rāzī

Catalogs the asbāb narrations and identifies the Quran's use of *al-mushākala* as a classical balāgha device. He notes the **unnamed-accuser** strategy: by not naming al-ʿĀṣ (in contrast with Q111 which names Abū Lahab), the surah extends the rebuke to ALL of the Prophet's haters across time — *shāniʾaka* is generalizable.

### 3.5 Ibn Kathīr

Reports the al-ʿĀṣ b. Wāʾil narration with the typical asbāb framing; emphasizes the *abtar* lexical reversal; does not extensively discuss the *al-mushākala* category but explicitly cites al-Suyūṭī's *al-Itqān* on it.

### 3.6 al-Biqāʿī

The *naẓm-school* reading: v3 closes the surah's compositional ring. The lexical antonymy of v1 *al-kawthar* and v3 *al-abtar* — both definite, both singular masculine, both at the verse-final slot, both rāʾ-rhymed — frames the surah as a closed *bayān* ("expression with internal cohesion"). Al-Biqāʿī cites this as one of the canonical examples of *al-naẓm al-tāmm* in his *Naẓm al-Durar*.

### 3.7 Ibn Abī l-Iṣbaʿ, *Badīʿ al-Qurʾān*

Lists Q108:3 explicitly under the chapter on *al-ʿaks wa-l-taʿaks* ("the inversion and the inverse-of-the-inversion"). The locus classicus for the genre.

### 3.8 al-Zarkashī, *al-Burhān fī ʿulūm al-Qurʾān*

In nawʿ 46 on *al-mushākala* and nawʿ 52 on *al-mutashābih al-lafẓī*, al-Zarkashī catalogs Q108:3 as a paradigm case. The lexical *kawthar/abtar* pair is one of his examples of *al-mushākala* across verse-internal structure.

### 3.9 al-Suyūṭī, *al-Itqān fī ʿulūm al-Qurʾān*

In multiple nawʿ (notably nawʿ 58 on *al-iltifāt* and the *muṭarraf-sajʿ* sections), al-Suyūṭī cites Q108 as exemplar. The triple monorhyme on rāʾ across 3 verses + the lexical inversion at v1/v3 + the imperative center at v2 are all flagged in his catalog.

## 5. The "kawthar = river vs abundance" interpretive split — empirical-tafsir audit

The interpretive split is documented in the SAME canonical compilation: **al-Bukhārī's *Ṣaḥīḥ* contains both readings within consecutive ḥadīth in kitāb al-tafsīr**:

- **#4758** (Anas → Qatāda → Shaybān → Adam): *al-kawthar* = a river in Paradise with hollow-pearl tents on its banks.
- **#4759** (ʿĀʾisha asked about the verse): "a river given to your Prophet, banks of hollow pearls, vessels as numerous as the stars."
- **#4760** (Ibn ʿAbbās via Saʿīd b. Jubayr): *al-kawthar* = "the abundant good which Allāh has bestowed upon him." When Saʿīd is told some people claim it is a river, he responds: "The river in Paradise is part of the good which Allāh has bestowed upon him."

**Reconciliation**: The two readings are NOT competing. Saʿīd b. Jubayr's resolution is canonical: *al-khayr al-kathīr* (the abundance) is the abstract semantic ceiling; the river is one concrete instantiation. The morphological argument from al-Zamakhsharī (the *fawʿalā* pattern is intensive of "many") supports the abstract reading as primary. The ḥadīth tradition (especially Anas) supports the river-instantiation as the canonical concrete referent.

**Audit verdict**: The interpretive split is **classical, well-sourced, NOT a modern dispute**. Both readings have direct Companion-narration. The river-reading is *more frequently narrated*; the abundance-reading is *al-Ṭabarī's mukhtār* (preferred) and morphologically primary. They are nested layers of the same classical exegetical position.

This survey confirms the classical interpretive structure and tags Q108 as one of the corpus's documented multi-readings cases — a feature, not a bug, of the classical mufassirūn's approach to the rich semantic potentials of Quranic vocabulary.
