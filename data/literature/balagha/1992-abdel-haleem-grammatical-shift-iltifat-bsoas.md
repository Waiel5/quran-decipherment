## Introduction

Theodor Nöldeke's pioneering work on Qur'ānic stylistic features identified grammatical shifts in pronouns and persons, which Arab rhetoricians term **iltifāt**. While Nöldeke viewed these as "unusual," Arab literary critics recognized them as "remarkable things and exquisite subtleties" reflecting sophisticated rhetorical technique.

Existing treatments in Arabic rhetorical studies (balāgha) present limited examples—Ibn al-Athīr discusses approximately 20 instances, Suyūtī about 35, and Badr al-Dīn al-Zarkashī provides roughly 50 examples. However, systematic examination reveals the feature occurs far more extensively throughout the Qur'ān than these earlier works suggest. The claim that such shifts appear primarily in Meccan sūras proves inaccurate when the full text is surveyed.

---

## The Meaning of Iltifāt

The term literally means "to turn/turn one's face to," originating from the poetic context of turning back toward memory of a beloved's encampment. Early scholars like **al-Asmaʿī (216/831)** applied it to departing from one subject to address another before resuming. By **Ibn al-Muʿtazz's** time (296/909), the term acquired technical precision. **Ibn al-Athīr** characterized iltifāt as the speaker's turning "from one form to another." **Al-Zarkashī** emphasized its function: "the change of speech from one mode to another, for the sake of freshness and variety for the listener, to renew his interest, and to keep his mind from boredom." The phenomenon solidified in the **Miftāḥ al-ʿulūm** of Sakkākī (626/1228).

Related terminology: *al-sarf* (Ibn Wahb), *al-insirāf* (Ibn Munqidh), *al-talwīn*, *talwīn al-khitāb*, *al-iʿtirāḍ*.

## Conditions

1. Identity preservation: pronoun shifted to must reference the same entity as that shifted from.
2. Independent sentences (later refuted by counterexamples, especially Q 25:17).

### VI. Using Noun in Place of Pronoun (>100 instances)

Divine names *Allāh* / *rabb* substituted for pronouns to emphasize exclusivity, causality, and theological distinctness. Key example Q 2:115 ("To Allāh… face of Allāh… Allāh is"). Words collocating primarily with *Allāh* rather than pronouns: *al-ḥamd, faḍl, rizq, sabīl, ajal, baʿth, hudā*.

## Functions (per Zarkashī and Zamakhsharī)

1. Honoring addressee (Q 1:4)
2. Adding useful information (Q 44:6; noun replacing pronoun)
3. Making public spectacle of addressee behavior (Q 10:22 travelers)
4. Indicating exclusive divine action (Q 35:9)
5. Highlighting particular interest (Q 41:12)
6. Expressing disapproval through sudden address (Q 19:99)

## Conclusion

The Qur'ān employs iltifāt and related features far more extensively and with greater variety than any other Arabic literary tradition. These are **systematic rhetorical choices** serving theological purposes (tawhīd, multiple addressees, universal applicability), not stylistic irregularities as Nöldeke implied.
