# Classical Commentary on Q 6:76-78 — Abraham's Afl Chain (Star, Moon, Sun)

## The verses

Q 6:76: "When the night overshadowed him, he saw a star. He said, 'This is my Lord.'
But when it set (**afala**), he said, 'I do not love those that set (**al-afilin**).'"
Q 6:77: "And when he saw the moon rising, he said, 'This is my Lord.' But when it set
(**afala**), he said..."
Q 6:78: "And when he saw the sun rising, he said, 'This is my Lord; this is greater.'
But when it set (**afalat**)..."

## Our finding

The rare root أ-ف-ل (afl, "to set/vanish") has only 4 total occurrences in the entire
Quran, and 4 of those 4 appear within a single 3-verse window: Q 6:76-78. The root IS
Abraham's rejection of the celestial bodies. Three consecutive verses, each closing with
a form of afl. This is the only such 3-verse rare-root chain in the Quran catalog.

## What classical commentators DO say

All classical tafsirs (Ibn Kathir, al-Tabari, al-Jalalayn, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Zamakhshari,
al-Baydawi, al-Kashani, al-Tustari) comment on these verses at length. They discuss:
- The theological argument: a true Lord cannot "set" / "vanish" / be subject to change.
- Abraham's reasoning by elimination (star → moon → sun → transcendent creator).
- Whether Abraham was truly a polytheist in his youth (no, classical consensus: he was
  engaging dialectically with his people's worship).
- The lexical meaning of afala (to disappear, to vanish, to set below the horizon).
- Interesting observation from some tafsirs: the form afala vs afalat vs al-afilin
  reflects grammatical gender of the celestial body referenced.

## What they do NOT observe (quantitatively)

No classical commentator we could find notes that:
- The root أفل occurs only 4 times in the entire Quran
- All 4 occurrences are confined to this 3-verse passage
- This is the ONLY 3-verse rare-root chain in the whole Quran

Again, the reason is infrastructural: without a computerized concordance, counting
lemma-exclusive-to-passage is prohibitively laborious.

## The classical rhetoric category that WOULD have covered this

The classical device **tarāduf** (serial synonymy / near-synonymous repetition) and the
related **tikrār** (structured repetition) are the right categories. Zamakhshari's Kashshaf
discusses tikrar extensively. But the specific observation that the verbal repetition
*exhausts* the rare root's entire Quranic inventory is not recorded.

Classical commentators did note that the narrative's THEMATIC escalation (star → moon → sun)
follows ascending celestial hierarchy. The LEXICAL mirror of this — that each rejection
closes with the same root and that the root has no home anywhere else in the Quran — is
novel.

## Verdict

Content-level recognition: ancient. Quantitative exclusivity observation: novel.
