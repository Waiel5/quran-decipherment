## Definition and History

The Abjad numerals, also called **Hisab al-Jummal** (حِسَاب ٱلْجُمَّل), constitute "a decimal alphabetic numeral system" where the 28 letters of the Arabic alphabet receive numerical assignments. These numerals have been employed in Arabic-speaking regions since before the eighth century, predating the adoption of positional Arabic numerals.

## Basic System Structure

In the Abjad framework, letters represent values from 1 to 1000:
- Letters 1-9 represent single digits (alif through ta)
- The next set represents tens (ya through sad)
- Additional letters represent hundreds (qaf through ghayn)
- The final letter represents 1000

The term "abjad" itself derives from the first four letters (A-B-G-D) of ancient Semitic alphabets, including Aramaic, Hebrew, and Phoenician scripts.

## Modern Applications

Today, abjad numerals serve primarily for "numbering outlines, items in lists, and points of information." They function similarly to English lettering systems (A, B, C) or Roman numerals in contemporary usage.

### Numerical Properties

The system lacks representation for zero and fractions. By convention, numbers are written largest-value-first (right to left). Notably, reversing order indicates multiplication—for instance, writing letters in reverse sequence produces different numerical results than standard ordering.

## Numerology and Cultural Significance

The abjad system extends beyond mathematics into numerological applications. Each Arabic word receives a cumulative numerical value based on letter assignments. The Islamic invocation "bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm" (In the name of Allah, the most merciful) calculates to 786 through letter-by-letter summation.

## Related Systems

The Abjad approach parallels Hebrew gematria and Greek isopsephy—comparable alphabetic numeral systems assigning numerical values to letters for sacred and mystical purposes.

## Competing Orderings

Beyond the primary Mashriqi arrangement, the **Maghrebian** variant exists as an older form. Additionally, the modern **hijāʾī** (هجائي) ordering—which groups letters by shape similarity—never functions as numerals but serves as the contemporary standard alphabet sequence.
