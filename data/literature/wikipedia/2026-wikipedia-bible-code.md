## Definition and Overview

The Bible code, also known as the Torah code, purports to contain encoded messages within the Hebrew text of the Torah that allegedly predicted historical events. Contemporary scholarship widely considers these codes to be **"statistically insignificant, as similar phenomena can be observed in any sufficiently lengthy text."**

Modern popularization began with Michael Drosnin's 1997 bestseller *The Bible Code* and the 1999 film *The Omega Code*.

## The Equidistant Letter Sequence (ELS) Method

The primary technique involves selecting letters at fixed intervals within a text. This process, called dilug in Hebrew, works by identifying a starting point and counting every nth letter in a specified direction.

### Example Application

Researchers demonstrated the method using Genesis 26:5–10 arranged in a 21-column grid, extracting the words "Bible" and "code" through consistent letter spacing.

### Extended Sequences

Once initial words are found, proponents seek additional words using identical spacing. Advocates maintain longer sequences carry reduced probability of occurring randomly, while critics argue this represents an application of the **"look-elsewhere effect,"** essentially multiplying permutations indefinitely.

## Historical Development

### Early Foundations

The 13th-century Spanish rabbi Bachya ben Asher documented an ELS related to the Hebrew calendar. The technique appeared in 16th-century mystical texts like *Pardes Rimonim*.

### 20th Century Research

Michael Ber Weissmandl manually arranged Torah text into grids to identify divine messages before computerization. His 1958 posthumous publication *Torat Chemed* preserved his findings.

### The Rips-Witztum Studies

Israeli mathematician Eliyahu Rips, alongside Doron Witztum and Yoav Rosenberg, developed computer software for systematic ELS analysis. Their 1994 peer-reviewed study in *Statistical Science* examined whether biographical details of famous rabbis appeared encoded in Genesis in statistically significant clustering patterns.

The journal presented this as a **"challenging puzzle"** rather than confirmed findings—a distinction later misinterpreted by the public as validation.

### Contemporary Extensions

Harold Gans, a former NSA cryptanalyst, conducted 1997 experiments matching rabbi names with birthplace and death location information. His results appeared similarly suggestive, though Gans himself stated regarding Drosnin's predictive claims: **"This is absolutely unfounded. There is no scientific or mathematical basis for such a statement."**

## Critical Refutation

### The MBBK Challenge (1999)

Australian mathematician Brendan McKay alongside Israeli researchers Dror Bar-Natan, Gil Kalai, and Maya Bar-Hillel published a comprehensive critique in *Statistical Science*. Their analysis demonstrated the original research was **"fatally defective, and that their result merely reflects on the choices made in designing their experiment."**

MBBK argued the researchers had:
- Pre-selected rabbi names and dates
- Designed experiments to match predetermined selections
- Exploited sensitivity to spelling variations

To support this theory, MBBK successfully generated comparable results using a Hebrew translation of *War and Peace*, a secular novel obviously lacking divine encoding.

Journal editor Robert Kass concluded the **"puzzle has been solved,"** essentially validating the refutation.

### Textual Variation Problems

The Hebrew Masoretic Text lacks consistent letter sequences across historical manuscripts. Dead Sea Scroll variations demonstrate textual instability through the first century CE, undermining the entire code hypothesis.

## Defenders' Responses

### Harold Gans

Gans argued MBBK's conspiracy theory required unlikely coordination among multiple rabbis and contributors who independently verified name lists. He contended McKay and colleagues produced results only through deliberately rigged experiments.

### WRR Authors

The original researchers issued multiple responses denying data manipulation. They claimed their alternative name formats—which MBBK suggested were intentionally avoided—actually produced equivalent or superior results when tested.

### Robert Aumann (Nobel Laureate)

Game theorist and 2005 Nobel Prize winner Robert Aumann, initially convinced of the codes, wrote: **"Though this work did not convince me that the data had been manipulated, it did convince me that it could have been."**

After extensive analysis, Aumann returned to his initial assessment that the codes phenomenon remains **"improbable."**

### Robert Haralick

Computer science professor Haralick tested extended ELSs across multiple minimal levels, finding WRR's Genesis list successful through the 20th minimal ELS while MBBK's *War and Peace* list failed after the second level. He interpreted this convergence as supporting code authenticity.

## Drosnin's Failed Predictions

Journalist Michael Drosnin's popular books made specific forecasts that subsequently proved incorrect:

- **World War III**: Predicted an atomic holocaust and end times in *Bible Code II* (2002)—never occurred
- **Libya**: Claimed weapons of mass destruction development and terrorist transfer—Libya instead improved Western relations in 2003 and surrendered weapons programs
- **Yasser Arafat**: Predicted assassination by Hamas gunmen—Arafat died of natural causes in 2004

Additionally, critics identified fundamental errors in his Rabin assassination analysis. Drosnin misapplied Deuteronomy 4:42, which actually discusses accidental killings in cities of refuge, fundamentally changing the supposed code's meaning.

## Scholarly Consensus

No peer-reviewed refutation of MBBK's critique has appeared in scientific journals. The mainstream academic position considers Bible codes a product of pattern-seeking cognition applied to random text, compounded by selective data analysis and confirmation bias.

The phenomenon parallels apophenia—perceiving meaningful patterns in random data—rather than demonstrating encoded prophecy.
