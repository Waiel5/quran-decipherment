## Overview
Al-Fatiha (Arabic: ٱلۡفَاتِحَةِ) is the opening chapter of the Quran, consisting of seven verses that form a prayer for divine guidance and mercy. The name translates to "The Opener" or "The Key."

## Basic Facts
- **Position**: First Surah (chapter) of the Quran
- **Verses**: 7 Ayat (verses)
- **Word Count**: 25 or 29 words (depending on whether Basmala is counted)
- **Letter Count**: 113 or 139 letters (variant counts)
- **Classification**: Meccan Surah (though some scholars debate this)
- **Division**: Juz' 1, Hizb 1

## Origins and Names
The predominant scholarly view, attributed to Ibn Abbas and others, identifies Al-Fatiha as a Meccan revelation. Most narrators agree it was "the first complete Surah revealed to Muhammad."

The chapter is known by multiple names:
- Al-Hamd (The Praise)
- As-Salah (The Prayer)
- Umm al-Kitab (Mother of the Book)
- Umm al-Quran (Mother of the Quran)
- Sab'a min al-Mathani (Seven Repeated Ones)
- Ash-Shifa' (The Cure)

## Structural Division
According to Islamic tradition, the chapter divides into two halves shared between God and the supplicant:
- **Verses 1-3**: God's portion (praising His attributes)
- **Verses 4-7**: The servant's portion (requesting guidance)

## Content Summary

**Verses 1-3 (God's Half)**:
The opening affirms that all praise belongs to God, the Lord of all universes, the Most Compassionate and Merciful, and the Master of the Day of Judgment.

**Verses 4-7 (Servant's Half)**:
The supplicant declares exclusive worship and reliance upon God alone, requesting guidance along the Straight Path. These final verses ask to follow the path of those blessed by God, avoiding the paths of those who incurred divine anger or went astray.

## Verse Text and Transliteration

**Verse 1 (Basmala)**:
"In the name of God, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful"

**Verse 2**:
"All praise is for God—Lord of all universes"

**Verse 3**:
"The Most Compassionate, Most Merciful"

**Verse 4**:
"Master of the Day of Judgment" (with variant readings: "King of the Day of Judgment")

**Verse 5**:
"Thee [alone] we worship and Thee [alone] we ask for help"

**Verse 6**:
"Guide us along the Straight Path"

**Verse 7**:
"The Path of those You have blessed—not of those who have evoked [Your] anger or of those who are astray"

## Interpretive Perspectives

### General Interpretation
Many Muslim commentators interpret verses 6-7 in universal terms, not referring to specific groups but rather to general spiritual states and conditions.

### Specific Group Interpretations
Some commentators identify particular groups:
- "Those who incurred God's anger" → Often interpreted as referring to Jews
- "Those who went astray" → Often interpreted as referring to Christians

However, scholars like Muhammad Asad and Abdullah Yusuf Ali present more nuanced interpretations emphasizing spiritual states rather than specific communities.

## Religious Significance

### Recitation Practice
Al-Fatiha holds central importance in Islamic worship, being recited in every unit (Rakat) of the obligatory and voluntary daily prayers (Salah).

### Virtues and Benefits
Islamic tradition ascribes special significance to Al-Fatiha, with both Sunni and Shia Muslims regarding it as "one of the greatest surahs in the Quran." It is believed to serve as "a cure for several diseases and poisons, both spiritual and mental."

### Prophetic Tradition
According to a hadith in Sahih Muslim:
"The Opening of the Book (Al-Fatiha) and [the last verses of Al-Baqarah] were gifts to the Prophet that no predecessor received, with reward promised for each recitation."
